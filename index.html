The Mathematical Toolkit

Physics is written in the language of rates of change and sums of small pieces. Five ideas carry us through the whole course. Limits, derivatives, integrals, motion graphs, and turning in a circle.

  • Limits
  • Derivative as a slope
  • Integral as an area
  • Position, velocity, acceleration
  • Radians, ω, f and T

1. Limits

A limit asks what value a function approaches as its input gets closer and closer to a point. The function does not even need to be defined at that point.

Definition$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$$
Example$$\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\lim_{x\to1}(x+1)=2$$
A famous one$$\lim_{\theta\to0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}=1$$

Why do we need limits in physics? Velocity at an instant is the distance moved divided by the time taken, when the time taken shrinks to zero. That is exactly a limit.

2. The derivative

The derivative of $x(t)$ is the slope of its graph. It tells how fast $x$ is changing at one instant.

Definition$$\frac{dx}{dt}=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{x(t+\Delta t)-x(t)}{\Delta t}$$
Power rule$$\frac{d}{dt}t^n = n\,t^{n-1}$$
Sine and cosine$$\tfrac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t=\omega\cos\omega t$$$$\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t=-\omega\sin\omega t$$
Exponential$$\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\gamma t}=-\gamma e^{-\gamma t}$$
Chain rule$$\frac{d}{dt}f(g(t))=f'(g)\,g'(t)$$
Product rule$$\frac{d}{dt}(uv)=u'v+uv'$$

3. The integral

The integral adds up many thin slices. If you know the velocity at every instant, the area under the $v$–$t$ graph is the distance travelled. Integration undoes differentiation.

As a sum$$\int_a^b v\,dt=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N} v(t_i)\,\Delta t$$
Power rule$$\int t^n\,dt=\frac{t^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\;\;(n\neq-1)$$
Useful ones$$\int\frac{dr}{r^2}=-\frac1r,\quad\int\frac{dr}{r}=\ln r$$
Fundamental theorem$$\int_a^b\frac{dx}{dt}\,dt=x(b)-x(a)$$

4. Position, velocity and acceleration

Velocity$$v=\frac{dx}{dt}$$
Acceleration$$a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$$
Constant a only$$v=u+at,\quad x=ut+\tfrac12at^2,\quad v^2=u^2+2ax$$

5. Turning in a circle

Angles in physics are measured in radians. One full turn is $2\pi$ rad. If a point turns at a steady rate, its shadow on a diameter performs simple harmonic motion. This is the bridge to Class 1.

Radian$$\theta=\frac{s}{r},\quad 2\pi\ \text{rad}=360^\circ$$
Angular velocity$$\omega=\frac{d\theta}{dt}=2\pi f=\frac{2\pi}{T}$$
Linear from angular$$v=\omega r,\quad a_c=\omega^2 r=\frac{v^2}{r}$$

Key ideas

A motion is periodic if it repeats itself after a fixed time $T$, called the period. A body oscillates when a restoring force always pulls it back toward its equilibrium position.

The simplest and most important case is when the restoring force grows in direct proportion to the displacement. This gives simple harmonic motion (SHM).

Condition for SHM (the rule to remember)

Whenever the acceleration of a body is directly proportional to its displacement from a fixed point and is always directed toward that point, the body executes simple harmonic motion.

$$a=-\omega^2x\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\omega^2x=0$$

Here $\omega^2$ must be a positive constant. The minus sign means the acceleration always points opposite to the displacement, that is, back toward equilibrium. So any system whose equation of motion can be brought to the form $\ddot x+\omega^2x=0$ is an SHM, and its angular frequency is read off directly as $\omega$, with period $T=2\pi/\omega$.

Deriving the equation of motion: spring–mass system

  1. Forces. Vertically, $N$ and $mg$ balance, so there is no vertical motion. Horizontally, the only force is the spring force. By Hooke's law it is proportional to the stretch and opposite to it, $F=-kx$.
  2. Newton's second law along $x$ gives $$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx$$
  3. Rearrange by dividing by $m$ $$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\frac{k}{m}\,x=0$$
  4. Compare with $\ddot x+\omega^2x=0$. The acceleration is proportional to $-x$, so the motion is SHM with $$\omega=\sqrt{\frac km},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac mk}$$

Deriving the equation of motion: simple pendulum

  1. Forces on the bob. Tension $T$ along the string toward the pivot, and weight $mg$ straight down. Resolve $mg$ into two parts, $mg\cos\theta$ along the string and $mg\sin\theta$ along the arc (tangential), pointing back toward equilibrium.
  2. Along the string (radial direction), $T-mg\cos\theta=\dfrac{mv^2}{L}$. This only fixes the tension and does not affect the swinging.
  3. Along the arc (tangential direction), the displacement is the arc length $s=L\theta$, so the tangential acceleration is $\ddot s=L\ddot\theta$. Newton's second law gives $$mL\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-mg\sin\theta$$ The minus sign shows the force always acts back toward $\theta=0$.
  4. Small angle approximation. For small $\theta$ (in radians, up to about $10^\circ$), $\sin\theta\approx\theta$. Then $$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+\frac{g}{L}\,\theta=0$$
  5. Compare with $\ddot x+\omega^2x=0$. The angular acceleration is proportional to $-\theta$, so the small swings are SHM with $$\omega=\sqrt{\frac gL},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg}$$ The period depends only on $L$ and $g$, not on the mass or (for small swings) the amplitude.

For large angles $\sin\theta$ is no longer close to $\theta$, the acceleration is no longer exactly proportional to $-\theta$, and the motion is periodic but not simple harmonic.

Solving the SHM equation with the exponential method

Why do we guess an exponential? Look at the SHM equation again, $\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-\omega^2x$. It says that differentiating the displacement twice gives back the same function, only multiplied by a constant ($-\omega^2$).

So we ask which functions behave like this. We need to make an educated guess. The obvious candidate is the exponential, because it hardly changes under differentiation. $\dfrac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t}=\lambda e^{\lambda t}$ and $\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}e^{\lambda t}=\lambda^2e^{\lambda t}$. Each derivative only brings down a constant $\lambda$, and the function itself stays the same. That is exactly the property the equation asks for, so we start with this guess and let the equation tell us what $\lambda$ must be.

The same guess works again for damped and forced oscillations, so it is worth learning this method well.

  1. Trial solution. Put $x=e^{\lambda t}$. Then $\dot x=\lambda e^{\lambda t}$ and $\ddot x=\lambda^2e^{\lambda t}$.
  2. Substitute. $$\lambda^2e^{\lambda t}+\omega^2e^{\lambda t}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\lambda^2+\omega^2)\,e^{\lambda t}=0$$ Since $e^{\lambda t}$ is never zero, we get the characteristic (auxiliary) equation $$\lambda^2+\omega^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=\pm i\omega$$
  3. General solution. Both roots give a solution, and their sum is also a solution $$x(t)=C_1e^{i\omega t}+C_2e^{-i\omega t}$$
  4. Make it real. Using Euler's formula $e^{\pm i\omega t}=\cos\omega t\pm i\sin\omega t$, and choosing $C_1=\tfrac A2e^{i\phi}$ and $C_2=\tfrac A2e^{-i\phi}$ so that $x$ is real, $$x(t)=\frac A2\left[e^{i(\omega t+\phi)}+e^{-i(\omega t+\phi)}\right]=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$$
  5. Constants. The amplitude $A$ and the phase constant $\phi$ come from the starting conditions $x(0)$ and $v(0)$.

Looking ahead. For damped motion the same trial $x=e^{\lambda t}$ turns $\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$ into $\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0$. The nature of its roots (real, equal or complex) decides whether the motion is overdamped, critically damped or underdamped.

Variation of position, velocity and acceleration with time

To see the three graphs clearly, take the simplest case $\phi=0$, so the body is released from the right end at $t=0$.

(a) Position–time graph

$$x=A\cos\omega t$$

The graph starts from the maximum position $+A$ at $t=0$.

  • It is sinusoidal.
  • Its maximum is $+A$ and its minimum is $-A$.
  • It repeats every period $T$.

(b) Velocity–time graph

Velocity is the derivative of displacement, $v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}$. With $x=A\cos\omega t$,

$$\boxed{v=-A\omega\sin\omega t}$$
  • It is also sinusoidal.
  • It is shifted in phase from the displacement by $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$).
  • Velocity is zero at the extreme positions.
  • Velocity is maximum at the mean position.

(c) Acceleration–time graph

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity, $a=\dfrac{dv}{dt}$, so

$$a=-A\omega^2\cos\omega t\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\boxed{a=-\omega^2x}$$
  • It is sinusoidal.
  • It is exactly opposite in phase to the displacement.
  • When the displacement is maximum positive, the acceleration is maximum negative.
  • When the displacement is zero, the acceleration is zero.

Easy sketch on the board. Draw $x=A\cos\omega t$ starting from $+A$. Draw $v=-A\omega\sin\omega t$ starting from $0$ and going negative first. Draw $a=-A\omega^2\cos\omega t$ starting from its maximum negative value. All three are similar wave-like curves, only shifted relative to each other.

Phase relations between x, v and a

For $x=A\cos\omega t$ we have $v=-A\omega\sin\omega t$ and $a=-A\omega^2\cos\omega t$. So

  • velocity is $90^\circ$ out of phase with displacement,
  • acceleration is $180^\circ$ out of phase with displacement,
  • acceleration and velocity differ by $90^\circ$.

In simple words, when the displacement is maximum the velocity is zero, when the velocity is maximum the displacement is zero, and the acceleration always points toward the centre.

Important relations in SHM

Displacement and acceleration

$$\boxed{a=-\omega^2x}$$

The acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement but opposite in direction. That is why SHM is a restoring motion.

Displacement and velocity

$$\boxed{v=\pm\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad v^2=\omega^2(A^2-x^2)$$

The velocity depends on position. It is maximum at the mean position ($x=0$) and zero at the extreme positions ($x=\pm A$).

Maximum and minimum velocity and acceleration

Since $\sin\omega t$ and $\cos\omega t$ vary between $-1$ and $+1$,

$$\boxed{v_{\max}=+A\omega,\quad v_{\min}=-A\omega}\qquad\boxed{a_{\max}=+A\omega^2,\quad a_{\min}=-A\omega^2}$$

If a question asks for the maximum speed (the size of the velocity), the answer is $A\omega$. The maximum size of the acceleration is $A\omega^2$.

Time period and frequency

The time period $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation, and the frequency $f$ is the number of oscillations per second, measured in hertz (Hz).

$$\boxed{T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}}\qquad\boxed{f=\frac1T=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}}$$

Energy in SHM

As the block oscillates, energy keeps changing form between the spring (potential energy) and the moving block (kinetic energy). With $x=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and $v=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ we can write both explicitly.

Kinetic energy

$$K=\tfrac12mv^2=\tfrac12m\omega^2A^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)=\tfrac12k\left(A^2-x^2\right)$$

using $m\omega^2=k$. It is largest at the mean position, $K_{\max}=\tfrac12kA^2$, and zero at the extreme positions.

Potential energy

The work done in stretching the spring from $0$ to $x$ is stored as potential energy,

$$U=\int_0^x kx\,dx=\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac12kA^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi)$$

It is largest at the extreme positions and zero at the mean position.

Total energy

$$E=K+U=\tfrac12kA^2\left[\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)+\cos^2(\omega t+\phi)\right]=\boxed{\tfrac12kA^2=\tfrac12m\omega^2A^2}$$

The total energy is constant and proportional to the square of the amplitude. Doubling the amplitude makes the energy four times larger.

  • K and U oscillate with frequency $2f$, twice as fast as the displacement.
  • K and U are equal when $\tfrac12kx^2=\tfrac14kA^2$, that is at $x=\pm A/\sqrt2$.
  • Averaged over one period, $\langle K\rangle=\langle U\rangle=\tfrac14kA^2=\tfrac12E$.
  • With friction the amplitude decays as $Ae^{-\gamma t}$, so the energy decays as $E_0e^{-2\gamma t}$. This links to damped oscillations in Class 4 and 5.

What the phase constant φ means

In $x=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, the quantity $\omega t+\phi$ is the phase. It tells where the oscillator is in its cycle at time $t$. The constant $\phi$ is the phase at $t=0$, so it fixes the starting position $x(0)=A\cos\phi$ and the starting velocity $v(0)=-A\omega\sin\phi$. Two oscillators with the same $\omega$ but different $\phi$ do exactly the same motion, one ahead of the other by $\Delta t=\phi/\omega$.

Worked examples

Example 1

A block of mass 680 g is fastened to a spring of spring constant 65 N/m. The block is pulled 11 cm from equilibrium on a frictionless table and released. Find (a) the angular frequency, frequency and period, (b) the amplitude, (c) the maximum speed.

Show solution

(a) $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}=\sqrt{65/0.68}=9.78$ rad/s, $f=\omega/2\pi=1.56$ Hz, $T=1/f=0.64$ s.

(b) It is released from rest at 11 cm, so $A=0.11$ m.

(c) $v_{\max}=\omega A=9.78\times0.11=1.08$ m/s.

Example 2

A spring stretches by 3.90 cm when a 10.0 g mass hangs from it. A 25.0 g mass attached to this spring oscillates in SHM. Calculate the period, the frequency and the angular frequency.

Show solution

At rest $kx=mg$, so $k=\dfrac{(0.010)(9.8)}{0.039}=2.51$ N/m.

$\omega=\sqrt{2.51/0.025}=10.0$ rad/s, $f=1.60$ Hz, $T=0.627$ s.

Example 3

For an oscillator with $k=19.6$ N/m and $x=-(0.100\text{ m})\cos 8.08t$, find (a) the total energy, (b) $K$ and $U$ as functions of time, (c) the speed when $x=0.050$ m, (d) $K$ and $U$ at half amplitude.

Show solution

(a) $E=\tfrac12kA^2=\tfrac12(19.6)(0.100)^2=9.80\times10^{-2}$ J.

(b) $U=\tfrac12kx^2=(9.80\times10^{-2})\cos^2 8.08t$ J and $K=E-U=(9.80\times10^{-2})\sin^2 8.08t$ J.

(c) $v=\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}=8.08\sqrt{0.100^2-0.050^2}=0.70$ m/s.

(d) $U=\tfrac12k(A/2)^2=\tfrac14E=2.45\times10^{-2}$ J, so $K=\tfrac34E=7.35\times10^{-2}$ J.

Example 4

A 500 g block on a spring is pulled 20 cm and released. The period is 0.80 s. At what positions is the speed 1.0 m/s?

Show solution

$\omega=2\pi/T=7.85$ rad/s. From $v=\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}$, $x=\pm\sqrt{A^2-(v/\omega)^2}=\pm\sqrt{0.04-0.0162}=\pm0.154$ m. So at 15.4 cm on either side of equilibrium.

Example 5

A loudspeaker diaphragm vibrates at $f=1.0$ kHz with amplitude 0.20 mm. Find the maximum speed and maximum acceleration, and where each occurs.

Show solution

$\omega=2\pi f=6283$ rad/s. $v_{\max}=\omega A=1.26$ m/s, at the centre $x=0$.

$a_{\max}=\omega^2A=7.9\times10^3$ m/s² (about 800 g), at the ends $x=\pm A$.

Example 6

A particle moves as $y=12\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi t}{10}+\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$ cm. Find (i) the amplitude, (ii) the frequency, (iii) the displacement at $t=1.25$ s, (iv) the velocity at $t=2.5$ s, (v) the acceleration at $t=5$ s.

Show solution

(i) $A=12$ cm. (ii) $\omega=2\pi/10$ so $f=0.1$ Hz, $T=10$ s.

(iii) Phase $=\pi/4+\pi/4=\pi/2$, so $y=12$ cm.

(iv) $v=12\omega\cos(\pi/2+\pi/4)=12(0.628)(-0.707)=-5.33$ cm/s.

(v) $a=-\omega^2y=-\omega^2\,12\sin(\pi+\pi/4)=+3.35$ cm/s².

Example 7

A mass in SHM starts at $x=A$ with period $T$. At what time does it first pass $x=A/2$?

Show solution

$A/2=A\cos(2\pi t/T)$ gives $2\pi t/T=\pi/3$, so $t=T/6$.

Key ideas

When a particle is driven by two SHMs at right angles, it traces a closed curve called a Lissajous figure. Its shape depends on the frequency ratio, the phase difference and the amplitudes. An oscilloscope uses this to compare two signals.

Any system with a restoring torque proportional to the angle performs angular SHM. The period then depends on the inertia and the stiffness of the restoring torque.

Equal frequencies$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}-\frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta$$

How an oscilloscope works

An analogue oscilloscope draws a voltage as a moving spot of light. Inside is a cathode-ray tube (CRT), a glass tube with almost all the air pumped out, and it has three main parts.

  1. Electron gun. A heated cathode releases electrons. A positive anode at voltage $V_a$ accelerates them into a narrow beam. The work done by the field becomes kinetic energy, so $eV_a=\tfrac12 mv^2$ and $$v=\sqrt{\frac{2eV_a}{m}}.$$ For $V_a=1000$ V this is about $1.9\times10^7$ m/s.
  2. Deflection plates. The beam passes between two pairs of parallel metal plates. A voltage $V$ across plates a distance $d$ apart makes a uniform electric field $E=V/d$, pointing from the $+$ plate to the $-$ plate. Each electron feels a force $F=eE$ towards the positive plate, so its acceleration is $a=\dfrac{eV}{md}$. Inside the plates it moves like a ball thrown sideways under gravity, along a parabola. After the plates it flies straight to the screen at an angle. The Y plates move the spot up and down and the X plates move it left and right.
  3. Phosphor screen. Where the beam lands, the coating glows. The glow fades slowly, so a fast-moving spot looks like a steady line.

For plates of length $l$ whose centre is a distance $L$ from the screen, the spot moves by $$y=\frac{L\,l\,V}{2\,d\,V_a}.$$ The deflection is proportional to the plate voltage $V$. This is why the screen can be marked in volts per division. A larger $V_a$ makes the electrons faster, so they spend less time between the plates and the deflection is smaller.

The scope can be used in two ways. In time-base mode the X plates receive a sawtooth voltage that sweeps the spot steadily from left to right and then snaps it back. Horizontal position then means time, and the screen draws the Y signal against time. In XY mode one signal goes to the X plates and another to the Y plates. The spot then does two SHMs at right angles and draws a Lissajous figure, which is how a scope compares two frequencies or measures a phase difference.

Lissajous figures from real oscillators

Any body that can oscillate in two perpendicular directions at once draws a Lissajous figure. A sand pendulum (a leaking funnel on a Y-shaped string) has different effective lengths in the two directions, so $\omega_x=\sqrt{g/L_x}$ and $\omega_y=\sqrt{g/L_y}$ differ and the sand traces the figure on the floor. A mass held by two pairs of springs does the same with $\omega_x=\sqrt{k_x/m}$ and $\omega_y=\sqrt{k_y/m}$. In the simulation the red and green markers show $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ separately, and the big graph shows how the two waves combine point by point into the figure.

Special cases for equal frequencies. $\delta=0$ gives a straight line $y=(b/a)x$. $\delta=\pi$ gives $y=-(b/a)x$. $\delta=\pi/2$ gives an ellipse, which is a circle when $a=b$.

Damped Harmonic Motion and the RLC Circuit

  • Damping force −bv
  • Differential equation of DHM
  • Under, critical and over damping
  • Logarithmic decrement, damping coefficient
  • LC and series RLC circuits
  • Reactance and impedance

Key ideas

Real oscillators lose energy to friction or resistance. With a damping force $-bv$ the amplitude decays exponentially. A spring–mass system with friction and an RLC circuit obey the same differential equation, with $m\leftrightarrow L$, $b\leftrightarrow R$, $k\leftrightarrow 1/C$ and $x\leftrightarrow q$. Here, I have defined $\dot x=\frac{dx}{dt}$.

Damped oscillator$$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
Standard form$$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\ \gamma=\frac b{2m}$$
Characteristic equation$$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0$$
Roots$$\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
Underdamped solution$$x=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega' t+\phi),\ \omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$$
LC circuit$$\frac{d^2q}{dt^2}+\frac{q}{LC}=0,\quad f=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$
Series RLC$$L\ddot q+R\dot q+\frac qC=0,\ \gamma=\frac R{2L}$$
Oscillatory if$$\frac1{LC}>\frac{R^2}{4L^2}$$
Log decrement$$\lambda=\ln\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}=\gamma T'$$
Reactance and impedance$$X_L=\omega L,\ X_C=\frac1{\omega C},\ Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}$$

Setting up the equation for a mass in water

Hang a block from a spring and let it oscillate inside a beaker of water. Its weight and the buoyant force only move the rest position a little, so we measure the displacement $x$ from that new rest position. When the block is at $x$ and moving with velocity $v=\dot x$, two forces act on it.

  1. Spring force. $F_s=-kx$. It always pulls the block back toward $x=0$.
  2. Damping force. At low speed the water pushes against the motion with a force proportional to the speed, $F_d=-bv=-b\dot x$. The constant $b$ is the damping coefficient, measured in kg/s. A thicker liquid such as oil or honey has a larger $b$.
  3. Newton's second law. $m\ddot x=-kx-b\dot x$, which gives $$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
  4. Divide by $m$. $$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad \gamma=\frac b{2m},\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac km}$$ Here $\gamma$ tells how strongly the liquid damps the motion and $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency the block would have with no damping.
  5. Same equation for a pendulum. For a bob swinging in a liquid, with small angle $\theta$, $$\ddot\theta+2\gamma\dot\theta+\frac gL\,\theta=0,\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac gL}$$ So everything below works for both systems.

Solving the damped equation with the exponential method

Exactly as for SHM, try $x=e^{\lambda t}$ in $\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$.

  1. Substitute. $(\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2)\,e^{\lambda t}=0$, so the characteristic equation is $$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
  2. Overdamped ($\gamma>\omega_0$). Two real negative roots. With $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{\beta t}+C_2e^{-\beta t}\right)$$ The body creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
  3. Critically damped ($\gamma=\omega_0$). One repeated root $\lambda=-\gamma$, $$x=(C_1+C_2t)\,e^{-\gamma t}$$ This returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without overshooting.
  4. Underdamped ($\gamma<\omega_0$). Complex roots $\lambda=-\gamma\pm i\omega'$ with $\omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{i\omega't}+C_2e^{-i\omega't}\right)=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega't+\phi)$$ The body oscillates at $\omega'$ inside a shrinking envelope $Ae^{-\gamma t}$.
  5. Check. Setting $\gamma=0$ gives back $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$, the SHM result.

Key ideas

A wave is a travelling disturbance that carries energy without carrying matter. In a transverse wave the medium moves perpendicular to the direction of travel (string, light). In a longitudinal wave it moves along it (sound).

Particle velocity and wave velocity are different things. Every particle does SHM about its own position while the pattern moves at $v$.

Relations$$k=\frac{2\pi}\lambda,\ \omega=2\pi f,\ v=f\lambda=\frac\omega k$$
Wave equation$$\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}$$
Particle velocity$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}=-v\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}$$
Wave on a string$$v=\sqrt{\frac T\mu}$$
Power and intensity$$P=\tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2,\quad I=2\pi^2f^2A^2\rho v$$
EM wave$$c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}=f\lambda,\quad \frac EB=c$$

Key ideas

Two identical waves travelling in opposite directions form a standing wave. The pattern does not move. Some points (nodes) never move and others (antinodes) swing with double amplitude.

A real signal is a group of waves. The crests inside it move at the phase velocity, while the envelope that carries the information moves at the group velocity.

Standing wave$$y=2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$$
Nodes and antinodes$$x_N=\frac{n\lambda}2,\quad x_A=\frac{(2n+1)\lambda}4$$
String fixed at both ends$$\lambda_n=\frac{2L}n,\ f_n=\frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac T\mu}$$
Phase and group velocity$$v_p=\frac\omega k,\quad v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}$$
Relation$$v_g=v_p-\lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$$
Doppler (sound)$$f'=f\,\frac{v\pm v_o}{v\mp v_s}$$

In the Doppler formula use the upper signs when source and observer move toward each other. Radar speed guns, ultrasound blood-flow meters and the red shift of galaxies all use this effect.

Show solution

$v_p=\omega/k=\sqrt{g/k}$. $v_g=d\omega/dk=\tfrac12\sqrt{g/k}=\tfrac12v_p$.

Key ideas

Charge comes in whole multiples of $e=1.6\times10^{-19}$ C. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract with a force that falls as $1/r^2$. When there are many charges, the forces simply add as vectors (superposition).

The electric field $\vec E=\vec F/q_0$ is the force per unit test charge. Gauss's law says the total flux of $\vec E$ out of any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$. With the right symmetry it gives $E$ in one line.

Quantisation$$q=ne$$
Coulomb's law$$F=k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2},\ k=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=8.99\times10^9$$
Point charge field$$E=\frac{kq}{r^2}$$
Dipole moment and torque$$p=qd,\quad\vec\tau=\vec p\times\vec E,\quad U=-\vec p\cdot\vec E$$
Dipole field on axis$$E=\frac{2kp}{r^3}\ (r\gg d)$$
Flux$$\Phi_E=\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A$$
Gauss's law$$\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A=\frac{q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Line and plane$$E_{line}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r},\ E_{sheet}=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$
Charged sphere$$E=\frac{kQ}{r^2}\ (r>R),\quad E_{inside\ conductor}=0$$

Show solution

Three charges lie on the x axis. $q_1=+25$ nC at the origin, $q_2=-12$ nC at $x=2$ m, $q_3=+18$ nC at $x=3$ m. Find the net force on $q_1$.

Key ideas

The potential $V=U/q$ is the potential energy per unit charge. It is a scalar, so adding potentials of many charges is just adding numbers with signs. Worked examples

A downward field of 150 N/C moves an electron 520 m upward. Find the change in its potential energy and the change in potential.

Show solution

A charge of −1.0 µC is at (0, 1 m) and +1.0 µC is at (1 m, 0). At the origin find $|E|$, its direction, $V$, and the work needed to bring a +1.0 µC charge there from infinity.

  • Energy stored
  • Energy density
  • Electron volt

Key ideas

A capacitor stores charge and energy in the electric field between two conductors. Its capacitance $C=q/V$ depends only on geometry and the material between the plates. Every DRAM bit in your laptop is a tiny capacitor.

A dielectric is polarised by the field. The induced surface charges reduce the field inside, so for the same charge the voltage drops and $C$ rises by the factor $\kappa$.

Definition$$C=\frac qV$$
Parallel plate$$C=\frac{\kappa\varepsilon_0A}{d}$$
Cylindrical and spherical$$C=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}{\ln(b/a)},\ C=4\pi\varepsilon_0\frac{ab}{b-a}$$
Combinations$$C_p=\sum C_i,\quad\frac1{C_s}=\sum\frac1{C_i}$$
Energy$$U=\frac{q^2}{2C}=\tfrac12CV^2$$
Energy density$$u=\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$$
D, E and P$$\vec D=\varepsilon_0\vec E+\vec P=\kappa\varepsilon_0\vec E$$

Worked examples

Example 1

A storage capacitor on a RAM chip has $C=55$ fF and is charged to 5.3 V. How many excess electrons are on its negative plate?

A 3.55 µF capacitor charged to 6.30 V is connected across an uncharged 8.95 µF capacitor. Find the final common voltage.

Show solution

Charge is conserved. $C_1V_0=(C_1+C_2)V$ so $V=\dfrac{3.55\times6.30}{3.55+8.95}=1.79$ V.

Example 3

A parallel plate capacitor has $A=0.01$ m² and $d=1$ mm and is connected to 12 V. Find $C$, $q$, $U$ and the energy density.

Show solution

$C=\varepsilon_0A/d=88.5$ pF, $q=CV=1.06$ nC, $U=\tfrac12CV^2=6.4$ nJ. $E=V/d=1.2\times10^4$ V/m so $u=\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2=6.4\times10^{-4}$ J/m³.

Photon$$E=hf=\frac{hc}\lambda,\ p=\frac h\lambda$$
Handy form$$E\,(\text{eV})=\frac{1240}{\lambda\,(\text{nm})}$$
Einstein's equation$$K_{\max}=hf-\phi=eV_0$$
Threshold$$f_0=\frac\phi h,\ \lambda_0=\frac{hc}\phi$$
Compton shift$$\Delta\lambda=\frac h{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)$$
Compton wavelength$$\frac h{m_ec}=2.43\ \text{pm}$$

Photoelectrons have energies from zero to $4\times10^{-19}$ J when 3000 Å light falls on a surface. Find the stopping potential and the threshold wavelength.

X-rays, de Broglie Waves, the Uncertainty Principle and Pair Production

  • X-ray production and properties
  • Applications of X-rays
  • Bragg diffraction
  • de Broglie wavelength
  • Heisenberg's uncertainty principle
  • Correspondence principle
  • Pair production and annihilation

X-rays are made when fast electrons slam into a metal target. The shortest wavelength appears when an electron gives all its energy $eV$ to one photon. Because X-ray wavelengths match atomic spacings, crystals diffract them, which lets us measure crystal structures.

If light waves act like particles, particles should act like waves. de Broglie proposed $\lambda=h/p$. Electron diffraction proved it. A wave cannot have both a sharp position and a sharp wavelength, which is the uncertainty principle.

Cut-off wavelength$$\lambda_{\min}=\frac{hc}{eV}$$
Bragg's law$$2d\sin\theta=n\lambda$$
de Broglie$$\lambda=\frac hp=\frac h{mv}$$
Accelerated electron$$\lambda=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}=\frac{1.226}{\sqrt V}\ \text{nm}$$
Uncertainty$$\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\frac\hbar2,\ \Delta E\,\Delta t\ge\frac\hbar2$$
Pair production threshold$$hf\ge2m_ec^2=1.022\ \text{MeV}$$

The correspondence principle says quantum results must reduce to classical ones when quantum numbers become large. In pair production a photon near a nucleus turns into an electron and a positron. In annihilation the pair disappears into two 0.511 MeV photons, which is what a PET scanner detects.

Worked examples

Example 1

A 20 keV electron is brought to rest in one collision with a heavy metal target. Find the frequency of the photon produced.

Show solution

$f=eV/h=(20\times10^3)(1.6\times10^{-19})/6.63\times10^{-34}=4.8\times10^{18}$ Hz, so $\lambda_{\min}=62$ pm.

Example 2

A football of 0.4 kg moves at 30 m/s. Find its de Broglie wavelength. Why don't we see it diffract?

Show solution

$\lambda=h/mv=6.6\times10^{-34}/12=5.5\times10^{-35}$ m. This is far smaller than any opening, so wave effects are invisible.

Example 3

An electron is confined to 0.1 nm. Find the minimum uncertainty in its momentum and speed.

Show solution

$\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\Delta x=5.3\times10^{-25}$ kg m/s, so $\Delta v\ge\Delta p/m=5.8\times10^5$ m/s.

Practice

  1. Find $\lambda_{\min}$ for a 30 kV X-ray tube.
    Answer41 pm
  2. Cu Kα X-rays (0.154 nm) reflect from NaCl planes with $d=0.282$ nm. Find the first-order Bragg angle.
    Answer15.8°
  3. Find the de Broglie wavelength of a 54 eV electron (the Davisson–Germer experiment).
    Answer0.167 nm

The Schrödinger Equation and its Applications

  • Wave function
  • Time dependent and independent forms
  • Expectation value
  • Operators
  • Tunnelling
  • Particle in a box
  • Quantum dots and corrals
  • Bohr orbits and quantum numbers

The state of a particle is described by a wave function $\psi$. Its square $|\psi|^2$ gives the probability of finding the particle at a place. The Schrödinger equation tells how $\psi$ behaves. Confining a particle makes its energy quantised, and a barrier is not a perfect wall.

Time dependent$$i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}+U\Psi$$
Time independent$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}+U\psi=E\psi$$
Normalisation$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2dx=1$$
Expectation value$$\langle x\rangle=\int\psi^*x\,\psi\,dx$$
Operators$$\hat p=-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\ \hat E=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}$$
Particle in a box$$\psi_n=\sqrt{\tfrac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}L,\ E_n=\frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}$$
Tunnelling$$T\approx e^{-2\kappa L},\ \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(U_0-E)}}\hbar$$
Bohr atom$$E_n=-\frac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV},\ r_n=n^2a_0$$
Quantum numbers$$n,\ l=0..n-1,\ m_l=-l..l,\ m_s=\pm\tfrac12$$

A quantum corral is a ring of atoms placed one by one on a metal surface with an STM tip. Electrons trapped inside form circular standing waves, a two-dimensional particle in a box you can actually see.

Worked examples

Example 1

An electron is trapped in a 0.5 nm box. Find $E_1$ and the wavelength emitted in the jump $n=2\to1$.

Show solution

$E_1=h^2/8mL^2=1.50$ eV. $E_2-E_1=3E_1=4.51$ eV, so $\lambda=1240/4.51=275$ nm.

Example 2

Show that $\langle x\rangle=L/2$ for any state of the particle in a box.

Show solution

$\langle x\rangle=\frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx$. Using $\sin^2u=\frac12(1-\cos2u)$ the cosine part integrates to zero, leaving $\frac1L\int_0^Lx\,dx=L/2$. This matches the symmetry of $|\psi|^2$.

Example 3

An electron of 1 eV meets a 5 eV barrier 0.5 nm wide. Estimate $T$. What happens if the width is halved?

Show solution

$\kappa=\sqrt{2m(4\text{ eV})}/\hbar=1.02\times10^{10}$ m⁻¹. $T\approx e^{-2(1.02\times10^{10})(0.5\times10^{-9})}=3.6\times10^{-5}$. Halving $L$ gives $6.0\times10^{-3}$, about 170 times larger. Tunnelling is extremely sensitive to width.
