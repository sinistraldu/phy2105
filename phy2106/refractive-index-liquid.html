Refractive Index of a Liquid
by the Plane Mirror and Pin Method using a Convex Lens

Theory, illustrations, a live ray simulator, step by step procedure and a data calculator

Aim and apparatusTheoryDerivationsSimulator ProcedureSpherometerObservations PrecautionsViva

Aim and apparatus

Aim. To find the refractive index μ of a given transparent liquid (for example water) using a convex lens, a plane mirror and a pin.

  • Convex lens (focal length about 15 to 25 cm)
  • Plane mirror (a polished, flat mirror strip)
  • Optical needle (pin)
  • Clamp stand with a horizontal arm
  • Plumb line and a metre scale
  • Spherometer and a flat glass plate
  • The given liquid and a dropper
  • Soft cloth for cleaning

Theory

1. Why the pin and its image coincide at the focus

The convex lens rests on a horizontal plane mirror. A pin is held horizontally above the lens with its tip on the principal axis (the vertical line through the optical centre).

If the pin tip is exactly at the principal focus of the lens, every ray from the tip leaves the lens parallel to the axis. Parallel vertical rays strike the horizontal mirror normally (at 90°), so they are reflected straight back along the same path. Passing through the lens again they converge back to the very point they started from.

So the image of the pin is formed at the pin itself. It is real, inverted and the same size. Looking down from above you see the pin tip and the image tip touching. When you move your eye sideways they do not separate. This condition is called no parallax.

The distance from the pin to the lens at that position is the focal length.

2. The liquid forms a plano-concave lens

A few drops of liquid are put on the mirror and the lens is placed over them. The liquid spreads into the gap. Its lower face is flat (it touches the mirror) and its upper face takes the shape of the lens surface, which is curved inward into the liquid.

That thin layer is therefore a plano-concave liquid lens. It is diverging, so it has a negative focal length f₂.

The glass lens and the liquid lens are in contact, so they act as one combination of focal length F. Because a diverging lens is added, F is longer than f₁. You will find the no-parallax position higher up.

3. Equations used

Two lenses in contact

1F = 1f₁ + 1f₂   ⟹   |f₂| = f₁ FF − f₁

Lens maker's formula for the liquid lens with surfaces of radius R and ∞

1|f₂| = (μ − 1)1R   ⟹   μ = 1 + R|f₂|

Radius of curvature from the spherometer (l is the mean distance between the legs, h is the sagitta)

R = l²6h + h2

Each focal length is the mean of two distances measured from the pin tip, x₁ to the top of the lens and x₂ to the mirror. This gives the distance to the middle of the lens.

f = x₁ + x₂2

Here R is the radius of the lens surface that touches the liquid (the lower face).

Detailed theory and derivations

Sign convention used throughout is the Cartesian convention. Distances are measured from the optical centre (or the pole), positive in the direction in which light travels and negative against it. Heights above the axis are positive. All angles are small (paraxial rays), so sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ.

D1. Refraction at a single spherical surface

A ray from a point object O on the axis meets a spherical surface of radius R at a point P, a height h above the axis. The surface separates media of refractive index n₁ (incident side) and n₂. The refracted ray crosses the axis at the image I. Let the ray make angles α with the axis at O, β with the axis at I, and let the normal CP make angle γ with the axis.

Snell's law at P for small angles gives

$$n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_1\, i \approx n_2\, r .$$

From the triangles OPC and IPC, using the exterior angle theorem,

$$i = \alpha + \gamma, \qquad r = \gamma - \beta .$$

For small angles each angle equals the height h divided by the corresponding distance,

$$\alpha \approx \frac{h}{-u}, \qquad \beta \approx \frac{h}{v}, \qquad \gamma \approx \frac{h}{R}.$$

Substituting into n₁ i = n₂ r and cancelling h,

$$n_1\left(\frac{1}{-u}+\frac{1}{R}\right) = n_2\left(\frac{1}{R}-\frac{1}{v}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\frac{n_2}{v}-\frac{n_1}{u}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}\;}$$

D2. Lens maker's formula

A thin lens of index μ in air (index 1) has two surfaces of radii R₁ and R₂. Apply D1 twice. At the first surface (air to glass) the image of O is formed at v′

$$\frac{\mu}{v'}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{\mu-1}{R_1}.$$

This image is the object for the second surface (glass to air). For a thin lens the thickness is negligible, so the object distance is the same v′

$$\frac{1}{v}-\frac{\mu}{v'}=\frac{1-\mu}{R_2}.$$

Adding the two equations removes v′

$$\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}=(\mu-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}\right).$$

For an object at infinity (u → −∞) the image is at the focus, v = f, which gives the lens maker's formula and the lens formula

$$\boxed{\;\frac{1}{f}=(\mu-1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1}-\frac{1}{R_2}\right)\;},\qquad \boxed{\;\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{1}{f}\;}$$

D3. The glass lens and the liquid lens

Glass lens. For an equiconvex lens of index μg, the first surface has its centre on the far side (R₁ = +R) and the second has its centre on the near side (R₂ = −R)

$$\frac{1}{f_1}=(\mu_g-1)\left(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{R}\right)=\frac{2(\mu_g-1)}{R}.$$

For μg = 1.5 this gives f₁ = R, which is why a common equiconvex lens has a focal length close to its radius.

Liquid lens. The liquid fills the space between the lower face of the glass lens and the mirror. Light going downward first meets the curved face of the liquid. That face bulges into the liquid, so its centre of curvature lies on the incoming side, R₁ = −R. The lower face lies on the flat mirror, R₂ = ∞. With liquid index μ

$$\frac{1}{f_2}=(\mu-1)\left(\frac{1}{-R}-\frac{1}{\infty}\right)=-\frac{\mu-1}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_2=-\frac{R}{\mu-1}.$$

f₂ is negative, so the liquid layer is a diverging (plano-concave) lens, as expected.

D4. Two thin lenses in contact

Let the first lens form an image at v₁ of an object at u. That image is the object for the second lens, which is in contact, and forms the final image at v

$$\frac{1}{v_1}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{1}{f_1},\qquad \frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{v_1}=\frac{1}{f_2}.$$

Adding removes v₁, so the pair acts as one lens of focal length F with

$$\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{u}=\frac{1}{f_1}+\frac{1}{f_2}\equiv\frac{1}{F} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\frac{1}{F}=\frac{1}{f_1}+\frac{1}{f_2}\;}$$

In terms of power P = 1/f, the powers simply add, P = P₁ + P₂. Since P₂ < 0, the combination is weaker than the glass lens alone, and F > f₁.

D5. Why the pin and image coincide only at the focus

Light from the pin passes through the lens, reflects at the plane mirror and passes through the lens again. A plane mirror only reverses the direction of travel, so the system "lens + mirror + lens" has power

$$P_{\text{sys}}=P+P=\frac{2}{F}.$$

The system therefore behaves like a concave mirror of focal length F/2. Using the mirror formula with object and image both measured upward from the lens (real distances positive)

$$\frac{1}{v}+\frac{1}{u}=\frac{2}{F}.$$

The image falls on the pin when v = u. Then 2/u = 2/F, so

$$\boxed{\;u=v=F\;},\qquad m=-\frac{v}{u}=-1 .$$

The image is real, inverted and the same size, exactly what is seen tip to tip. If the pin is higher than F, then v < u and the image lies below the pin. If it is lower than F (but above F/2), then v > u and the image is above the pin. Only at u = F is there no parallax. The simulator above uses exactly this ray algebra.

D6. Parallax rule

Let the eye be at height E above the mirror and move sideways by a small distance e. A point at height y on the axis then appears at an angular position

$$\phi(y)\approx\frac{-e}{E-y}.$$

Two points at different heights y₁ < y₂ appear to separate by

$$\Delta\phi=\phi(y_2)-\phi(y_1)=-e\left(\frac{1}{E-y_2}-\frac{1}{E-y_1}\right)\neq 0 \quad\text{unless}\quad y_1=y_2 .$$

The nearer point (larger y) shifts more against the eye, so the farther point appears to move with the eye. Parallax vanishes only when the pin and image are at the same height, which is the test used in the experiment.

D7. Spherometer formula

The three legs of a spherometer form an equilateral triangle of side l. They sit on a circle of radius r through the three legs, where

$$r=\frac{l}{\sqrt3}.$$

The central screw rises by h above the plane of the legs when placed on a sphere of radius R. In the vertical section through the centre, the chord of half length r and the sagitta h satisfy the intersecting chords theorem

$$r\cdot r = h\,(2R-h) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R=\frac{r^2}{2h}+\frac{h}{2}=\boxed{\;\frac{l^2}{6h}+\frac{h}{2}\;}$$

Here h is the difference between the reading on the lens and the reading on a flat glass plate.

D8. Final working formula

From D4, the focal length of the liquid lens is

$$\frac{1}{f_2}=\frac{1}{F}-\frac{1}{f_1}=\frac{f_1-F}{f_1F}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f_2|=\frac{f_1F}{F-f_1}.$$

From D3, |f₂| = R/(μ − 1), so

$$\boxed{\;\mu=1+\frac{R}{|f_2|}=1+R\,\frac{F-f_1}{f_1F}\;}$$

Why (x₁ + x₂)/2. The pin height is measured to the top of the lens (x₁) and to the mirror (x₂). The thin lens formula refers to the optical centre, which lies close to the middle of the lens, so the mean of the two is the correct distance.

D9. Worked example

Typical readings for water are f₁ = 20.07 cm, F = 30.08 cm, l = 3.00 cm and h = 0.076 cm.

$$R=\frac{(3.00)^2}{6(0.076)}+\frac{0.076}{2}=19.74+0.04=19.78\ \text{cm}$$ $$|f_2|=\frac{(20.07)(30.08)}{30.08-20.07}=60.31\ \text{cm},\qquad \mu=1+\frac{19.78}{60.31}=1.328$$

This is close to the accepted value 1.333 for water.

D10. Error estimate

Write μ − 1 = R(1/f₁ − 1/F). Taking logarithms and differentiating gives the maximum fractional error

$$\frac{\delta(\mu-1)}{\mu-1}=\frac{\delta R}{R}+\frac{\dfrac{\delta f_1}{f_1^2}+\dfrac{\delta F}{F^2}}{\dfrac{1}{f_1}-\dfrac{1}{F}}, \qquad \frac{\delta R}{R}\approx\frac{2\,\delta l}{l}+\frac{\delta h}{h}.$$

Since h is very small (under 1 mm), the δh/h term usually dominates. A spherometer with a good least count, and averaging several readings of h, matter most for accuracy.

Procedure, step by step

1

Rough focal length

Hold the lens in front of a wall and focus the image of a distant window or tree on it. The lens to wall distance is a rough value of f₁. This tells you roughly where to put the pin.

2

Set up the mirror and lens

Clean and dry the mirror and the lens. Put the mirror on a horizontal table and place the lens on it. Fix the pin horizontally in the clamp so that its tip lies vertically above the optical centre of the lens (check with a plumb line).

3

Look for the inverted image

Look vertically down from about 30 cm above the pin. You see the pin and, through the lens, an inverted image of it. Usually the two tips are not together and they slide apart when you move your eye.

4

Remove parallax (lens alone)

Move the pin up or down until the tips touch and stay together however you move your eye. If the image follows your eye, it is farther than the pin, so lower the pin. If the pin follows your eye, raise the pin.

5

Measure f₁

With the plumb line and metre scale measure x₁ (pin tip to top of lens) and x₂ (pin tip to mirror). Then f₁ = (x₁ + x₂)/2. Disturb the pin, find the position again and repeat for at least three readings.

6

Introduce the liquid

Lift the lens, put a few drops of liquid on the mirror and replace the lens gently. Avoid air bubbles. The liquid now forms a plano-concave lens. With the pin left where it was, you will see parallax again because the focal length has changed.

7

Remove parallax again and measure F

Raise the pin until parallax disappears once more. Measure x₁ and x₂ again and find F = (x₁ + x₂)/2. Take three readings.

8

Measure R with the spherometer

Remove the lens, wipe off the liquid. Find the spherometer reading on a flat glass plate and then on the lens surface that touched the liquid. The difference is h. Press the spherometer on paper to get the three leg marks and measure the mean leg distance l. Calculate R.

9

Calculate μ

Find |f₂| = f₁F/(F − f₁) and then μ = 1 + R/|f₂|. Use the calculator below with your own readings.

Precautions and sources of error

  • The mirror and lens must be clean, and dry for the first part.
  • Use only a few drops of liquid so that it does not spread beyond the lens.
  • No air bubbles should be trapped in the liquid layer.
  • The pin tip must be on the principal axis, checked with a plumb line.
  • Keep the eye at least 30 cm above the pin so the view is clear.
  • Parallax must be removed tip to tip, slowly and carefully.
  • Find R for the face that was in contact with the liquid.
  • Avoid backlash error in the spherometer by turning the screw in one direction only.
  • A thin lens is assumed. Thickness of the lens is taken care of by averaging x₁ and x₂.
  • Liquid should not be volatile, or readings must be taken quickly.

Quick viva questions

Why is the image formed at the pin itself when the pin is at the focus?

Rays from the focus come out of the lens parallel, fall normally on the mirror and retrace their path, so they meet again at the focus.

What kind of lens does the liquid form?

A plano-concave lens, which is diverging and has negative focal length.

Why is F greater than f₁?

A diverging lens is added to a converging one, so the total power decreases and the focal length increases.

What happens if the liquid has the same μ as the glass?

The liquid lens then cancels one surface of the glass lens, leaving a plano-convex glass lens, and F becomes 2f₁ for an equiconvex lens with μ = 1.5 glass.

Can this method be used for a liquid with μ greater than that of the glass?

Yes, as long as the combination is still converging. If μ is large enough the combination becomes diverging and no real image forms, so the method fails.

Why do we take the mean of x₁ and x₂?

The focal length is measured from the optical centre, which lies roughly midway between the top and bottom of the lens.
