Determination of the Frequency of a Tuning Fork by Melde's Experiment

Standing waves on a string driven by an electrically maintained fork, in transverse and longitudinal arrangements

Aim and apparatus

Aim. To determine the frequency n of an electrically maintained tuning fork by Melde's method, using both the transverse and the longitudinal arrangement.

  • Electrically maintained tuning fork on a stand
  • Battery or low voltage supply, key and connecting wires
  • Light, uniform, inextensible thread (2 to 3 m)
  • Smooth pulley clamped to the table edge
  • Light scale pan and a set of small weights
  • Metre scale and a physical balance

Theory

1. Waves on a stretched string

A transverse wave on a string of mass per unit length m under tension T travels with speed

v = √Tm

In Melde's experiment the tension is supplied by a pan of weights hanging over a pulley, so T = (M + m₀)g, where M is the load and m₀ is the pan mass.

2. Standing waves and loops

Waves sent from the fork reflect at the pulley and travel back. The two waves add and form a stationary wave. Both ends behave as nodes, so the string can only resonate when its vibrating length l holds a whole number p of half wavelengths

l = p λ2    ⟹    fstring = vλ = p2l√Tm

Each half wavelength appears as one bright loop. When the condition is met exactly the loops are large and steady with sharp nodes. Slightly off resonance the string only shivers.

3. The two arrangements

Transverse arrangement. Each swing of the prong pushes the thread sideways once, so the string vibrates at the same frequency as the fork.

n = p2l√Tm

Longitudinal arrangement. The prong only tightens and slackens the thread. The string bulges up during one fork cycle and down during the next, so it completes one vibration for every two vibrations of the fork. The string frequency is n/2.

n = pl√Tm

For the same tension and length the longitudinal arrangement therefore gives half as many loops. Also, for a fixed length, T·p² stays constant in each arrangement, which is a quick check on your readings.

Why it works, in simple words

You do not need to memorise any derivation. If you understand the ten ideas below, every formula and every step of the experiment will make sense.

1. Why do we see loops on the thread?

The fork shakes one end of the thread. A wave runs along the thread to the pulley, bounces back and runs toward the fork again. Now two waves are on the same thread, one going and one coming back. Where they always cancel, the thread stays still. These are the nodes. Where they always add up, the thread swings the most. These are the antinodes. Because the thread moves so fast, your eye sees a blur shaped like a row of loops.

Each loop is exactly half a wavelength long.

2. Why do loops appear only for certain loads or lengths?

Both ends of the thread (at the fork and at the pulley) must be still points. So a whole number of loops must fit exactly between them. If they do not fit, the returning wave arrives at the wrong moment and fights the new wave, so the thread only shivers.

Think of pushing a child on a swing. Push at the right moment every time and the swing goes higher and higher. Push at random moments and nothing builds up. When the loops fit exactly, every new wave arrives "at the right moment", and the loops grow big and steady. That is resonance.

$$\text{length} = p \times \frac{\lambda}{2}\qquad (p = \text{number of loops})$$

3. Why does a heavier load give fewer loops?

A heavier load pulls the thread tighter. Waves run faster on a tight thread (this is why tightening a guitar string raises its pitch). The fork still shakes at the same rate, so a faster wave travels further in one shake, which means each wave is longer. Longer waves mean bigger loops, so fewer of them fit in the same length.

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\qquad\text{tighter thread (bigger } T) \Rightarrow \text{faster waves}$$

4. Why does the mass of the thread matter?

A heavy thread is harder to move, so a wave travels more slowly along it. That is why m (mass per metre) sits in the bottom of the formula for v. We weigh a long piece of thread because a short piece is too light to weigh accurately.

5. Transverse arrangement. Why is the thread's frequency the same as the fork's?

Here the prong moves up and down, across the thread. When the prong goes up, it lifts the thread. When it comes down, it pulls the thread down. So in one fork vibration the thread gets one push up and one push down. The thread simply follows the prong, making one up and down swing for each fork vibration.

$$\text{thread frequency} = n$$

6. Longitudinal arrangement. Why is the thread's frequency only half?

Now the prong moves back and forth along the thread. It cannot push the thread up or down at all. All it can do is make the thread tight (prong moves away from the pulley) and then slack (prong moves toward the pulley). So in each fork vibration there is exactly one tight pull.

A tight pull helps the swing only if it comes when the thread is bent the most, that is when it is at the very top or the very bottom of its swing. At that moment the pull straightens the thread hard and throws it back the other way, adding energy, just like the push on a swing.

But in one complete swing the thread is at the top once and at the bottom once. So the thread needs two tight pulls for each of its own swings, one at the top and one at the bottom. The fork gives one pull per vibration, so it must vibrate twice while the thread swings once.

$$\text{thread frequency} = \frac{n}{2}$$

Everyday picture. A child "pumps" a swing by standing up at each end of the swing, front and back. That is two pumps for each full swing. The pumping is twice as fast as the swinging. In the longitudinal arrangement the fork is the pumping child.

7. How the two formulas come from this

For a thread with p loops, the thread's own frequency is

$$f_{\text{thread}}=\frac{p}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}$$

Transverse, the fork frequency equals the thread frequency. Longitudinal, the fork frequency is twice the thread frequency. So

$$\text{Transverse: } n=\frac{p}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\qquad\qquad \text{Longitudinal: } n=\frac{p}{l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}$$

The only difference is the factor 2, and it comes straight from idea 6.

8. A quick check you can do in the lab

Keep the same load and the same length and turn the fork from transverse to longitudinal. The thread now vibrates at half the frequency, so its waves become twice as long, and you should see half as many loops. If you had 6 loops, expect 3.

9. Why include the pan, and why a smooth pulley?

The tension is the total weight hanging on the thread, which is the load plus the pan, T = (M + m₀)g. A rough pulley would hold back part of that weight, so the real tension would be less than we calculate.

10. Why measure the length between nodes?

The fork end of the thread is moving a little, so it is not a perfect still point. The first clear node near the fork and the point where the thread touches the pulley are true still points, so the distance between them is the true length of the loops.

Worked example

Transverse, load 40 g, pan 5 g, 5 loops in 1.125 m, thread 4.2 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m.

$$T=(0.045)(9.8)=0.441\text{ N},\quad v=\sqrt{\frac{0.441}{0.00042}}=32.4\text{ m/s},\quad n=\frac{5}{2\times1.125}\times32.4=72\text{ Hz}$$

Procedure, with the theory behind each step

1

Find the mass per unit length of the thread

Weigh a long, measured length of the same thread (for example 5.00 m) on a sensitive balance. Then m = mass ÷ length. Example 2.10 g ÷ 5.00 m = 0.420 g/m = 4.20 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m.

Why. The wave speed v = √(T/m) depends on m. A long piece is weighed because a short piece is too light to weigh accurately.
2

Set up the transverse arrangement

Tie one end of the thread to a prong of the fork. Pass the thread over the pulley and tie the pan to the other end. Turn the fork so that its prongs vibrate at right angles to the thread. Connect the battery and key.

Why. With the prong moving across the thread, the thread is pushed sideways once in every fork cycle, so the string is driven at the fork frequency n.
3

Start the fork and adjust for sharp loops

Close the key so the fork vibrates. Add or remove small weights, or slide the fork stand, until the thread vibrates in a number of well defined, steady loops with sharp nodes.

Why. Resonance needs l = pλ/2 exactly. Changing T changes v and so λ, and changing l changes how many half waves fit. When the condition is met the amplitude builds up and the loops are clear.
4

Count loops and measure the length

Count the number of loops p. Measure the length l from the first node near the prong to the point where the thread leaves the pulley. Note the load M on the pan and the pan mass m₀.

Why. These three quantities, with m, are all that the formula n = (p/2l)√(T/m) needs. Measuring between nodes avoids error from the small motion at the prong end.
5

Repeat for different loads

Change the load to get a different number of loops and repeat steps 3 and 4. Take at least four sets.

Why. Several independent sets reduce random error. Heavier loads give faster waves, longer λ and so fewer loops for the same length. For a fixed l, T·p² should stay nearly constant.
6

Change to the longitudinal arrangement

Turn the fork through 90° so that the prongs vibrate along the length of the thread. Repeat steps 3 to 5.

Why. Now the prong only changes the tension, once per fork cycle. The string vibrates at n/2, so for the same T and l you see half as many loops, and the formula becomes n = (p/l)√(T/m).
7

Calculate n and plot a graph

Work out n for every reading and take the mean. As a graphical check, plot √T against l/p. Both arrangements give straight lines through the origin with slopes 2n√m (transverse) and n√m (longitudinal).

Why. Rearranging the two formulas gives √T = 2n√m·(l/p) and √T = n√m·(l/p). A straight line confirms the theory and the slope gives n from all readings together.

Precautions and sources of error

  • The thread should be light, uniform and nearly inextensible.
  • The thread between fork and pulley must be horizontal.
  • The pulley must be smooth and frictionless so the full weight sets the tension.
  • Include the mass of the pan in the load.
  • Adjust until the loops are sharp and steady before taking a reading.
  • Measure l between nodes, not to the prong itself.
  • The fork's prong should not touch the electromagnet. Keep the current steady.
  • Air currents and a sagging thread change the effective length.

Quick viva questions

Why are fewer loops seen in the longitudinal arrangement?

The string vibrates at half the fork frequency, so λ is twice as long for the same tension, giving half as many loops in the same length.

What are nodes and antinodes?

Nodes are points of zero displacement. Antinodes are points of maximum displacement, halfway between nodes.

What happens to the number of loops if the tension is increased?

v and λ increase, so fewer half wavelengths fit in the length and p decreases. p is proportional to 1/√T.

Why is the fork electrically maintained?

An ordinary fork dies away in seconds. The electromagnet gives it a push each cycle, so the amplitude stays constant while the string is adjusted and observed.

What kind of wave is set up on the string?

A transverse stationary wave formed by the superposition of incident and reflected waves.
