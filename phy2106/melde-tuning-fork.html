Determination of the Frequency of a Tuning Fork by Melde's Experiment

Standing waves on a string driven by an electrically maintained fork, in transverse and longitudinal arrangements

Aim and apparatusTheoryDerivations3D virtual lab Procedure with theoryObservations and graphPrecautionsViva

Aim and apparatus

Aim. To determine the frequency n of an electrically maintained tuning fork by Melde's method, using both the transverse and the longitudinal arrangement.

  • Electrically maintained tuning fork on a stand
  • Battery or low voltage supply, key and connecting wires
  • Light, uniform, inextensible thread (2 to 3 m)
  • Smooth pulley clamped to the table edge
  • Light scale pan and a set of small weights
  • Metre scale and a physical balance

Theory

1. Waves on a stretched string

A transverse wave on a string of mass per unit length m under tension T travels with speed

v = √Tm

In Melde's experiment the tension is supplied by a pan of weights hanging over a pulley, so T = (M + m₀)g, where M is the load and m₀ is the pan mass.

2. Standing waves and loops

Waves sent from the fork reflect at the pulley and travel back. The two waves add and form a stationary wave. Both ends behave as nodes, so the string can only resonate when its vibrating length l holds a whole number p of half wavelengths

l = p λ2    ⟹    fstring = vλ = p2l√Tm

Each half wavelength appears as one bright loop. When the condition is met exactly the loops are large and steady with sharp nodes. Slightly off resonance the string only shivers.

3. The two arrangements

Transverse arrangement. Each swing of the prong pushes the thread sideways once, so the string vibrates at the same frequency as the fork.

n = p2l√Tm

Longitudinal arrangement. The prong only tightens and slackens the thread. The string bulges up during one fork cycle and down during the next, so it completes one vibration for every two vibrations of the fork. The string frequency is n/2.

n = pl√Tm

For the same tension and length the longitudinal arrangement therefore gives half as many loops. Also, for a fixed length, T·p² stays constant in each arrangement, which is a quick check on your readings.

Detailed theory and derivations

D1. Wave equation for a stretched string

Consider a small piece of string between x and x + dx, with mass m dx, where m is the mass per unit length. Let y(x, t) be its sideways displacement. The tension T is the same everywhere for small displacements. At each end the tension pulls along the string, at angles θ(x) and θ(x + dx) to the x axis.

The sideways components of the two tension forces do not cancel, because the string is curved

$$F_y=T\sin\theta(x+dx)-T\sin\theta(x)\approx T\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{x+dx}-T\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right]_{x}=T\,\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}\,dx .$$

Here we used sin θ ≈ tan θ = ∂y/∂x for small slopes. Newton's second law for the piece is Fy = (m dx) ∂²y/∂t², so

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}=\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2},\qquad v=\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\;}$$

This is the wave equation. Any function y = f(x − vt) or g(x + vt) satisfies it, so disturbances travel along the string with speed v = √(T/m).

D2. Travelling waves and stationary waves

Take the origin at a fixed end. A wave travelling one way and its reflection travelling back are

$$y_1=a\sin(kx-\omega t),\qquad y_2=a\sin(kx+\omega t),\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\quad \omega=2\pi f,\quad v=\frac{\omega}{k}=f\lambda .$$

Their sum is zero at x = 0 for all t, as a fixed end requires. Using sin A + sin B = 2 sin((A + B)/2) cos((A − B)/2)

$$y=y_1+y_2=2a\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t).$$

Every point moves in step with amplitude 2a sin kx. The pattern does not travel, so it is a stationary (standing) wave.

  • Nodes, where sin kx = 0, at x = 0, λ/2, λ, 3λ/2, …, never move.
  • Antinodes, where |sin kx| = 1, at x = λ/4, 3λ/4, …, have the largest amplitude.

The distance between neighbouring nodes is λ/2. Each segment between two nodes is seen as one loop.

D3. Normal modes of a string fixed at both ends

Both ends are (very nearly) nodes, so y(0, t) = 0 and y(l, t) = 0. The second condition requires

$$\sin(kl)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad kl=p\pi,\quad p=1,2,3,\ldots$$ $$\lambda_p=\frac{2l}{p},\qquad \boxed{\;f_p=\frac{v}{\lambda_p}=\frac{p}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\;}$$

A string of length l can only vibrate strongly at these frequencies, its normal modes. In mode p it shows p loops.

D4. Why the loops are sharp only at resonance

The fork end is not a perfect node. It moves with a small amplitude a, y(0, t) = a cos ωt, while the pulley end is fixed, y(l, t) = 0. A stationary solution of the wave equation that meets both conditions is

$$y(x,t)=a\,\frac{\sin k(l-x)}{\sin kl}\,\cos\omega t .$$

Check it. At x = 0 it gives a cos ωt, and at x = l it gives 0. The amplitude along the string is a/|sin kl| times a sine curve. When kl is close to pπ, sin kl → 0 and the amplitude becomes very large. That is resonance. A small push at the fork then builds up big, steady loops, and the fork end itself is almost a node.

Slightly away from resonance, sin kl is not small and the string only shivers. In a real string, damping keeps the amplitude finite, but the peak is still sharp. This is why the load or length must be fine adjusted.

D5. Transverse arrangement

The prongs vibrate at right angles to the string. Each time the prong moves up and down once, it pushes the string up and down once. So the string is driven at the fork frequency n, and the resonance condition of D3 gives

$$f_{\text{string}}=n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;n=\frac{p}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\;}$$

D6. Longitudinal arrangement

Now the prongs vibrate along the string. They do not push the string sideways. Instead the tension changes periodically

$$T(t)=T_0+T_1\cos(2\pi nt).$$

For one normal mode y = q(t) sin(pπx/l), the wave equation with this varying tension gives

$$\ddot q+\omega_p^2\left[1+h\cos(2\pi nt)\right]q=0,\qquad \omega_p=\frac{p\pi}{l}\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{m}},\quad h=\frac{T_1}{T_0}.$$

This is the Mathieu equation, which describes parametric resonance. A swing pumped by standing up and crouching works the same way. The motion grows most strongly when the tension changes twice per oscillation of the string, that is when

$$2\pi n=2\,\omega_p\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_{\text{string}}=\frac{\omega_p}{2\pi}=\frac{n}{2}.$$

A simple picture helps. When the prong moves toward the pulley the string slackens and sags, and when it moves back the string is pulled tight. The tight pull straightens the string twice per string cycle, once at the top and once at the bottom of its swing, so the string completes one vibration for every two vibrations of the fork. So

$$\frac{n}{2}=\frac{p}{2l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;n=\frac{p}{l}\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}\;}$$

D7. Consequences that can be checked

Loops at constant length. For a fixed l and arrangement, the transverse formula gives

$$p^2T=4n^2l^2m=\text{constant}.$$

Doubling the tension reduces the number of loops by a factor √2. For the same T and l, the longitudinal arrangement gives half as many loops, since the string frequency is halved.

Straight-line graph. Rearranging the two formulas

$$\sqrt T=2n\sqrt m\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)\ \ \text{(transverse)},\qquad \sqrt T=n\sqrt m\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)\ \ \text{(longitudinal)}.$$

A graph of √T against l/p is a straight line through the origin. The slope gives n = slope/(2√m) or n = slope/√m. The longitudinal line is half as steep as the transverse one.

D8. Tension and mass per unit length

The thread passes over a smooth pulley, so the tension equals the total hanging weight, including the pan

$$T=(M+m_0)\,g .$$

The mass per unit length is m = (mass of a long piece)/(its length). For example 2.10 g for 5.00 m gives m = 4.20 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m.

D9. Worked example

Transverse arrangement with M = 40.0 g, m₀ = 5.0 g, p = 5 loops and l = 1.125 m.

$$T=(0.045)(9.8)=0.441\ \text{N},\qquad v=\sqrt{\frac{0.441}{4.20\times10^{-4}}}=32.4\ \text{m/s}$$ $$n=\frac{5}{2(1.125)}\times32.4=72.0\ \text{Hz}$$

D10. Error estimate

From n = (p/2l)√(T/m), with p exact (it is counted)

$$\frac{\delta n}{n}=\frac{\delta l}{l}+\frac12\frac{\delta T}{T}+\frac12\frac{\delta m}{m}.$$

The largest error usually comes from judging exactly where the loops are sharpest, which shows up as an error in l. The error in m is reduced by weighing a long length of thread.

Procedure, with the theory behind each step

1

Find the mass per unit length of the thread

Weigh a long, measured length of the same thread (for example 5.00 m) on a sensitive balance. Then m = mass ÷ length. Example 2.10 g ÷ 5.00 m = 0.420 g/m = 4.20 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m.

Why. The wave speed v = √(T/m) depends on m. A long piece is weighed because a short piece is too light to weigh accurately.
2

Set up the transverse arrangement

Tie one end of the thread to a prong of the fork. Pass the thread over the pulley and tie the pan to the other end. Turn the fork so that its prongs vibrate at right angles to the thread. Connect the battery and key.

Why. With the prong moving across the thread, the thread is pushed sideways once in every fork cycle, so the string is driven at the fork frequency n.
3

Start the fork and adjust for sharp loops

Close the key so the fork vibrates. Add or remove small weights, or slide the fork stand, until the thread vibrates in a number of well defined, steady loops with sharp nodes.

Why. Resonance needs l = pλ/2 exactly. Changing T changes v and so λ, and changing l changes how many half waves fit. When the condition is met the amplitude builds up and the loops are clear.
4

Count loops and measure the length

Count the number of loops p. Measure the length l from the first node near the prong to the point where the thread leaves the pulley. Note the load M on the pan and the pan mass m₀.

Why. These three quantities, with m, are all that the formula n = (p/2l)√(T/m) needs. Measuring between nodes avoids error from the small motion at the prong end.
5

Repeat for different loads

Change the load to get a different number of loops and repeat steps 3 and 4. Take at least four sets.

Why. Several independent sets reduce random error. Heavier loads give faster waves, longer λ and so fewer loops for the same length. For a fixed l, T·p² should stay nearly constant.
6

Change to the longitudinal arrangement

Turn the fork through 90° so that the prongs vibrate along the length of the thread. Repeat steps 3 to 5.

Why. Now the prong only changes the tension, once per fork cycle. The string vibrates at n/2, so for the same T and l you see half as many loops, and the formula becomes n = (p/l)√(T/m).
7

Calculate n and plot a graph

Work out n for every reading and take the mean. As a graphical check, plot √T against l/p. Both arrangements give straight lines through the origin with slopes 2n√m (transverse) and n√m (longitudinal).

Why. Rearranging the two formulas gives √T = 2n√m·(l/p) and √T = n√m·(l/p). A straight line confirms the theory and the slope gives n from all readings together.

Precautions and sources of error

  • The thread should be light, uniform and nearly inextensible.
  • The thread between fork and pulley must be horizontal.
  • The pulley must be smooth and frictionless so the full weight sets the tension.
  • Include the mass of the pan in the load.
  • Adjust until the loops are sharp and steady before taking a reading.
  • Measure l between nodes, not to the prong itself.
  • The fork's prong should not touch the electromagnet. Keep the current steady.
  • Air currents and a sagging thread change the effective length.

Quick viva questions

Why are fewer loops seen in the longitudinal arrangement?

The string vibrates at half the fork frequency, so λ is twice as long for the same tension, giving half as many loops in the same length.

What are nodes and antinodes?

Nodes are points of zero displacement. Antinodes are points of maximum displacement, halfway between nodes.

What happens to the number of loops if the tension is increased?

v and λ increase, so fewer half wavelengths fit in the length and p decreases. p is proportional to 1/√T.

Why is the fork electrically maintained?

An ordinary fork dies away in seconds. The electromagnet gives it a push each cycle, so the amplitude stays constant while the string is adjusted and observed.

What kind of wave is set up on the string?

A transverse stationary wave formed by the superposition of incident and reflected waves.
