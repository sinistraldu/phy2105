Determination of the Spring Constant and Effective Mass of a Spiral Spring

Static method (Hooke's law) and dynamic method (oscillations), with a 3D virtual lab and live graphs

Aim and apparatusTheoryDerivations3D virtual lab Procedure with theoryObservations and graphsPrecautionsViva

Aim and apparatus

Aim. To determine the spring constant k of a spiral spring by the static and dynamic methods, and to find its effective mass m′.

  • Spiral (helical) spring with a pointer
  • Rigid clamp stand
  • Hanger and slotted weights (50 g each)
  • Vertical metre scale
  • Stopwatch
  • Physical balance to weigh the spring

Theory

1. Static method, Hooke's law

Within the elastic limit, the extension x of a spring is proportional to the stretching force. A load M hanging at rest stretches the spring until the spring force balances the weight

k x = M g    ⟹    k = M gx

k is the spring constant, the force needed per unit extension (N/m). A graph of load (as force Mg) against extension is a straight line whose slope is k.

The hanger and the spring's own weight already stretch the spring a little. So the reading with the hanger alone is taken as the zero, and only the extra loads and extra extensions are used.

2. Dynamic method, oscillations

Pull the load down by a small distance y below its rest position. The extra spring force is ky upward, so

M d²ydt² = − k y    ⟹    T = 2π √Mk   (massless spring)

The weight Mg does not appear because it is already balanced at the rest position. Gravity only shifts where the oscillation is centred.

3. Why the spring has an effective mass

A real spring of mass ms also moves. A small piece at distance s from the top of a spring of length ℓ moves with speed u = v·s/ℓ, where v is the speed of the load. Its kinetic energy added over the whole spring is

KE = ∫₀ℓ ½ msℓ ds (v sℓ)² = ½ (ms3) v²

So the spring behaves as if a mass m′ ≈ ms/3 were added to the load. This is the effective mass.

T = 2π √M + m′k    ⟹    T² = 4π²k M + 4π²k m′

A graph of T² against M is a straight line.

  • Its slope is 4π²/k, so k = 4π² / slope.
  • Extended backward, it cuts the M axis at M = −m′. The size of that intercept is the effective mass.

Comparing k from both methods and m′ with ms/3 checks the whole theory.

Detailed theory and derivations

D1. Hooke's law and elastic energy

Within the elastic limit the restoring force of a spring is proportional to its extension x and opposite to it

$$F=-kx .$$

The work done in stretching it slowly from 0 to x is stored as elastic potential energy

$$U=\int_0^x kx'\,dx'=\tfrac12kx^2 .$$

D2. Static equilibrium

A load M hangs at rest on a light spring. The spring force balances the weight

$$kx_0=Mg\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;k=\frac{Mg}{x_0}\;}$$

Adding a load ΔM increases the extension by Δx = ΔM g/k. So a graph of added load (as force ΔMg) against extra extension Δx is a straight line through the origin with slope k. Taking the reading with the hanger alone as zero means the hanger, pointer and the spring's own weight drop out.

D3. Stretch of the spring under its own weight

A uniform spring of mass ms and unstretched length ℓ₀ hangs vertically. A short piece at distance s from the top, of unstretched length ds, carries the weight of the spring below it, msg(ℓ₀ − s)/ℓ₀. A piece of length ds has spring constant kℓ₀/ds (a shorter spring is stiffer). Its stretch is therefore

$$d(\Delta)=\frac{m_sg(\ell_0-s)/\ell_0}{k\ell_0/ds}=\frac{m_sg}{k\ell_0^2}(\ell_0-s)\,ds .$$ $$\Delta=\frac{m_sg}{k\ell_0^2}\int_0^{\ell_0}(\ell_0-s)\,ds=\frac{m_sg}{2k}.$$

So the spring stretches as though half of its weight hung at the bottom. This shifts the zero reading but not the slope of the static graph.

D4. Equation of motion (light spring)

Measure y downward from the equilibrium position, so the total extension is x₀ + y. Newton's second law for the load is

$$M\ddot y=Mg-k(x_0+y)=Mg-kx_0-ky=-ky ,$$

because kx₀ = Mg. Gravity cancels exactly. It only fixes the centre of the motion. So

$$\ddot y+\omega^2y=0,\qquad \omega^2=\frac{k}{M}.$$

D5. Solving it with the exponential method

Try \(y=e^{\lambda t}\). The characteristic equation is λ² + ω² = 0, so λ = ±iω and

$$y(t)=C_1e^{i\omega t}+C_2e^{-i\omega t}=A\cos(\omega t+\varphi),$$

using \(C_2=C_1^{*}\) for a real solution and Euler's formula. A and φ are fixed by how far the load is pulled and when it is released. If it is pulled down by y₀ and released from rest, then y = y₀ cos ωt. The period is

$$\boxed{\;T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{M}{k}}\;}\qquad\text{(massless spring)}$$

T does not depend on the amplitude or on g.

D6. Effective mass of the spring

The top of the spring is fixed and the bottom moves with the load at speed v = ẏ. For a uniformly stretched spring, a coil at distance s from the top (total length ℓ) moves with speed

$$u(s)=\frac{s}{\ell}\,v .$$

The mass of a piece ds is (ms/ℓ) ds. Its kinetic energy added over the whole spring is

$$K_{\text{spring}}=\int_0^{\ell}\frac12\,\frac{m_s}{\ell}\,ds\left(\frac{s\,v}{\ell}\right)^2=\frac{m_sv^2}{2\ell^3}\int_0^{\ell}s^2\,ds=\frac12\left(\frac{m_s}{3}\right)v^2 .$$

The total energy of the oscillating system (load and spring, about equilibrium) is constant

$$E=\frac12\left(M+\frac{m_s}{3}\right)\dot y^2+\frac12ky^2=\text{constant}.$$

Differentiating with respect to time and dividing by ẏ

$$\left(M+\frac{m_s}{3}\right)\ddot y+ky=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^2=\frac{k}{M+m'},\qquad \boxed{\;m'=\frac{m_s}{3}\;}$$ $$\boxed{\;T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{M+m'}{k}}\;}$$

The spring adds one third of its own mass to the load. In a real spring the coils near the ends may not stretch evenly, so the measured m′ is often a little different from ms/3.

D7. Straight-line form and how the graph gives k and m′

Squaring the period

$$T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{k}\,M+\frac{4\pi^2}{k}\,m' .$$

This has the form y = (slope)x + c, with y = T² and x = M. So

$$\text{slope}=\frac{4\pi^2}{k}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{\;k=\frac{4\pi^2}{\text{slope}}\;},\qquad T^2=0\ \text{at}\ M=-m' .$$

The line extended back cuts the M axis at −m′, so the size of that intercept is the effective mass. Equivalently m′ = c/slope, where c is the intercept on the T² axis.

D8. Damping

Air resistance adds a small force proportional to velocity, −bẏ. The equation becomes

$$\ddot y+2\gamma\dot y+\omega_0^2y=0,\qquad \gamma=\frac{b}{2(M+m')},\quad \omega_0^2=\frac{k}{M+m'}.$$

With \(y=e^{\lambda t}\), λ² + 2γλ + ω₀² = 0, so λ = −γ ± i√(ω₀² − γ²). For light damping (γ ≪ ω₀)

$$y=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t+\varphi),\qquad \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}\approx\omega_0\left(1-\frac{\gamma^2}{2\omega_0^2}\right).$$

The amplitude dies away slowly, but the period changes only by a term of order (γ/ω₀)², which is negligible here. So timing a slowly decaying oscillation still gives the correct T.

D9. Springs in series and parallel

For two springs in series, the same force acts in each and the extensions add, x = F/k₁ + F/k₂, so

$$\frac{1}{k_{\text{series}}}=\frac1{k_1}+\frac1{k_2}.$$

For two springs side by side (parallel), the extensions are equal and the forces add, so kparallel = k₁ + k₂. Cutting a spring into two equal halves gives each half spring constant 2k.

D10. Worked example

Static readings rise by 2.45 cm for every 50 g added

$$k=\frac{(0.050)(9.8)}{0.0245}=20.0\ \text{N/m}.$$

Dynamic readings give a T² against M slope of 1.98 s²/kg and an intercept on the M axis of −17.7 g

$$k=\frac{4\pi^2}{1.98}=19.9\ \text{N/m},\qquad m'=17.7\ \text{g}\ \ (\text{compare } m_s/3=54/3=18.0\ \text{g}).$$

D11. Error estimate

For the static method k = Mg/x, so δk/k = δM/M + δx/x. The extension error is about the scale least count (0.1 cm) on each reading. For the dynamic method

$$\frac{\delta k}{k}=\frac{\delta M}{M}+2\,\frac{\delta T}{T},\qquad \delta T=\frac{\delta t}{N}.$$

Because T is short (about 0.5 s), a reaction error of 0.1 s over 20 oscillations already gives 2δT/T ≈ 2%. The intercept m′ is very sensitive to this. Timing more oscillations and repeating each timing improves it.

Procedure, with the theory behind each step

1

Weigh the spring

Find the mass ms of the spring on a balance before hanging it.

Why. The effective mass found from the graph will be compared with ms/3.
2

Suspend the spring and set the scale

Hang the spring from a rigid clamp. Attach the hanger and pointer at its lower end. Fix the metre scale vertically so the pointer moves just in front of it without touching. Note the reading with the hanger only.

Why. A rigid support ensures that only the spring stretches. The hanger and the spring's own weight produce an initial extension, so this reading is the zero for measuring extra extensions.
3

Load the spring step by step

Add 50 g at a time. After each addition, wait until the pointer is at rest and note the reading. Go up to about 300 g.

Why. At rest the spring force equals the weight, kx = Mg. Equal load steps should give equal extension steps if Hooke's law holds.
4

Unload step by step

Remove the weights one at a time and note the reading again for each load. Take the mean of loading and unloading readings.

Why. If the readings return to the same values, the spring was not stretched beyond its elastic limit. Averaging cancels small lag and friction effects.
5

Plot load against extension (static k)

Plot Mg (N) against extension x (m). Draw the best straight line. Its slope is k.

Why. From kx = Mg the graph should be a straight line through the origin with slope k. Using the slope uses every reading at once.
6

Set the load oscillating

With a load M on the hanger, pull it down by 2 to 3 cm and release. It should move straight up and down without swinging sideways.

Why. A small pull keeps the spring in its elastic range so the restoring force stays ky, which gives simple harmonic motion. Sideways swinging adds a pendulum motion with a different period.
7

Time 20 oscillations for each load

Start the stopwatch as the pointer passes the rest position and count 20 oscillations. Repeat and take the mean. T = t/20. Do this for every load.

Why. Timing many oscillations spreads the reaction error. Starting at the rest position is best because the motion is fastest there.
8

Plot T² against M (dynamic k and m′)

Draw the best straight line and extend it back until it meets the M axis. Then k = 4π²/slope and m′ is the size of the negative intercept on the M axis.

Why. T² = (4π²/k)(M + m′). T² becomes zero when M = −m′, which is where the line crosses the M axis.
9

Compare

Compare k from the two methods. Compare m′ with ms/3.

Why. Agreement confirms Hooke's law, SHM and the energy argument for the effective mass together.

Precautions and sources of error

  • The support must be rigid and the scale vertical.
  • Do not load beyond the elastic limit. Readings on unloading should return to the loading values.
  • Take readings only when the pointer is at rest, with the eye level with the pointer to avoid parallax.
  • Keep the amplitude small and the motion vertical.
  • Time at least 20 oscillations, counting from zero.
  • The effective mass ms/3 assumes a uniform spring that stretches evenly.

Quick viva questions

What is the spring constant?

The force required to produce unit extension in the spring, k = F/x, measured in N/m.

Why does the period not depend on g?

Gravity only changes the rest position. The restoring force about that position is ky, which does not involve g.

What is effective mass and why is it ms/3?

It is the part of the spring's mass that effectively moves with the load. Since the speed of the coils grows linearly from top to bottom, the total kinetic energy equals that of mass ms/3 moving with the load.

If a spring is cut in half, what happens to k?

It doubles. Each half stretches half as much under the same force.

What happens to T if the load is quadrupled?

For a light spring T doubles, since T ∝ √M.
