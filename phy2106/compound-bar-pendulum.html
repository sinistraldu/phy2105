Determination of g with a Compound Bar Pendulum

Theory, 3D illustration, a working virtual lab with stopwatch and graph, and the reasoning behind every step

Aim and apparatus

Aim. To determine the acceleration due to gravity g and the radius of gyration k of a bar about its centre of gravity, using the bar as a compound (physical) pendulum.

  • Compound bar pendulum (a uniform metal bar about 1 m long with a row of equally spaced holes)
  • Knife edge fixed on a rigid wall bracket
  • Spirit level
  • Stopwatch
  • Metre scale
  • Graph paper

Theory

1. A rigid body swinging about a fixed axis

A compound pendulum is any rigid body that swings about a horizontal axis that does not pass through its centre of gravity G. Let the axis (the knife edge S) be a distance l from G.

When the bar is turned through a small angle θ, its weight mg acts at G and produces a restoring torque

τ = − m g l sin θ ≈ − m g l θ

If I is the moment of inertia about the knife edge, Newton's law for rotation gives

I d²θdt² = − m g l θ

This is simple harmonic motion with ω² = mgl/I, so

T = 2π √Im g l

2. Parallel axis theorem and the equivalent length

If the moment of inertia about G is IG = mk², where k is the radius of gyration, then by the parallel axis theorem I = m(k² + l²). Putting this in the period gives

T = 2π √k² + l²g l = 2π √Lg    with   L = l + k²l

So the bar swings exactly like a simple pendulum of length L, the equivalent length. The virtual lab can draw this simple pendulum next to the bar so you can see that both keep perfect step.

3. Two points with the same period

Rearranging L = l + k²/l gives a quadratic in l

l² − L l + k² = 0

For one value of T (and so of L) there are two distances l₁ and l₂ from G on each side, with

l₁ + l₂ = L     l₁ l₂ = k²

The point at distance l₂ on the other side of G from S is called the centre of oscillation. Suspension and oscillation points can be swapped without changing T.

A graph of T against hole position gives two symmetric curves. A horizontal line at period T cuts them at A, B, C and D. Then AC = BD = L and AD·BC/4 = k², so

g = 4π² LT²    L = AC + BD2    k = √AD2·BC2

The period is smallest when l = k, where Tmin = 2π√(2k/g). For a uniform bar of length ℓ and width b, theory gives k² = (ℓ² + b²)/12.

Detailed theory and derivations

D1. Equation of motion of a compound pendulum

A rigid body of mass m turns about a fixed horizontal axis S. Its centre of gravity G is a distance l from S. When the line SG makes an angle θ with the vertical, the weight mg acting at G has a perpendicular distance l sin θ from the axis. The torque about S is

$$\tau=-mgl\sin\theta .$$

The minus sign shows that the torque always acts to reduce θ. Newton's second law for rotation about a fixed axis is τ = Iα, where I is the moment of inertia about S and α = d²θ/dt². So

$$I\,\ddot\theta=-mgl\sin\theta .$$

For small swings, sin θ ≈ θ (the error is below 0.1% for θ < 4.5°), and

$$\ddot\theta+\omega^2\theta=0,\qquad \omega^2=\frac{mgl}{I}.$$

D2. Solving the equation (exponential method)

Try \(\theta=e^{\lambda t}\). Then \(\ddot\theta=\lambda^2e^{\lambda t}\), and substituting gives the characteristic equation

$$\lambda^2+\omega^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda=\pm i\omega .$$

The general solution is a combination of both roots

$$\theta(t)=C_1e^{i\omega t}+C_2e^{-i\omega t}.$$

Because θ is real, C₂ is the complex conjugate of C₁. Writing \(C_1=\tfrac{\theta_0}{2}e^{i\varphi}\) and using Euler's formula \(e^{ix}=\cos x+i\sin x\)

$$\theta(t)=\theta_0\cos(\omega t+\varphi).$$

This is simple harmonic motion with period

$$\boxed{\;T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgl}}\;}$$

D3. Parallel axis theorem

Take G as origin and let the axis S be at position vector d (with |d| = l) perpendicular to the axis direction. A mass element dm at position r from G is at r − d from S. Its contribution to I is dm|r − d|². So

$$I=\int|\mathbf r-\mathbf d|^2dm=\int r^2dm-2\,\mathbf d\cdot\!\int\mathbf r\,dm+d^2\!\int dm .$$

The first term is IG. The middle term is zero because ∫r dm = 0 when G is the origin (that is the definition of the centre of mass). The last term is ml². Therefore

$$\boxed{\;I=I_G+ml^2=m(k^2+l^2)\;}$$

where k is the radius of gyration about G, defined by IG = mk².

D4. Moment of inertia of the bar about G

Treat the bar as a thin rectangular plate of length ℓ, width b and mass m. The axis passes through G perpendicular to the plate. For a thin rod of length ℓ about its centre

$$I_x=\int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}\frac{m}{\ell}\,x^2\,dx=\frac{m}{\ell}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}=\frac{m\ell^2}{12}.$$

By the same integral across the width, the moment about the other in-plane axis is mb²/12. The perpendicular axis theorem for a plane lamina adds the two

$$I_G=\frac{m(\ell^2+b^2)}{12}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;k=\sqrt{\frac{\ell^2+b^2}{12}}\;}$$

For ℓ = 100 cm and b = 4 cm this gives k = 28.9 cm.

D5. Equivalent length

Put I = m(k² + l²) into the period from D2

$$T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2+l^2}{gl}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}},\qquad \boxed{\;L=l+\frac{k^2}{l}\;}$$

A simple pendulum has T = 2π√(L/g), so the compound pendulum behaves exactly like a simple pendulum of length L. This L is the length of the equivalent simple pendulum. It is always longer than l, because k²/l is positive.

D6. Two suspension points with the same period

For a given T, L is fixed and L = l + k²/l becomes a quadratic in l

$$l^2-Ll+k^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad l=\frac{L\pm\sqrt{L^2-4k^2}}{2}.$$

The two roots l₁ and l₂ are real when L ≥ 2k. From the relations between roots and coefficients of a quadratic

$$\boxed{\;l_1+l_2=L,\qquad l_1l_2=k^2\;}$$

So on each side of G there are two holes with the same period. Since the bar is symmetric, there are four such holes in total, which are the points A, B, C and D on the graph.

D7. Centres of suspension and oscillation are interchangeable

Hang the bar from S at distance l₁ from G. Mark the point O on the line SG, beyond G, at distance l₂ = k²/l₁ from G. Then SO = l₁ + l₂ = L. Now hang the bar from O instead. Its distance from G is l₂, and its equivalent length is

$$L'=l_2+\frac{k^2}{l_2}=\frac{k^2}{l_1}+l_1=L .$$

The period is unchanged. O is called the centre of oscillation, and S and O can be swapped. This is the principle of Kater's reversible pendulum.

D8. Reading L and k from the graph

With distances measured from end A, G is at the middle. A horizontal line at period T cuts the curves at four points. Their distances from G are

$$A:\ -l_1,\qquad B:\ -l_2,\qquad C:\ +l_2,\qquad D:\ +l_1 .$$

So

$$AC=l_1+l_2=L,\quad BD=l_2+l_1=L,\quad AD=2l_1,\quad BC=2l_2 .$$ $$\boxed{\;g=\frac{4\pi^2L}{T^2},\qquad L=\frac{AC+BD}{2},\qquad k=\sqrt{\frac{AD}{2}\cdot\frac{BC}{2}}\;}$$

D9. Minimum period

T is smallest when L(l) = l + k²/l is smallest. Differentiate and set the derivative to zero

$$\frac{dL}{dl}=1-\frac{k^2}{l^2}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad l=k,\qquad L_{\min}=2k,\qquad \boxed{\;T_{\min}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2k}{g}}\;}$$

The second derivative 2k²/l³ is positive, so this is a minimum. At this point l₁ = l₂ = k and the horizontal line just touches the curve. Near the minimum the curve is flat, so T hardly changes if the hole position has a small error. This makes the minimum region a good place to work.

As l → 0, L → ∞ and T → ∞, because the weight then passes through the axis and gives no torque. As l → ∞, L → l and the bar behaves like a simple pendulum.

D10. A straight-line method from the same data

Square the period and multiply by l

$$T^2l=\frac{4\pi^2}{g}\,l^2+\frac{4\pi^2k^2}{g}.$$

A graph of T²l against l² is a straight line with slope 4π²/g and intercept 4π²k²/g. So

$$g=\frac{4\pi^2}{\text{slope}},\qquad k^2=\frac{\text{intercept}}{\text{slope}} .$$

The results box in the observations section also calculates g and k this way, using all readings at once.

D11. Correction for finite amplitude

Without the small angle approximation, energy conservation gives ½Iθ̇² = mgl(cos θ − cos θ₀). Solving for dt and integrating over a quarter cycle

$$T=4\sqrt{\frac{I}{2mgl}}\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0}} =T_0\left(1+\frac{\theta_0^2}{16}+\frac{11\,\theta_0^4}{3072}+\cdots\right),$$

with T₀ the small amplitude period and θ₀ in radians. At θ₀ = 5° the correction is only 0.05%, but at 20° it is 0.8%. This is why the amplitude is kept small.

D12. Error estimate

From g = 4π²L/T² the maximum fractional error is

$$\frac{\delta g}{g}=\frac{\delta L}{L}+2\,\frac{\delta T}{T}.$$

If the time t for N oscillations has an error δt (reaction time about 0.2 s), then T = t/N and δT = δt/N. For N = 20 and T ≈ 1.6 s, 2δT/T ≈ 2(0.01)/1.6 ≈ 1.2%. Reading L from the graph to ±0.5 cm out of 62 cm adds about 0.8%. Timing more oscillations reduces the first term.

Procedure, with the theory behind each step

1

Locate the centre of gravity G

Balance the bar horizontally on a knife edge (or a sharp wedge). Mark the balance point G. On a uniform bar it is the midpoint.

Why. Every distance l in the formula is measured from G, because the parallel axis theorem I = IG + ml² refers to the axis through G.
2

Level the knife edge and hang the bar

Check with a spirit level that the knife edge is horizontal. Hang the bar from the hole nearest end A, with the knife edge resting at the top of the hole. The bar should hang vertically, close to the wall but not touching it.

Why. The theory assumes rotation about a fixed horizontal axis. A tilted or loose edge makes the bar wobble or slide, adding friction and changing the period.
3

Set it swinging through a small angle

Pull the lower end aside by about 3° to 5° and release it gently. Make sure it swings in one plane, parallel to the wall, with no twisting.

Why. The equation of motion is SHM only when sin θ ≈ θ. At larger angles T grows by a factor of about (1 + θ₀²/16), so a large swing gives a period that is too long.
4

Time 20 oscillations

Start the stopwatch as the bar passes the mean (vertical) position and count 20 complete oscillations. Repeat the timing and take the mean. Then T = t/20.

Why. Your reaction time error (about 0.2 s) is spread over 20 swings, so the error in T falls to about 0.01 s. Starting at the mean position is best because the bar moves fastest there and the instant is easy to judge.
5

Measure the hole position

Measure the distance of the knife edge (top of the hole) from end A with the metre scale. The distance from G is l = 50 cm minus this, for a 1 m bar.

Why. The graph needs T for each position. Measuring from one fixed end for all holes keeps the x axis consistent for both halves.
6

Repeat for all holes on side A

Move the bar to each hole in turn up to the hole nearest G and repeat steps 3 to 5.

Why. As l falls from the end toward G, T first decreases, reaches a minimum at l = k, and then rises steeply (T → ∞ as l → 0 because the torque vanishes). This gives one full branch of the curve.
7

Invert the bar and repeat for side B

Turn the bar upside down and suspend it from the holes on the other half, again measuring all distances from end A.

Why. The bar is symmetric about G, so the second branch is a mirror image. Having both branches lets a horizontal line cut the graph at four points.
8

Plot T against distance from end A and find L

Draw smooth curves through the points. Draw a horizontal line at a chosen period T cutting the curves at A, B, C, D. Measure AC and BD and take L = (AC + BD)/2. Then g = 4π²L/T². Draw two or three such lines and average.

Why. A and C are a pair with l₁ and l₂ on opposite sides of G, so AC = l₁ + l₂ = L. The same holds for B and D.
9

Find k and compare with theory

From the same line, k = √(AD/2 × BC/2). Compare with k = √((ℓ² + b²)/12) for the bar.

Why. AD/2 = l₁ and BC/2 = l₂, and the quadratic gives l₁ l₂ = k². The lowest point of each curve should also be at a distance k from G.

Precautions and sources of error

  • The knife edge must be horizontal and sharp, and the bar must rest on it at the top of the hole.
  • Amplitude must stay small, below about 5°.
  • The bar should swing in a vertical plane parallel to the wall, without twisting or touching the wall.
  • Count oscillations carefully, starting the count from zero.
  • Measure all distances from the same end and to the knife edge.
  • Draw smooth curves, not straight joins, through the points.
  • Air resistance and friction at the knife edge slightly increase T.
  • The holes reduce the mass of the bar slightly, so k from theory is approximate.

Quick viva questions

What is a compound pendulum?

A rigid body of any shape free to swing about a horizontal axis that does not pass through its centre of gravity.

What is the equivalent length of a simple pendulum?

L = l + k²/l. A simple pendulum of this length has the same period as the compound pendulum.

What are centres of suspension and oscillation, and why are they interchangeable?

They are two points on opposite sides of G at distances l₁ and l₂ with l₁ l₂ = k². Since the period depends on l + k²/l, which is the same for l₁ and l₂, swapping them leaves T unchanged.

Why is the period infinite when the axis passes through G?

With l = 0 the weight acts through the axis and gives no restoring torque, so there is no oscillation.

At what point is the period minimum?

When l = k. Then L = 2k and Tmin = 2π√(2k/g).

Why is the compound pendulum better than a simple pendulum?

Its length L is found from the graph instead of measuring to an uncertain centre of a bob. The mass is distributed and real, there is no stretching string, and it does not twist easily.
