Class 16

The first law, path-dependent work and the four thermodynamic processes

By the end you can: apply $\Delta U = Q - W$; explain why $Q$ and $W$ depend on the path but $\Delta U$ does not; derive the work in isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric and isobaric processes; derive $PV^\gamma$ = constant and $C_P - C_V = R$.

16.1The first law

Key results
$$\Delta U = Q - W, \qquad W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV, \qquad dU = nC_V\,dT\ \text{(ideal gas)}$$

$Q$ = heat added to the gas, $W$ = work done by the gas. $U$ depends only on the state (a state function); $W$ is the area under the path on a $P$–$V$ diagram, so different paths between the same two states do different work and need different heat.

16.2The four processes

Derivations

Isochoric ($V$ constant): $W = 0$, so $Q = \Delta U = nC_V\Delta T$.

Isobaric ($P$ constant): $W = P(V_2 - V_1) = nR\Delta T$, $Q = nC_P\Delta T$. Since $Q = \Delta U + W$: $nC_P\Delta T = nC_V\Delta T + nR\Delta T$, giving Mayer’s relation $C_P - C_V = R$.

Isothermal ($T$ constant): $\Delta U = 0$, $Q = W = \displaystyle\int\frac{nRT}{V}dV = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}$.

Adiabatic ($Q = 0$): $dU = -P\,dV \Rightarrow nC_VdT = -P\,dV$. From $PV = nRT$: $nR\,dT = P\,dV + V\,dP$. Eliminate $dT$: $C_V(P\,dV + V\,dP) = -R\,P\,dV$, i.e. $C_PP\,dV + C_VV\,dP = 0$, so $\gamma\dfrac{dV}{V} + \dfrac{dP}{P} = 0$:

$$PV^\gamma = \text{const}, \qquad TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}, \qquad W_{\text{adiabatic}} = \frac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{\gamma - 1} = nC_V(T_1 - T_2)$$
Simulation

P–V process explorer

Try: choose a process, then change the final volume (or temperature for isochoric) and $\gamma$. Read $Q$, $W$ and $\Delta U$ and check that $Q = \Delta U + W$.

What to notice: the shaded area is the work. An adiabat is steeper than an isotherm because the gas cools as it expands. Compression gives negative $W$ (work is done on the gas).

3D simulation

Gas in a cylinder: watch each process happen

Try: choose a process and press Run. Watch the piston, the heat flow (orange arrows in or out) and the molecules’ speed (colour = temperature).

What to notice: isothermal expansion needs heat flowing in to keep $T$ fixed; adiabatic expansion has no heat flow, so the gas cools as it does work; isochoric heating raises $T$ and $P$ without moving the piston.

Practice problems

  1. 1 mol of ideal gas at 300 K expands isothermally to twice its volume. Find $W$, $Q$ and $\Delta U$. easy

    $W = Q = RT\ln2 = 1729$ J; $\Delta U = 0$.

  2. Air ($\gamma = 1.4$) at 300 K is compressed adiabatically to 1/10 of its volume. Find the final temperature. easy

    $T_2 = T_1(V_1/V_2)^{\gamma-1} = 300\times10^{0.4} \approx 754$ K. (This is how a diesel engine ignites its fuel.)

  3. 1 mol of a diatomic gas is heated at constant pressure from 300 K to 400 K. Find $Q$, $W$ and $\Delta U$. medium

    $Q = \tfrac72R\Delta T = 2910$ J; $W = R\Delta T = 831$ J; $\Delta U = 2079$ J.

  4. Why is $\oint dU = 0$ around a cycle but $\oint dW \ne 0$? easy

    $U$ is a state function: returning to the same state returns the same $U$. Work is the enclosed area on the $P$–$V$ diagram, which is not zero for a cycle.

Class 17

Heat engines, refrigerators and the Carnot, Otto and Diesel cycles

By the end you can: define efficiency and COP; derive the Carnot efficiency; derive the Otto efficiency; state the Diesel efficiency; compare the cycles.

17.1Engines and refrigerators

Key results
$$\eta = \frac{W}{Q_H} = 1 - \frac{Q_C}{Q_H}, \qquad \text{COP}_{\text{fridge}} = \frac{Q_C}{W} = \frac{Q_C}{Q_H - Q_C}, \qquad \text{COP}_{\text{heat pump}} = \frac{Q_H}{W}$$

Heat flows naturally from hot to cold. An engine takes $Q_H$ from a hot reservoir, turns part into work $W$ and must reject $Q_C$ to a cold reservoir. A refrigerator uses work to pump heat from cold to hot.

17.2The Carnot cycle

Derivation

1→2Isothermal expansion at $T_H$: $Q_H = nRT_H\ln(V_2/V_1)$.

2→3Adiabatic expansion: $T_HV_2^{\gamma-1} = T_CV_3^{\gamma-1}$.

3→4Isothermal compression at $T_C$: $Q_C = nRT_C\ln(V_3/V_4)$.

4→1Adiabatic compression: $T_CV_4^{\gamma-1} = T_HV_1^{\gamma-1}$.

CombineDividing the two adiabatic relations gives $V_2/V_1 = V_3/V_4$, so the logarithms cancel:

$$\frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \eta_{\text{Carnot}} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \text{COP}_{\text{Carnot}} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C}$$

No engine working between the same two temperatures can beat this (Carnot’s theorem).

17.3Otto and Diesel cycles

Otto cycle (petrol engine)

Two adiabats and two isochores. Heat in at constant volume ($Q_H = nC_V(T_3 - T_2)$), heat out at constant volume ($Q_C = nC_V(T_4 - T_1)$). Using $TV^{\gamma-1}$ = const on both adiabats with compression ratio $r = V_1/V_2$: $T_3/T_4 = T_2/T_1 = r^{\gamma-1}$, so

$$\eta_{\text{Otto}} = 1 - \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}$$
Diesel cycle

Heat is added at constant pressure while the piston moves out (cut-off ratio $\rho = V_3/V_2$); air alone is compressed to a high ratio $r$ so the fuel self-ignites:

$$\eta_{\text{Diesel}} = 1 - \frac{1}{\gamma\,r^{\gamma-1}}\,\frac{\rho^\gamma - 1}{\rho - 1}$$

Differences: Carnot uses isotherms (ideal, impractical); Otto adds heat at constant volume (spark ignition, $r\approx 8$–12, limited by knocking); Diesel adds heat at constant pressure (compression ignition, $r\approx 15$–22, so it is more efficient in practice even though for the same $r$ Otto would win).

Simulation

Engine cycles on the P–V diagram

Try: pick Carnot, Otto or Diesel. Change $T_H$ and $T_C$ (Carnot), the compression ratio $r$, the cut-off ratio (Diesel) and $\gamma$. Watch the state point go round.

What to notice: the enclosed area is the net work per cycle. Higher compression ratios (and larger $\gamma$) raise the efficiency; for Carnot only the two temperatures matter.

Simulation

Refrigerator and heat pump

Try: change the inside (cold) and outside (hot) temperatures and the work input.

What to notice: the closer the two temperatures, the more heat each joule of work can move (larger COP). Heat dumped outside is always $Q_H = Q_C + W$.

Practice problems

  1. A Carnot engine works between 500 K and 300 K and takes in 1000 J per cycle. Find $\eta$, $W$ and $Q_C$. easy

    $\eta = 0.40$; $W = 400$ J; $Q_C = 600$ J.

  2. A Carnot refrigerator keeps 5 °C in a 30 °C room. Find its COP. easy

    COP $= 278.15/25 \approx 11.1$.

  3. Find the Otto efficiency for $r = 8$, $\gamma = 1.4$. easy

    $1 - 8^{-0.4} \approx 56.5\%$.

  4. Find the Diesel efficiency for $r = 18$, $\rho = 2$, $\gamma = 1.4$. medium

    $1 - \dfrac{1}{1.4\times18^{0.4}}\dfrac{2^{1.4}-1}{1} \approx 63.2\%$.

Class 18

Entropy, T–S diagrams and the thermodynamic potentials

By the end you can: define and compute entropy changes; show that $\Delta S = 0$ for reversible and $\Delta S > 0$ for irreversible processes; read a T–S diagram; define $U$, $H$, $A$ and $G$ and their differentials.

18.1Entropy

Key results
$$dS = \frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}, \qquad \Delta S = \int\frac{dQ_{\text{rev}}}{T}, \qquad \Delta S_{\text{universe}} \ge 0$$

Entropy measures how spread out (how unavailable for work) energy is. Physically, it counts the number of microscopic arrangements: $S = k\ln\Omega$. In any real (irreversible) process the total entropy grows; only in an ideal reversible process does it stay constant. The energy made unavailable for work is $T_0\,\Delta S_{\text{universe}}$.

Entropy changes

Isothermal ideal gas: $\Delta S = Q/T = nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$.

Heating at constant pressure: $\Delta S = \displaystyle\int\frac{mc\,dT}{T} = mc\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}$.

Phase change: $\Delta S = mL/T$.

General ideal gas: $\Delta S = nC_V\ln\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} + nR\ln\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$.

Carnot cycle: $\Delta S = Q_H/T_H - Q_C/T_C = 0$ (reversible), which is the same statement as $Q_C/Q_H = T_C/T_H$.

Worked example: entropy increases in an irreversible process

Mix 1 kg of water at 80 °C with 1 kg at 20 °C; final 50 °C. $\Delta S = mc\left[\ln\frac{323}{353} + \ln\frac{323}{293}\right] = 4186(-0.0888 + 0.0975) \approx +36$ J/K > 0. The hot water loses less entropy than the cold water gains.

Simulation

Two bodies reach the same temperature

Try: set the two starting temperatures and masses, then press Let heat flow. Watch both entropy changes.

What to notice: heat leaves the hot body at high $T$ (small $dQ/T$) and enters the cold body at low $T$ (large $dQ/T$), so the total entropy always rises. It is zero only if the bodies start at the same temperature.

3D simulation

Free expansion: entropy as disorder

Try: press Remove the partition and watch the gas fill the box. Press Reset and try a different number of molecules.

What to notice: no work is done and no heat flows, yet the process is irreversible: the molecules never all go back to one side by themselves. $\Delta S = nR\ln2$; the number of arrangements has grown by $2^N$.

18.2The T–S diagram

Plotting $T$ against $S$, a reversible adiabat is a vertical line (isentropic) and an isotherm is horizontal, so the Carnot cycle is a rectangle. The area under a path is the heat $\int T\,dS$; the area inside a cycle is the net work.

18.3Thermodynamic potentials

Definitions and differentials
$$\begin{aligned} U &:& dU &= T\,dS - P\,dV \\ H = U + PV &:& dH &= T\,dS + V\,dP \\ A = U - TS &:& dA &= -S\,dT - P\,dV \\ G = H - TS &:& dG &= -S\,dT + V\,dP \end{aligned}$$

$H$ (enthalpy) is the heat absorbed at constant pressure; $A$ (Helmholtz free energy) is the maximum work at constant $T$; $G$ (Gibbs free energy) is constant during a phase change at fixed $T$ and $P$.

Practice problems

  1. Find the entropy change when 1 kg of ice melts at 0 °C ($L = 334$ kJ/kg). easy

    $\Delta S = mL/T = 334000/273.15 \approx 1223$ J/K.

  2. 1 mol of gas expands freely into a vacuum, doubling its volume. Find $\Delta S$. easy

    $\Delta S = R\ln2 \approx 5.76$ J/K (use a reversible isothermal path between the same states).

  3. Show that the area of a Carnot rectangle on a T–S diagram equals the work done. medium

    Area $= (T_H - T_C)\Delta S$. $Q_H = T_H\Delta S$, $Q_C = T_C\Delta S$, so $W = Q_H - Q_C = (T_H-T_C)\Delta S$.

Class 19

Maxwell’s thermodynamic relations and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation

By the end you can: derive the four Maxwell relations; derive the Clausius–Clapeyron equation; predict how pressure changes boiling and melting points.

19.1Maxwell’s relations

Derivation

IdeaIf $dz = M\,dx + N\,dy$ is an exact differential, then $\left(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right)_x = \left(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}\right)_y$ (mixed partial derivatives are equal).

Applyto each potential:

$$\begin{aligned} dU = T\,dS - P\,dV &\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V \\ dH = T\,dS + V\,dP &\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P \\ dA = -S\,dT - P\,dV &\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V \\ dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP &\Rightarrow \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P \end{aligned}$$

They swap hard-to-measure entropy derivatives for easy $P$, $V$, $T$ measurements.

19.2The Clausius–Clapeyron equation

Derivation

Step 1During a phase change at fixed $T$ the pressure stays at the vapour pressure $P(T)$, so $(\partial P/\partial T)_V = dP/dT$.

Step 2The third Maxwell relation: $\left(\dfrac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \dfrac{dP}{dT}$. Converting a mass $m$ from phase 1 to phase 2: $\Delta S = mL/T$ and $\Delta V = m(v_2 - v_1)$.

Step 3$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T(v_2 - v_1)}$$

Vapour formWith $v_{\text{gas}}\gg v_{\text{liquid}}$ and $v_{\text{gas}} = RT/(MP)$: $\ln\dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = -\dfrac{L_m}{R}\left(\dfrac1{T_2}-\dfrac1{T_1}\right)$.

Effect of pressure on boiling and melting

Boiling: $v_{\text{vapour}} > v_{\text{liquid}}$, so $dP/dT > 0$: higher pressure raises the boiling point (pressure cooker: about 121 °C at 2 atm), lower pressure lowers it (about 71 °C on Everest).

Melting of ice: ice is less dense than water ($v_{\text{water}} < v_{\text{ice}}$), so $dT/dP < 0$: pressure lowers the melting point (about $-0.0075$ K per atm), which is why ice under a skate blade or a glacier can melt.

Simulation

Boiling point of water vs pressure

Try: change the pressure from a mountain top to a pressure cooker. Change the latent heat to see how a different liquid would behave.

What to notice: the boiling curve rises steeply: the slope is $dP/dT = L/T\Delta v$. Doubling the pressure raises water’s boiling point by about 20 °C.

3D simulation

The P–V–T surface of an ideal gas

Try: move the state point by changing $V$ and $T$. Switch on the isotherm, isobar and adiabat through the point. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: every equilibrium state lies on the surface $PV = nRT$. Processes are paths on it: isotherms are hyperbolas, isobars straight lines, and the adiabat cuts across the isotherms, losing temperature as volume grows.

Practice problems

  1. Estimate the boiling point of water in a pressure cooker at 2 atm ($L_m = 40.7$ kJ/mol). medium

    $1/T_2 = 1/373.15 - R\ln2/L_m \Rightarrow T_2 \approx 394$ K $\approx 121$ °C.

  2. Find $dT/dP$ for melting ice ($v_{\text{water}} = 1.000\times10^{-3}$, $v_{\text{ice}} = 1.091\times10^{-3}$ m³/kg, $L = 334$ kJ/kg). medium

    $dT/dP = T\Delta v/L = 273.15\times(-9.1\times10^{-5})/3.34\times10^5 \approx -7.4\times10^{-8}$ K/Pa $\approx -0.0075$ K/atm.

  3. Use a Maxwell relation to show that for an ideal gas $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = nR/V$. medium

    $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V = \partial(nRT/V)/\partial T = nR/V$.
