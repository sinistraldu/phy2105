Electrostatics

Charges at rest: the forces between them, the fields and potentials they create, Gauss’s shortcut for symmetric problems, and how capacitors and dielectrics store electrical energy.

Classes 1–2

Charge, Coulomb’s law, electric field, dipoles, flux and Gauss’s law

By the end you can: add Coulomb forces as vectors; find E for point charges and continuous distributions; derive the field and torque of a dipole; compute flux; use Gauss’s law for spheres, lines and planes.

1.1Electric charge

Big idea

Charge comes in two kinds (like charges repel, unlike attract), is quantised ($q = ne$, $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C) and is conserved. Conductors let charge move freely; insulators do not.

1.2Coulomb’s law and superposition

Key result
$$\vec F_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r_{12}, \qquad k=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}$$

The force on one charge due to several others is the vector sum of the individual forces (superposition): $\vec F = \sum_i \vec F_i$.

Simulation

Superposition of Coulomb forces

Try: drag the three source charges and the test charge $q_0$ (green). Change the charges with the sliders. The thin arrows are the individual forces; the thick arrow is their vector sum.

What to notice: each force points along the line joining the two charges and falls as $1/r^2$. The net force is found by adding components, not magnitudes; it can be zero at special points.

1.3Electric field

Key result
$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0}, \qquad \vec E_{\text{point}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat r, \qquad \vec E = \int \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}\hat r$$
Derivation: field on the axis of a charged ring

Step 1A ring of radius $R$ carries charge $q$ uniformly. A small piece $dq$ is a distance $r=\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$ from a point $P$ on the axis.

Step 2By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel in pairs. Only the axial part $dE\cos\theta$ survives, with $\cos\theta = z/r$.

Step 3$$E = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r^2}\frac{z}{r} = \frac{kz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\int dq = \frac{kqz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$$

CheckFor $z\gg R$, $E\to kq/z^2$: from far away the ring looks like a point charge.

Simulation

Field lines and equipotentials

Try: choose a set-up and drag the charges. Change the size of charge 2. Switch on Equipotentials.

What to notice: lines start on + and end on −, never cross, and crowd where $E$ is strong. Equipotentials always cross field lines at right angles.

1.4The electric dipole

Key results
$$\vec p = q\vec d, \qquad E_{\text{axis}} \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{z^3}, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E, \qquad U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$$
Derivation: field on the dipole axis

Step 1Charges $+q$ at $z=d/2$ and $-q$ at $z=-d/2$. At a point on the axis a distance $z$ from the centre: $$E = kq\left[\frac{1}{(z-d/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z+d/2)^2}\right]$$

Step 2Combine: $\displaystyle E = kq\,\frac{2zd}{(z^2-d^2/4)^2}$.

Step 3For $z \gg d$: $\displaystyle E \approx \frac{2kqd}{z^3} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{z^3}$. A dipole field falls as $1/r^3$, faster than a point charge.

Derivation: torque and energy in a uniform field

Step 1In a uniform $\vec E$ the forces $+q\vec E$ and $-q\vec E$ cancel, so the net force is zero. They form a couple with moment arm $d\sin\theta$: $\tau = qE\,d\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta$, i.e. $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$.

Step 2Work done by the field when the dipole turns from $\theta_0$ to $\theta$: $W = -\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}pE\sin\theta'\,d\theta' = pE(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)$.

Step 3$U = -W$; choosing $U=0$ at $\theta_0 = 90^\circ$ gives $U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E$. Minimum (stable) at $\theta=0$, maximum at $\theta=180^\circ$.

3D simulation

A dipole twisting in a uniform field

Try: set $p$, $E$ and the starting angle, then press Release. Add damping to let it settle. Drag to rotate the view.

What to notice: the torque $pE\sin\theta$ swings the dipole toward the field and it oscillates about $\theta=0$ (like a pendulum), with $U=-pE\cos\theta$ trading with rotational kinetic energy. A larger $pE$ means faster oscillation.

1.5Electric flux and flux density

Key result
$$\Phi_E = \int \vec E\cdot d\vec A = EA\cos\theta \ \text{(uniform field, flat surface)}, \qquad \vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E \ \text{(flux density in vacuum)}$$

Flux measures how much field passes through a surface. It is largest when the surface faces the field ($\theta=0$) and zero when the field skims along it.

3D simulation

Flux through a tilted surface

Try: tilt the surface, change its area and the field strength. Drag to look along the field.

What to notice: only the component of $\vec E$ along the normal $\hat n$ (purple) gets through: $\Phi = EA\cos\theta$. At $90^\circ$ the field lines slide past and the flux is zero.

1.6Gauss’s law

Key result
$$\varepsilon_0\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{enc}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \oint \vec D\cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{enc}}$$

The total flux out of any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. Charges outside contribute equal inward and outward flux.

Derivation: Coulomb’s law from Gauss’s law

Step 1Surround a point charge $q$ with a sphere of radius $r$. By symmetry $\vec E$ is radial and has the same size everywhere on the sphere.

Step 2$\varepsilon_0\oint E\,dA = \varepsilon_0 E(4\pi r^2) = q$, so $E = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$. Then $F = q_0E$ is Coulomb’s law.

Applications

Infinite line ($\lambda$ C/m), Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$, length $h$: $\varepsilon_0E(2\pi rh) = \lambda h \Rightarrow E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$.

Infinite sheet ($\sigma$ C/m²), pillbox of face area $A$: $\varepsilon_0(2EA) = \sigma A \Rightarrow E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$, independent of distance.

Charged conductor: $E=0$ inside, so a pillbox with one face inside gives $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside.

Uniformly charged insulating sphere (radius $R$, charge $Q$): outside $E = kQ/r^2$; inside, $q_{\text{enc}} = Q r^3/R^3$ so $E = \dfrac{kQ}{R^3}\,r$, growing linearly from zero at the centre.

Simulation

Gauss’s law: E(r) for different charge shapes

Try: pick a charge distribution and slide the Gaussian surface radius $r$ in and out. Change the total charge and the object’s radius $R$.

What to notice: outside any sphere the field is exactly $kQ/r^2$. Inside a conductor it is zero; inside an insulating sphere it grows like $r$. A line gives $1/r$ and a sheet gives a constant.

3D simulation

Flux through a Gaussian sphere

Try: change the sphere’s radius and move the two charges in and out of it. Change their values.

What to notice: the net flux only changes when a charge crosses the surface. A charge outside sends flux in on one side and out on the other, adding nothing: $\Phi = q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$.

Worked example

Charges $q_1 = +2\ \mu$C at the origin and $q_2 = -3\ \mu$C at $x = 0.5$ m. Find $E$ at $x = 1.0$ m.

Step 1$E_1 = kq_1/1.0^2 = 1.80\times10^4$ N/C, pointing $+x$.

Step 2$E_2 = k(3\ \mu\text{C})/0.5^2 = 1.08\times10^5$ N/C, pointing $-x$ (toward the negative charge).

Step 3$E = 1.80\times10^4 - 1.08\times10^5 = -8.99\times10^4$ N/C, i.e. $8.99\times10^4$ N/C in the $-x$ direction.

Practice problems

  1. Two charges of $+4\ \mu$C and $-2\ \mu$C are 30 cm apart. Find the force between them. easy

    $F = k|q_1q_2|/r^2 = 8.99\times10^9 \times 8\times10^{-12}/0.09 \approx 0.80$ N, attractive.

  2. A ring of radius 10 cm carries 5 nC. Find $E$ on its axis 20 cm from the centre. medium

    $E = kqz/(z^2+R^2)^{3/2} = 8.99\times10^9\times5\times10^{-9}\times0.2/(0.05)^{3/2} \approx 805$ N/C.

  3. A dipole has $p = 6.2\times10^{-30}$ C m in a field of $10^5$ N/C. Find the maximum torque and the work needed to turn it from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$. medium

    $\tau_{\max} = pE = 6.2\times10^{-25}$ N m. $W = U(180^\circ)-U(0^\circ) = 2pE = 1.24\times10^{-24}$ J.

  4. A long wire carries $\lambda = 2\ \mu$C/m. Find $E$ at 5 cm. easy

    $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r) = 2\times10^{-6}/(2\pi\times8.85\times10^{-12}\times0.05) \approx 7.2\times10^5$ N/C.

  5. A cube of side 10 cm sits in a uniform field of 500 N/C along one edge. Find the flux through each face and the net flux. easy

    Faces facing the field: $\pm EA = \pm 5$ N m²/C; the other four: 0. Net flux = 0 (no charge inside).

  6. A solid insulating sphere ($R = 10$ cm, $Q = 8$ nC). Find $E$ at $r = 5$ cm and $r = 20$ cm. medium

    Inside: $E = kQr/R^3 = 8.99\times10^9\times8\times10^{-9}\times0.05/10^{-3} \approx 3.6\times10^3$ N/C. Outside: $kQ/r^2 = 71.9/0.04 \approx 1.8\times10^3$ N/C.

Classes 3–4

Electric potential, potential energy and the field–potential relation

By the end you can: define $V$ and $U$; derive $V$ for a point charge, a dipole, a ring and a disc; get $\vec E$ from $V$ with the gradient; describe equipotential surfaces.

3.1Potential energy and potential

Key result
$$\Delta U = -W_{\text{field}}, \qquad V = \frac{U}{q_0}, \qquad V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E\cdot d\vec s$$

Potential (volts) is energy per unit charge. Only differences matter; we usually set $V = 0$ at infinity.

Derivation: potential of a point charge

Step 1Take the path straight out along $\hat r$ from $r$ to $\infty$: $V(\infty)-V(r) = -\int_r^\infty \dfrac{kq}{r'^2}dr'$.

Step 2$\displaystyle -\int_r^\infty \frac{kq}{r'^2}dr' = -\left[-\frac{kq}{r'}\right]_r^\infty = -\frac{kq}{r}$. With $V(\infty)=0$:

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}, \qquad U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r}$$
Derivation: potential of a dipole and of continuous charges

Dipole. $V = kq\left(\dfrac1{r_+}-\dfrac1{r_-}\right) = kq\dfrac{r_- - r_+}{r_+r_-}$. Far away, $r_- - r_+ \approx d\cos\theta$ and $r_+r_- \approx r^2$, so $V \approx \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}$.

Ring on its axis: every $dq$ is the same distance away, so $V = \dfrac{kq}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}$.

Disc ($\sigma$, radius $R$): add rings of radius $r'$, charge $\sigma 2\pi r'dr'$: $V = \displaystyle\int_0^R \frac{k\sigma 2\pi r'dr'}{\sqrt{z^2+r'^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{z^2+R^2}-z\right)$.

3.2Field from potential: the gradient

Key result
$$E_s = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial s}, \qquad \vec E = -\nabla V = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\hat i + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\hat j + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\hat k\right)$$

$\vec E$ points “downhill” in potential, along the steepest descent, and is perpendicular to every equipotential surface (surfaces of constant $V$). Moving a charge along an equipotential costs no work.

Check with the ring

$E_z = -\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\dfrac{kq}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}} = \dfrac{kqz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$, exactly the field found by integration in 1.3, with much less work.

Simulation

Potential and its slope: E = −dV/dx

Try: pick a charge arrangement and move the probe point $x$. The tangent line on the $V$ graph has slope $dV/dx$; the lower graph shows $E_x = -dV/dx$.

What to notice: where $V$ is steep, $E$ is large; where $V$ has a maximum or minimum, $E=0$. The field always points toward lower potential.

3D simulation

The potential landscape

Try: change the two charges and their separation. Press Roll a test charge to release a positive charge that slides downhill. Drag to look from the side.

What to notice: height is $V(x,y)$: positive charges make hills, negative charges make pits. A positive test charge rolls down the steepest slope, which is the direction of $\vec E = -\nabla V$. Contour lines at equal height are equipotentials.

Worked example

$V(x,y) = 3x^2y - 2y^3$ volts (x, y in m). Find $\vec E$ at $(1, 2)$.

$E_x = -\partial V/\partial x = -6xy = -12$ V/m; $E_y = -(3x^2 - 6y^2) = -(3 - 24) = 21$ V/m. So $\vec E = -12\hat i + 21\hat j$ V/m.

Practice problems

  1. Find the potential 2 cm from a proton and the potential energy of an electron there. easy

    $V = ke/r = 8.99\times10^9\times1.6\times10^{-19}/0.02 = 7.2\times10^{-8}$ V; $U = -eV = -1.15\times10^{-26}$ J.

  2. How much work is needed to bring three $+2\ \mu$C charges from infinity to the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 10 cm? medium

    Three pairs: $U = 3kq^2/a = 3\times8.99\times10^9\times4\times10^{-12}/0.1 \approx 1.08$ J.

  3. Find $V$ on the axis of a disc ($R = 5$ cm, $\sigma = 1\ \mu$C/m²) at $z = 5$ cm. medium

    $V = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}(\sqrt{z^2+R^2}-z) = 5.65\times10^4\times(0.0707-0.05) \approx 1.17\times10^3$ V.

  4. $V = 5x^2 - 3xy + z$ (SI). Find $\vec E$ at $(1, 1, 1)$. easy

    $\vec E = -(10x - 3y)\hat i - (-3x)\hat j - \hat k = -7\hat i + 3\hat j - \hat k$ V/m.

  5. A dipole ($p = 2\times10^{-9}$ C m). Find $V$ at $r = 10$ cm on its axis and at $90^\circ$. easy

    Axis: $kp/r^2 = 8.99\times10^9\times2\times10^{-9}/0.01 \approx 1.8\times10^3$ V. At $90^\circ$: $V = 0$.

Class 5

Capacitors, stored energy, the electron volt and dielectrics

By the end you can: derive $C$ for a parallel-plate capacitor; combine capacitors in series and parallel; derive $U = \tfrac12CV^2$ and $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$; use $\kappa$, $\vec P$ and $\vec D$.

5.1Capacitance

Key results
$$C = \frac{Q}{V}, \qquad C_{\text{plates}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$
Derivation: parallel-plate capacitor

Step 1Plates of area $A$ carry $\pm Q$, so $\sigma = Q/A$. Gauss’s law (conductor surface) gives $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0A)$ between the plates.

Step 2$V = Ed = Qd/(\varepsilon_0 A)$.

Step 3$C = Q/V = \varepsilon_0A/d$: it depends only on geometry.

Derivation: series and parallel

Parallel: same $V$ on each, charges add: $Q = \sum C_iV$, so $C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2 + \cdots$

Series: the same $Q$ sits on each (charge conservation on the isolated middle plates) and voltages add: $V = \sum Q/C_i$, so $\dfrac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac1{C_1}+\dfrac1{C_2}+\cdots$

Simulation

Capacitor network

Try: choose series, parallel or mixed, change $C_1$, $C_2$, $C_3$ and the battery. Read the charge, voltage and energy of each capacitor.

What to notice: in series every capacitor has the same $Q$ and the smallest $C$ takes the biggest voltage; in parallel each has the full $V$ and the biggest $C$ stores the most charge and energy.

5.2Energy stored and energy density

Derivation

Step 1Moving charge $dq$ across the gap when the capacitor already holds $q$ costs $dW = V\,dq = (q/C)\,dq$.

Step 2$U = \displaystyle\int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac12CV^2 = \frac12QV$.

Step 3For plates, $U = \tfrac12(\varepsilon_0A/d)(Ed)^2 = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2(Ad)$. Dividing by the volume $Ad$:

$$u = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 \quad \text{(J/m}^3\text{)}$$

The energy is stored in the field itself. Electron volt: $1$ eV $= 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J, the energy an electron gains through 1 V.

5.3Dielectrics, polarization and displacement

Key results
$$C = \kappa C_0, \qquad \vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E, \qquad \vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon_0\kappa\vec E = \varepsilon\vec E, \qquad \kappa = 1+\chi_e$$

An insulator in a field polarizes: its molecules’ dipoles line up, producing bound surface charge $\sigma_b = P$ that partly cancels the field inside. With the same free charge, $E$ and $V$ drop by $\kappa$, so $C$ rises by $\kappa$.

Simulation

Slide a dielectric into a capacitor

Try: raise $\kappa$. Compare Battery connected (fixed $V$) with Battery removed (fixed $Q$).

What to notice: with fixed $Q$, the bound charges (pale) cancel part of the free charge, so $E$, $V$ and $U$ fall by $\kappa$. With fixed $V$, the battery pushes in more charge: $Q$ and $U$ grow by $\kappa$, but $E = V/d$ stays the same.

3D simulation

Parallel-plate capacitor in 3D

Try: change the plate area, the gap, the voltage, and slide the dielectric slab in.

What to notice: $C = \kappa\varepsilon_0A/d$ grows with area and shrinks with gap. The field between the plates is uniform; the stored energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ is spread through the volume between them.

Worked example

2 µF and 3 µF in series across 10 V. Find $C_{\text{eq}}$, $Q$, each voltage and the total energy.

$C_{\text{eq}} = 6/5 = 1.2\ \mu$F; $Q = 12\ \mu$C; $V_1 = 6$ V, $V_2 = 4$ V; $U = \tfrac12(1.2\times10^{-6})(100) = 60\ \mu$J.

Practice problems

  1. Plates of area 100 cm², 1 mm apart, at 50 V. Find $C$, $Q$, $E$ and $U$. easy

    $C = 8.85\times10^{-12}\times0.01/0.001 = 88.5$ pF; $Q = 4.43$ nC; $E = 5\times10^4$ V/m; $U = \tfrac12CV^2 = 1.1\times10^{-7}$ J.

  2. Three 6 µF capacitors: find $C_{\text{eq}}$ (a) all in series, (b) all in parallel, (c) two in series, that pair in parallel with the third. easy

    (a) 2 µF; (b) 18 µF; (c) 3 + 6 = 9 µF.

  3. Find the energy density in a field of $10^6$ V/m in air. easy

    $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = 0.5\times8.85\times10^{-12}\times10^{12} \approx 4.4$ J/m³.

  4. A charged, isolated capacitor (100 pF, 200 V) is filled with a dielectric of $\kappa = 4$. Find the new $C$, $V$ and the energy change. medium

    $Q = 20$ nC fixed. $C = 400$ pF, $V = 50$ V. $U$: $2\ \mu$J $\to 0.5\ \mu$J, so 1.5 µJ is released (the slab is pulled in).

  5. In a dielectric of $\kappa = 5$ the field is $10^4$ V/m. Find $D$ and $P$. medium

    $D = \kappa\varepsilon_0E = 4.43\times10^{-7}$ C/m²; $P = D - \varepsilon_0E = 3.54\times10^{-7}$ C/m².

  6. An electron is accelerated through 500 V. Give its kinetic energy in eV and in J. easy

    500 eV $= 8.0\times10^{-17}$ J.
