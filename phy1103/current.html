Class 6

Laplace’s and Poisson’s equations, dielectrics, current and current density

By the end you can: derive $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$; solve it in one dimension; apply Gauss’s law with a dielectric using $\vec D$; define $I$, $\vec J$ and charge density; derive $J = nqv_d$.

6.1Poisson’s and Laplace’s equations

Derivation

Step 1Gauss’s law for a tiny volume $dV$ (divergence theorem) gives the differential form: $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, where $\rho$ is the volume charge density.

Step 2Substitute $\vec E = -\nabla V$: $\nabla\cdot(-\nabla V) = \rho/\varepsilon_0$.

$$\nabla^2 V = \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{(Poisson)}, \qquad \nabla^2 V = 0 \quad\text{(Laplace, where } \rho = 0)$$

Step 3In one dimension between plates at $x=0$ ($V_0$) and $x=L$ ($V_L$) with no charge: $V'' = 0 \Rightarrow V = V_0 + (V_L-V_0)\,x/L$, a straight line (uniform $E$).

Step 4With a uniform charge density $\rho$: $V'' = -\rho/\varepsilon_0 \Rightarrow V = -\dfrac{\rho}{2\varepsilon_0}x^2 + Ax + B$, a parabola. The constants come from the boundary values.

Laplace’s equation says the potential in a charge-free region has no local maximum or minimum: at every point $V$ is the average of its neighbours. That is why an electron cannot be held at rest by electrostatic forces alone, and why the inside of a closed conductor is field-free (a Faraday cage).

Simulation

Solve Poisson’s equation between two plates

Try: set the two plate voltages, then fill the gap with charge (positive or negative $\rho$). The lower graph is $E = -dV/dx$.

What to notice: with $\rho = 0$ the potential is a straight line and $E$ is constant (Laplace). Positive charge bends $V$ into a hill (curvature $-\rho/\varepsilon_0$), and $E$ then changes linearly across the gap, exactly as Gauss’s law requires.

6.2Capacitor with a dielectric; Gauss’s law in a dielectric

Derivation

Step 1Inside a dielectric the enclosed charge has two parts: free charge $q$ on the plates and bound charge $-q'$ on the dielectric surface. Gauss: $\varepsilon_0\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = q - q'$.

Step 2Experiment shows the field is reduced by $\kappa$: $E = E_0/\kappa$, so $q - q' = q/\kappa$, i.e. $q' = q(1 - 1/\kappa)$.

Step 3Hence $\varepsilon_0\oint\kappa\vec E\cdot d\vec A = q$. Writing $\vec D = \kappa\varepsilon_0\vec E = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$:

$$\oint \vec D\cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{free}}$$

Only the free charge appears; the bound charge is hidden inside $\vec D$. For a plate capacitor this gives $C = \kappa\varepsilon_0A/d$.

3D simulation

Molecules polarizing in a dielectric

Try: raise the applied field and the susceptibility $\chi_e$; lower the temperature to reduce the thermal jiggling.

What to notice: the molecular dipoles (arrows) swing toward the field. Their alignment is the polarization $\vec P$; it leaves a layer of bound charge on each face that opposes the applied field, so the field inside drops to $E_0/\kappa$.

6.3Current, current density and charge density

Key results
$$I = \frac{dq}{dt}, \qquad I = \int \vec J\cdot d\vec A, \qquad \vec J = nq\vec v_d = \rho_q\vec v_d$$

Current (A) is a scalar rate of charge flow; current density $\vec J$ (A/m²) is a vector that says how the flow is spread over a cross-section. $\rho_q = nq$ is the density of mobile charge.

Derivation: J = nqvd

Step 1Carriers (density $n$, charge $q$) drift with average speed $v_d$. In time $dt$ they move a distance $v_d\,dt$.

Step 2All carriers in the slab of volume $A\,v_d\,dt$ cross the area $A$: $dq = nq\,(A v_d\,dt)$.

Step 3$I = dq/dt = nqAv_d$, so $J = I/A = nqv_d$.

3D simulation

Electrons drifting through a wire that narrows

Try: change the current and how much the wire narrows. Watch the electrons in the narrow part.

What to notice: the current $I$ is the same everywhere (charge is conserved), but in the thin part the same flow is squeezed through a smaller area, so $J = I/A$ and the drift speed are larger there.

Worked example: drift speed in copper

A copper wire ($n = 8.5\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$) of cross-section 1 mm² carries 1 A. $v_d = I/(nqA) = 1/(8.5\times10^{28}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{-6}) \approx 7.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s, less than 0.1 mm/s. The signal travels fast, but each electron crawls.

Practice problems

  1. Between plates 1 cm apart the gap holds uniform charge $\rho = 1\ \mu$C/m³, and both plates are at 0 V. Find $V$ at the midpoint. medium

    $V = \frac{\rho}{2\varepsilon_0}x(L-x)$, at $x = L/2$: $V = \rho L^2/(8\varepsilon_0) = 10^{-6}\times10^{-4}/(8\times8.85\times10^{-12}) \approx 1.41$ V.

  2. Show that $V = 3x^2 - 3y^2$ satisfies Laplace’s equation. easy

    $\partial^2V/\partial x^2 = 6$, $\partial^2V/\partial y^2 = -6$, sum $= 0$.

  3. A 2 A current flows in a wire of radius 1 mm. Find $J$. easy

    $J = I/(\pi r^2) = 2/(\pi\times10^{-6}) \approx 6.4\times10^5$ A/m².

  4. A capacitor plate carries free charge 10 nC; the dielectric has $\kappa = 5$. Find the bound charge on the dielectric surface. easy

    $q' = q(1-1/\kappa) = 10\times0.8 = 8$ nC.

Class 7

Resistance, resistivity, colour code, Ohm’s law and EMF

By the end you can: derive $R = \rho L/A$ and the microscopic form $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$; derive $\rho = m/(ne^2\tau)$; read a colour code; use $V = \varepsilon - Ir$ and the maximum power theorem.

7.1Resistance and resistivity

Key results
$$R = \frac{V}{I}, \qquad \rho = \frac{E}{J}, \qquad R = \rho\frac{L}{A}, \qquad G = \frac1R, \qquad \sigma = \frac1\rho, \qquad \rho(T) = \rho_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]$$
Derivation: the resistance law

Step 1In a uniform wire, $E = V/L$ and $J = I/A$.

Step 2$\rho = E/J = (V/L)/(I/A) = \dfrac{V}{I}\dfrac{A}{L} = R\dfrac{A}{L}$.

Step 3So $R = \rho L/A$: longer wires resist more, thicker wires less.

Simulation

Resistance of a wire

Try: change the length, the diameter and the temperature, and switch materials.

What to notice: doubling $L$ doubles $R$; doubling the diameter quarters it ($A \propto d^2$). Metals get more resistive when hot (positive $\alpha$); a semiconductor such as carbon gets less resistive (negative $\alpha$).

7.2Why metals obey Ohm’s law: the expression for resistivity

Derivation (free-electron / Drude model)

Step 1An electron in field $E$ accelerates: $a = eE/m$. It collides with the lattice on average every $\tau$ seconds, losing its extra velocity each time.

Step 2Average drift speed: $v_d = a\tau = \dfrac{eE\tau}{m}$.

Step 3$J = nev_d = \dfrac{ne^2\tau}{m}E$. This is Ohm’s law in microscopic form, $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, with

$$\sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m}, \qquad \rho = \frac{m}{ne^2\tau}$$

$J$ is proportional to $E$ because $\tau$ does not depend on $E$. Heating the metal makes the lattice vibrate more, so $\tau$ falls and $\rho$ rises.

Simulation

Drift velocity and collisions

Try: switch the field on, then change the collision time $\tau$ (cleaner, colder metal = longer $\tau$) and the electron density.

What to notice: each electron moves randomly at high speed, but between collisions it gains a small push against the field. The average of all those pushes is the slow drift $v_d = eE\tau/m$. Longer $\tau$ means less resistance.

7.4EMF and internal resistance

Key results
$$\varepsilon = \frac{dW}{dq}, \qquad V_{\text{terminal}} = \varepsilon - Ir, \qquad I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R+r}, \qquad P_{\text{load}} = \frac{\varepsilon^2R}{(R+r)^2}$$

An EMF is not a force: it is the work per unit charge done by a non-electrostatic agent (chemical, magnetic) that pushes charge “uphill” inside the source.

Derivation: maximum power transfer

$\dfrac{dP}{dR} = \varepsilon^2\dfrac{(R+r)^2 - 2R(R+r)}{(R+r)^4} = 0 \Rightarrow R = r$, giving $P_{\max} = \dfrac{\varepsilon^2}{4r}$ (at 50% efficiency).

Simulation

A real battery: EMF, internal resistance and power

Try: change the load $R$ and watch the terminal voltage and the power curve. Then change $r$.

What to notice: the terminal voltage falls as more current is drawn. The power delivered to the load peaks exactly when $R = r$.

Worked example

A 12 V battery with $r = 0.5\ \Omega$ drives a 5 Ω load. $I = 12/5.5 = 2.18$ A; $V = 12 - 2.18\times0.5 = 10.9$ V; $P_{\text{load}} = I^2R = 23.8$ W. Maximum possible load power: $\varepsilon^2/4r = 72$ W at $R = 0.5\ \Omega$.

Practice problems

  1. Find the resistance of 10 m of copper wire ($\rho = 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega$ m) of diameter 1 mm. What is it at 100 °C ($\alpha = 0.0039$/K, $T_0 = 20$ °C)? easy

    $R = \rho L/A = 1.7\times10^{-8}\times10/(\pi\times(0.5\times10^{-3})^2) \approx 0.216\ \Omega$. At 100 °C: $0.216\times(1 + 0.0039\times80) \approx 0.284\ \Omega$.

  2. Estimate the collision time $\tau$ in copper ($\rho = 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega$ m, $n = 8.5\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$). medium

    $\tau = m/(ne^2\rho) = 9.11\times10^{-31}/(8.5\times10^{28}\times2.56\times10^{-38}\times1.7\times10^{-8}) \approx 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s.

  3. Read: brown, black, orange, gold. easy

    $10\times10^3 = 10$ kΩ ±5%.

  4. A cell gives 1.5 V on open circuit and 1.2 V when delivering 0.6 A. Find $r$. easy

    $Ir = 0.3$ V, so $r = 0.5\ \Omega$.

  5. Find the resistance of 1 m of nichrome wire ($\rho = 1.1\times10^{-6}\ \Omega$ m), diameter 0.5 mm. easy

    $R = 1.1\times10^{-6}/(\pi\times(0.25\times10^{-3})^2) \approx 5.6\ \Omega$.

Current

$I = dq/dt$, $J = I/A$

$J = nqv_d$

Resistance

$R = \rho L/A$, $G = 1/R$, $\sigma = 1/\rho$
