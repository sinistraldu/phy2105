Magnetism and Induction

Moving charges feel magnetic forces and create magnetic fields. Changing magnetic flux creates EMFs. These two facts run every motor, generator, transformer and sensor in electrical engineering.

Class 8

Magnetic field and flux, the Lorentz force, charged particle motion and the Hall effect

By the end you can: use $\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$; derive the radius and frequency of circular motion; explain why $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0$; derive the Hall voltage; relate $\vec B$, $\vec H$ and $\vec M$.

8.1Magnetic field, flux and Gauss’s law for magnetism

Key results
$$\Phi_B = \int\vec B\cdot d\vec A \ \ (\text{Wb} = \text{T m}^2), \qquad \oint\vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0$$

$\vec B$ is the magnetic flux density (tesla). Magnetic field lines always close on themselves: there are no magnetic monopoles, so every field line that enters a closed surface also leaves it and the net flux is zero.

8.2The Lorentz force

Key result
$$\vec F = q\left(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\right), \qquad F_B = |q|vB\sin\theta$$

The magnetic part is always perpendicular to $\vec v$, so it does no work and cannot change the particle’s speed, only its direction.

Derivation: circular motion and the cyclotron frequency

Step 1For $\vec v\perp\vec B$ the magnetic force supplies the centripetal force: $|q|vB = mv^2/r$.

Step 2$r = \dfrac{mv}{|q|B}$, and the period $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi m}{|q|B}$, independent of speed.

Step 3Cyclotron frequency $f = \dfrac{|q|B}{2\pi m}$. If $\vec v$ has a component along $\vec B$, that part is untouched and the path becomes a helix.

Velocity selector

Crossed $\vec E$ and $\vec B$: the electric force $qE$ and magnetic force $qvB$ point opposite ways. They cancel when $qE = qvB$, so only particles with $v = E/B$ pass straight through.

Simulation

Velocity selector: crossed E and B

Try: change $E$, $B$ and the particle speed $v$ until the particle goes straight through the slit. Then flip the charge sign.

What to notice: only $v = E/B$ goes straight; faster particles are bent by the magnetic force, slower ones by the electric force. Flipping the sign flips both forces, so the selected speed is the same for any charge.

3D simulation

A charge in E and B fields

Try: change $B$, the launch angle and speed; then add an electric field $E$ at right angles to $B$.

What to notice: with $E = 0$ the path is a circle or a helix with $r = mv\sin\theta/qB$. Switching on $E$ makes the whole orbit drift sideways at $E/B$ (the $\vec E\times\vec B$ drift), the principle behind magnetrons and plasma traps.

8.3The Hall effect

Derivation

Step 1A strip of thickness $t$ (along $\vec B$) and width $w$ carries current $I$; carriers drift with $v_d$ and are pushed sideways by $qv_dB$.

Step 2Charge piles up on one edge until the transverse Hall field balances the magnetic force: $qE_H = qv_dB$.

Step 3$V_H = E_Hw = v_dBw$. With $I = nqv_d(wt)$: $v_d = I/(nqwt)$, so

$$V_H = \frac{IB}{nqt}, \qquad R_H = \frac{1}{nq}$$

The sign of $V_H$ gives the sign of the carriers (electrons in metals, holes in p-type silicon); its size gives $n$. Hall sensors measure $B$ in phones, cars and current clamps.

Simulation

Hall effect in a strip

Try: change $I$, $B$, the thickness and the carrier density. Switch between electrons and holes.

What to notice: carriers are pushed to one edge, building up the Hall voltage. Fewer carriers (a semiconductor) means a much larger $V_H$, and positive carriers give the opposite sign.

8.4Magnetic field intensity

Key results
$$\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M), \qquad \vec M = \chi_m\vec H, \qquad \vec B = \mu_0\mu_r\vec H = \mu\vec H$$

$\vec H$ (A/m) is set by the free currents; $\vec M$ is the magnetization of the material; $\mu_r = 1 + \chi_m$.

Practice problems

  1. An electron moves at $2\times10^6$ m/s perpendicular to a 0.01 T field. Find the radius of its path. easy

    $r = mv/(eB) = 9.11\times10^{-31}\times2\times10^6/(1.6\times10^{-19}\times0.01) \approx 1.1$ mm.

  2. Find the cyclotron frequency of a proton in a 1 T field. easy

    $f = eB/(2\pi m_p) \approx 15.2$ MHz.

  3. A copper strip 0.1 mm thick carries 10 A in a 1.5 T field ($n = 8.5\times10^{28}$ m$^{-3}$). Find $V_H$. medium

    $V_H = IB/(nqt) = 15/(8.5\times10^{28}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{-4}) \approx 11\ \mu$V.

  4. A velocity selector has $E = 2\times10^4$ V/m. What $B$ selects $v = 4\times10^5$ m/s? easy

    $B = E/v = 0.05$ T.

  5. Find the flux through a 200 cm² loop in 0.4 T when the normal makes 60° with $\vec B$. easy

    $\Phi = BA\cos60^\circ = 0.4\times0.02\times0.5 = 4$ mWb.

Class 9

Magnetic dipole moment, Biot–Savart law, Ampère’s law and magnetic materials

By the end you can: find the torque on a current loop; derive $B$ for a straight wire, a loop and a solenoid; use Ampère’s law; describe dia-, para- and ferromagnetism and a hysteresis loop.

9.1Magnetic dipole moment

Key results
$$\vec\mu = NI\vec A, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec\mu\times\vec B, \qquad U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$$

A current loop behaves like a tiny bar magnet: it twists to line up with the field. This torque drives every DC motor and moving-coil meter. (Compare the electric dipole: $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$.)

3D simulation

Torque on a current loop (a simple motor)

Try: change the current, turns, loop size and field. Switch the commutator on to make it spin continuously.

What to notice: the forces on the two sides form a couple; $\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$ is largest when the plane of the loop is parallel to $\vec B$ and zero when $\vec\mu$ lines up with $\vec B$. Without a commutator the loop just rocks about that position.

9.2The Biot–Savart law

Key result
$$d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}, \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$$
Derivation: long straight wire

Step 1Element $dl$ at position $l$ along the wire, point $P$ at perpendicular distance $R$. $|d\vec l\times\hat r| = dl\sin\phi$, with $r = \sqrt{l^2+R^2}$ and $\sin\phi = R/r$.

Step 2$\displaystyle B = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{R\,dl}{(l^2+R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{R}$.

$$B_{\text{wire}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}$$
Derivation: circular loop

Centre: every element is at distance $R$ and perpendicular to $\hat r$: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R^2}\oint dl = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$.

On the axis at distance $z$: sideways components cancel; the axial parts add: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$. Far away this is $\dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\dfrac{\mu}{z^3}$, a dipole field.

3D simulation

Biot–Savart: building the field of a loop or wire

Try: choose a loop or a straight wire, change the current and loop radius, and move the probe along the axis. Press Sweep a current element to see each $d\vec B$ contribution.

What to notice: each element contributes a small $d\vec B$ perpendicular to both $d\vec l$ and $\hat r$. For a loop the sideways parts cancel and the axial parts add up to $B = \mu_0IR^2/2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}$; for a wire the field circles it.

9.3Ampère’s law and its applications

Key result
$$\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$
Applications

Long wire (circle of radius $r$): $B(2\pi r) = \mu_0I \Rightarrow B = \mu_0I/2\pi r$. Inside a wire of radius $a$ with uniform current: $I_{\text{enc}} = Ir^2/a^2$, so $B = \mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2$.

Solenoid ($n$ turns per metre). Rectangle of length $h$, one side inside: only that side contributes, $Bh = \mu_0(nh)I$:

$$B_{\text{solenoid}} = \mu_0 nI$$

Toroid ($N$ turns), circle of radius $r$ inside the core: $B(2\pi r) = \mu_0NI \Rightarrow B = \dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi r}$; zero outside.

3D simulation

Inside a solenoid

Try: change the number of turns, the length and the current. Slide the Ampèrian rectangle in and out of the coil.

What to notice: the field inside is strong and uniform, outside it is almost zero. Ampère’s law gives $B = \mu_0 nI$: only the turn density $n = N/L$ matters, not the coil’s width. When the rectangle is fully outside, it encloses no net current.

9.4Magnetic materials, magnetization and hysteresis

Type$\chi_m$OriginExamples
Diamagneticsmall, negative ($\sim-10^{-5}$)induced orbital moments oppose $\vec B$copper, water, bismuth
Paramagneticsmall, positive ($\sim10^{-5}$–$10^{-3}$)permanent atomic moments, partly alignedaluminium, oxygen
Ferromagnetichuge ($10^2$–$10^5$), non-lineardomains of aligned momentsiron, nickel, cobalt

In a ferromagnet $B$ depends on its history. Cycling $H$ traces a hysteresis loop: the retentivity (remanence) $B_r$ is what is left at $H = 0$; the coercivity $H_c$ is the reverse field needed to bring $B$ back to zero. The loop area is the energy lost as heat per cycle per unit volume. Soft iron (narrow loop) suits transformer cores; hard steel (wide loop) suits permanent magnets.

Simulation

Trace a hysteresis loop

Try: pick a material and raise the amplitude of the applied field $H$. Watch the loop being drawn.

What to notice: a soft material has a thin loop (small $H_c$, little loss); a hard material has a wide loop (large $H_c$, keeps its magnetism). Small drive amplitudes trace smaller inner loops.

Practice problems

  1. Find $B$ 5 cm from a long wire carrying 10 A. easy

    $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) = 4\times10^{-5}$ T.

  2. Find $B$ at the centre of a single loop of radius 10 cm carrying 5 A. easy

    $B = \mu_0I/2R = 3.14\times10^{-5}$ T.

  3. A solenoid has 1000 turns per metre and carries 2 A. Find $B$ inside. easy

    $B = \mu_0nI = 2.51$ mT.

  4. A toroid of 500 turns, mean radius 10 cm, carries 3 A. Find $B$ in the core. easy

    $B = \mu_0NI/(2\pi r) = 3.0$ mT.

  5. A 50-turn coil of radius 5 cm carries 2 A in a 0.5 T field at 30° between $\vec\mu$ and $\vec B$. Find $\mu$ and $\tau$. medium

    $\mu = NI\pi r^2 = 0.785$ A m²; $\tau = \mu B\sin30^\circ = 0.196$ N m.

Classes 10–11

Faraday’s law, Lenz’s law, mutual and self-inductance, energy and MMF

By the end you can: use $\varepsilon = -N\,d\Phi/dt$ and Lenz’s law; derive motional and generator EMFs; define and calculate $M$ and $L$; derive $U = \tfrac12LI^2$ and $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0$; use MMF and reluctance.

10.1Faraday’s and Lenz’s laws

Key results
$$\varepsilon = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

Lenz’s law (the minus sign): the induced current flows so as to oppose the change in flux that produced it. Otherwise energy would be created from nothing.

Derivation: motional EMF

Step 1A rod of length $L$ slides at speed $v$ on rails in field $B$. The circuit area grows at $dA/dt = Lv$.

Step 2$\varepsilon = d(BA)/dt = BLv$. (Equivalently, each charge in the rod feels $qvB$ along it, doing work $qvBL$.)

Derivation: AC generator

A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ spins at $\omega$ in field $B$: $\Phi = BA\cos\omega t$, so $\varepsilon = -N\dfrac{d\Phi}{dt} = NBA\omega\sin\omega t$, peak $\varepsilon_0 = NBA\omega$.

Simulation

Push a magnet through a coil

Try: press Push magnet, then change its speed, strength and the number of turns. Flip the magnet.

What to notice: the EMF is the slope of the flux graph: zero when the flux is momentarily constant, opposite signs entering and leaving. A faster magnet, a stronger magnet or more turns all give a bigger EMF.

3D simulation

An AC generator

Try: change the rotation speed, field, coil area and number of turns.

What to notice: the EMF is largest when the coil’s plane is parallel to $\vec B$ (flux zero but changing fastest) and zero when it faces the field (flux maximum, momentarily constant). Doubling $\omega$ doubles both the frequency and the peak voltage.

10.2Mutual and self-inductance

Key results
$$M = \frac{N_2\Phi_{21}}{I_1}, \quad \varepsilon_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt}, \qquad L = \frac{N\Phi}{I}, \quad \varepsilon = -L\frac{dI}{dt}$$
Derivation: inductance of a solenoid

Step 1Length $l$, $N$ turns, area $A$: $B = \mu_0(N/l)I$ and $\Phi = BA$ through each turn.

Step 2$L = \dfrac{N\Phi}{I} = \dfrac{N\mu_0NIA}{lI} = \dfrac{\mu_0N^2A}{l} = \mu_0n^2Al$.

10.3Energy stored in an inductor and in a magnetic field

Derivation

Step 1To raise the current against the back-EMF the source supplies power $P = LI\,dI/dt$.

Step 2$U = \displaystyle\int_0^I LI'\,dI' = \frac12LI^2$.

Step 3For a solenoid, $U = \tfrac12\mu_0n^2Al\,I^2 = \dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0}(Al)$, so the energy density is

$$u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}$$
Simulation

Current growth in an RL circuit

Try: close the switch, then open it. Change $L$ and $R$.

What to notice: the inductor resists changes in current: $I = (\varepsilon/R)(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$. The stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ grows as the current builds and is released when the switch opens.

10.4Magnetomotive force (MMF)

Magnetic circuits
$$\mathcal{F} = NI \ \ (\text{ampere-turns}), \qquad \mathcal{R} = \frac{l}{\mu A}, \qquad \Phi = \frac{\mathcal F}{\mathcal R}$$

This is “Ohm’s law” for magnetism: MMF drives flux through a core, opposed by its reluctance. Iron cores have a tiny reluctance (large $\mu$); an air gap adds a large one.

Practice problems

  1. A rod 0.5 m long moves at 3 m/s through a 0.2 T field. Find the EMF. easy

    $\varepsilon = BLv = 0.3$ V.

  2. A 200-turn coil of area 100 cm² sees $B$ fall from 0.5 T to 0.1 T in 0.2 s. Find the average EMF. easy

    $\varepsilon = N\Delta(BA)/\Delta t = 200\times0.4\times0.01/0.2 = 4$ V.

  3. A generator: 100 turns, area 100 cm², $B = 0.2$ T, 50 Hz. Find the peak EMF. easy

    $\varepsilon_0 = NBA\omega = 100\times0.2\times0.01\times2\pi\times50 \approx 62.8$ V.

  4. Find $L$ for a solenoid of 1000 turns, length 50 cm, area 1 cm². medium

    $L = \mu_0N^2A/l = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times10^6\times10^{-4}/0.5 \approx 0.25$ mH.

  5. A 100 mH inductor carries 2 A. Find the stored energy. Find the energy density in a 1 T field. easy

    $U = \tfrac12LI^2 = 0.2$ J; $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0 \approx 4\times10^5$ J/m³.

  6. $M = 2$ mH. The current in coil 1 changes at 100 A/s. Find the EMF in coil 2. easy

    $|\varepsilon_2| = M\,dI/dt = 0.2$ V.

  7. An iron ring ($\mu_r = 1000$, mean length 40 cm, area 4 cm²) has 500 turns carrying 2 A. Find the flux. medium

    $\mathcal R = l/(\mu_0\mu_rA) = 0.4/(4\pi\times10^{-7}\times1000\times4\times10^{-4}) \approx 7.96\times10^5$ A/Wb; $\Phi = NI/\mathcal R = 1000/7.96\times10^5 \approx 1.26$ mWb.
