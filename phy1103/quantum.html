Quantum Physics

Light comes in packets of energy $hf$, particles behave as waves of wavelength $h/p$, and nature limits how precisely position and momentum can be known together. These ideas explain photocells, X-ray machines, crystal diffraction, electron microscopes and the creation of matter from light.

Class 20

Quantum theory of radiation, photons, the photoelectric effect and the Compton effect

By the end you can: explain Planck’s quantum hypothesis; use $E = hf$; derive Einstein’s photoelectric equation and find work function, threshold frequency and stopping voltage; derive the Compton shift.

20.1Quantum theory of radiation

Big idea

Classical physics predicted that a hot body should radiate infinite energy at short wavelengths (the ultraviolet catastrophe, Rayleigh–Jeans law). Planck fixed this in 1900 by assuming that oscillators in the walls can only have energies $nhf$. Light is emitted and absorbed in quanta (photons):

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad p = \frac{h}{\lambda}, \qquad h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$$
$$u(\lambda,T) = \frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\,\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT}-1} \quad\text{(Planck)}, \qquad \lambda_{\max}T = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$$
Simulation

Blackbody radiation: Planck vs Rayleigh–Jeans

Try: change the temperature from a warm stove to the Sun. Switch on the classical Rayleigh–Jeans curve.

What to notice: the classical curve shoots to infinity at short wavelengths; Planck’s quanta make high-frequency modes too “expensive” to excite, so the curve turns over. Hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelength (Wien) and radiate far more ($\propto T^4$).

20.2The photoelectric effect

Einstein’s equation

Step 1A single photon gives all its energy $hf$ to a single electron.

Step 2The electron must spend at least the work function $\phi$ to escape; whatever is left is its maximum kinetic energy:

$$hf = \phi + K_{\max}, \qquad K_{\max} = eV_0, \qquad f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h}, \qquad \lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\phi}$$

ConsequencesBelow the threshold frequency $f_0$ no electrons come out, however bright the light. Above it, $K_{\max}$ rises linearly with $f$ (slope $h$) but does not depend on intensity; intensity only changes the number of electrons (the current). The stopping voltage $V_0$ is the reverse voltage that just stops the fastest electrons. Emission is instantaneous.

Simulation

Photoelectric effect lab

Try: choose a metal, change the wavelength and the intensity, and adjust the voltage. Find the threshold wavelength and the stopping voltage.

What to notice: red light ejects nothing from zinc however bright it is; UV ejects electrons immediately. Brighter light gives more current but the same stopping voltage. The $K_{\max}$–$f$ line has slope $h$ and crosses zero at $f_0$.

3D simulation

Photons knocking out electrons

Try: change the wavelength, the intensity (photons per second) and the work function. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: each photon (yellow) either frees one electron (blue) or nothing. The electrons’ speed depends only on $hf - \phi$; more photons give more electrons, not faster ones.

20.3The Compton effect

Derivation

Step 1A photon (energy $hc/\lambda$, momentum $h/\lambda$) hits a free electron at rest and scatters through angle $\theta$ with wavelength $\lambda'$; the electron recoils.

Step 2Conservation of momentum (vector): $p_e^2 = \left(\dfrac h\lambda\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac h{\lambda'}\right)^2 - \dfrac{2h^2}{\lambda\lambda'}\cos\theta$.

Step 3Conservation of energy (relativistic electron): $\dfrac{hc}\lambda + m_ec^2 = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda'} + \sqrt{p_e^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4}$.

Step 4Square, substitute $p_e^2$ and simplify:

$$\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta), \qquad \frac{h}{m_ec} = 2.426\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}$$

The shift is independent of $\lambda$ and the material, and is noticeable only for X-rays and gamma rays. It proves photons carry momentum.

Simulation

Compton scattering

Try: change the scattering angle and the incident X-ray wavelength.

What to notice: the shift is zero straight ahead and largest ($2h/m_ec = 4.85$ pm) for back-scattering. The same shift is a big fraction of a short wavelength (gamma ray) but tiny for visible light.

Practice problems

  1. Find the energy of a 500 nm photon in eV. easy

    $E = hc/\lambda = 2.48$ eV.

  2. Sodium has $\phi = 2.28$ eV. Find $f_0$, $\lambda_0$, and $K_{\max}$ and $V_0$ for 400 nm light. easy

    $f_0 = 5.51\times10^{14}$ Hz; $\lambda_0 = 544$ nm; $K_{\max} = 3.10 - 2.28 = 0.82$ eV; $V_0 = 0.82$ V.

  3. Why does doubling the intensity of light below threshold still give no photoelectrons? easy

    Each electron absorbs one photon; if $hf < \phi$ no single photon has enough energy, however many arrive.

  4. X-rays scatter at 60°. Find $\Delta\lambda$. easy

    $\Delta\lambda = 2.426\times10^{-12}(1-0.5) = 1.21$ pm.

  5. Find $\lambda_{\max}$ for a filament at 3000 K. easy

    $\lambda_{\max} = 2.898\times10^{-3}/3000 \approx 966$ nm (infrared; most of a bulb’s output is heat).

Class 21

De Broglie waves, group and phase velocity, X-rays and Bragg’s law

By the end you can: find de Broglie wavelengths; derive $v_g = v$ for a matter wave; describe X-ray production, types and spectrum; derive Bragg’s law; use Miller indices to find plane spacings.

21.1De Broglie wavelength

Key result
$$\lambda = \frac hp = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}\ \text{(electron accelerated through } V)$$

Every moving particle has a wavelength. For an electron through 100 V it is 0.123 nm, about an atom’s size, so electrons diffract from crystals (Davisson–Germer). For a cricket ball it is $\sim10^{-34}$ m, far too small to notice.

3D simulation

Electron waves fitting around an orbit

Try: change the orbit number $n$ and the orbit radius. Only when a whole number of wavelengths fits does the wave join smoothly.

What to notice: requiring $2\pi r = n\lambda = nh/mv$ gives $mvr = n\hbar$, Bohr’s quantization of angular momentum. The allowed orbits (and energies) are exactly those where the de Broglie wave forms a standing wave.

21.2Phase velocity and group velocity

Derivation

Phase velocityA single wave $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ moves at $v_p = \omega/k$. For a matter wave $E = \hbar\omega = mc^2$ and $p = \hbar k = mv$, so $v_p = c^2/v > c$. No information travels at $v_p$.

Group velocityAdding two waves with slightly different $k$ and $\omega$ gives $2\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta k}2x - \frac{\Delta\omega}2t\right)\cos(kx - \omega t)$: an envelope (the “group”) moving at $v_g = \Delta\omega/\Delta k \to d\omega/dk$.

Matter wave$v_g = \dfrac{d\omega}{dk} = \dfrac{dE}{dp}$. With $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m_0^2c^4$: $\dfrac{dE}{dp} = \dfrac{pc^2}{E} = v$. The group moves with the particle. Also $v_gv_p = c^2$.

Simulation

Phase velocity vs group velocity

Try: change the dispersion (how $\omega$ depends on $k$) and the spread $\Delta k$ between the two waves. Follow the red crest and the green envelope.

What to notice: with no dispersion both move together. With dispersion the crests slide through the envelope: they move at $v_p = \omega/k$ while the envelope (which carries the energy) moves at $v_g = d\omega/dk$. A smaller $\Delta k$ makes longer groups.

21.3X-rays: production, properties, types and spectrum

  • Production: electrons accelerated through a high voltage $V$ hit a heavy-metal target (tungsten, molybdenum, copper) in an evacuated tube.
  • Continuous spectrum (bremsstrahlung, “braking radiation”): electrons decelerate in the nuclei’s fields. Its sharp short-wavelength cut-off comes from an electron giving all its energy to one photon: $\lambda_{\min} = hc/eV$ (Duane–Hunt law).
  • Characteristic lines ($K_\alpha$, $K_\beta$): an electron knocks out an inner-shell electron; an outer electron drops in and emits a photon with energy fixed by the target element.
  • Hard X-rays (short $\lambda$, high tube voltage) penetrate deeply: radiography, CT, industrial inspection. Soft X-rays (longer $\lambda$) are absorbed easily: surface studies, mammography.
  • Properties: travel in straight lines, not deflected by fields, ionize gases, cause fluorescence, blacken film, and diffract from crystals.
Simulation

X-ray tube spectrum

Try: raise the tube voltage and change the target metal and the current.

What to notice: $\lambda_{\min} = hc/eV$ moves to shorter wavelength as $V$ rises, whatever the target. The characteristic peaks stay at fixed wavelengths for each metal and only appear once $V$ is high enough to knock out a K-shell electron. Current only scales the height.

21.4Bragg’s law and Miller indices

Derivation of Bragg’s law

Step 1X-rays reflect off successive parallel planes of atoms, spacing $d$, at glancing angle $\theta$.

Step 2The ray from the lower plane travels extra distance $d\sin\theta$ going in and $d\sin\theta$ coming out: path difference $2d\sin\theta$.

Step 3Constructive interference when the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths:

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$$
Miller indices

A crystal plane cutting the axes at $a/h$, $b/k$, $c/l$ has Miller indices $(hkl)$ (take reciprocals of the intercepts in units of the lattice constant and clear fractions). For a cubic crystal of side $a$:

$$d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}$$

Applications: finding crystal structures and lattice constants, identifying minerals and drugs (powder diffraction), measuring X-ray wavelengths, and solving the structures of DNA and proteins.

3D simulation

Miller planes and Bragg reflection

Try: set the Miller indices $h$, $k$, $l$ to light up a family of planes. Then change the glancing angle and wavelength to find the Bragg reflection for that family.

What to notice: higher indices mean more closely spaced planes, $d = a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$, which reflect at larger angles. The reflected beam is bright only when $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$.

Practice problems

  1. Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V, and of a 145 g ball at 40 m/s. easy

    Electron: $h/\sqrt{2meV} = 0.123$ nm. Ball: $h/mv = 1.1\times10^{-34}$ m.

  2. Find $\lambda_{\min}$ for an X-ray tube at 30 kV. easy

    $hc/eV = 4.14\times10^{-11}$ m $= 0.0414$ nm.

  3. X-rays of 0.154 nm reflect from planes 0.20 nm apart. Find the first-order Bragg angle. easy

    $\sin\theta = \lambda/2d = 0.385$, $\theta = 22.6^\circ$.

  4. A cubic crystal has $a = 0.40$ nm. Find $d_{111}$ and $d_{200}$. easy

    $d_{111} = 0.40/\sqrt3 = 0.231$ nm; $d_{200} = 0.40/2 = 0.20$ nm.

  5. For deep-water waves $\omega = \sqrt{gk}$. Show that $v_g = v_p/2$. medium

    $v_p = \omega/k = \sqrt{g/k}$; $v_g = d\omega/dk = \tfrac12\sqrt{g/k} = v_p/2$.

Class 22

Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, the correspondence principle, pair production and annihilation

By the end you can: state and use both forms of the uncertainty principle; explain it from wave packets; state the correspondence principle; find the threshold for pair production and the photons from annihilation.

22.1The uncertainty principle

Principles I and II
$$\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac\hbar2, \qquad \Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \frac\hbar2, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}$$
Where it comes from

Step 1A particle localised in space is a wave packet: a sum of waves with a spread of wavenumbers $\Delta k$.

Step 2To build a packet of length $\Delta x$ you need $\Delta k \gtrsim 1/\Delta x$ (a property of all waves: a short pulse needs many frequencies). Exactly, $\Delta x\,\Delta k \ge \tfrac12$.

Step 3With $p = \hbar k$: $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$. In time, a signal lasting $\Delta t$ has a spread of frequencies $\Delta\omega\ge 1/(2\Delta t)$, and $E = \hbar\omega$ gives $\Delta E\,\Delta t\ge\hbar/2$.

This is not a fault of instruments: it is built into the wave nature of matter. Consequence: electrons cannot sit inside nuclei (their confinement energy would be far too large), and excited states have a natural line width.

Simulation

Build a wave packet: Δx · Δk

Try: widen or narrow the spread of wavenumbers $\Delta k$ and the number of waves added. Watch the packet in space (top) and the spread of $k$ (bottom).

What to notice: a narrow range of $k$ (well-known momentum) gives a long packet (poorly known position), and vice versa. The product $\Delta x\,\Delta k$ never drops below $\tfrac12$.

22.2The correspondence principle

Bohr’s correspondence principle: in the limit of large quantum numbers (or when $h$ is negligible), quantum physics must reproduce classical physics. Example: for a hydrogen atom with very large $n$, the frequency of light emitted in a jump $n\to n-1$ approaches the classical orbital frequency of the electron, and energy levels become so close they look continuous. A cricket ball’s de Broglie wavelength is so small that it simply follows Newton’s laws.

22.3Pair production and pair annihilation

Key results
$$\gamma \to e^- + e^+ \quad (hf \ge 2m_ec^2 = 1.022\ \text{MeV}), \qquad e^- + e^+ \to 2\gamma\ (0.511\ \text{MeV each, back to back})$$

A high-energy photon passing close to a nucleus can turn into an electron and a positron; the nucleus takes up some momentum so both energy and momentum can be conserved (this cannot happen in empty space). Energy above the threshold becomes the pair’s kinetic energy. When a positron meets an electron they annihilate into two photons going in opposite directions (to conserve momentum). PET scanners detect exactly these back-to-back 511 keV photons.

3D simulation

Pair production and annihilation in a magnetic field

Try: set the gamma-ray energy and press Fire gamma. Then press Annihilate. Change the magnetic field (as in a bubble chamber).

What to notice: below 1.022 MeV nothing happens. Above it, the electron and positron curl in opposite directions in the field (opposite charges); extra energy makes them faster, with larger spirals. Annihilation always gives two photons flying apart in opposite directions.

Practice problems

  1. An electron is confined to $\Delta x = 0.1$ nm. Find the minimum uncertainty in its velocity. easy

    $\Delta v = \hbar/(2m\Delta x) \approx 5.8\times10^5$ m/s.

  2. An excited state lives $10^{-8}$ s. Find the minimum energy uncertainty. easy

    $\Delta E \ge \hbar/(2\Delta t) \approx 3.3\times10^{-8}$ eV.

  3. Find the threshold photon energy for pair production and the wavelength of each annihilation photon. easy

    1.022 MeV; $\lambda = hc/(0.511\ \text{MeV}) = 2.43$ pm.

  4. A 3 MeV photon produces a pair. Find the total kinetic energy shared by the electron and positron. easy

    $3 - 1.022 = 1.978$ MeV (ignoring the tiny recoil of the nucleus).
