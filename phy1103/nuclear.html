Nuclear Physics

The nucleus is tiny, dense and held together by the strong force. Its mass is less than the mass of its parts, and that missing mass is the energy that powers the Sun, nuclear reactors and radioactive decay. This unit ends with a review of the whole course.

Class 23

Properties of the nucleus, binding energy, radioactivity, decay law, chain reactions, fission and fusion

By the end you can: find the size and density of a nucleus; calculate mass defect and binding energy; read the B/A curve; derive and use the decay law, half-life and mean life; describe radioactive series and α, β, γ radiation; explain chain reactions, fission and fusion.

23.1Properties of the nucleus

Big idea

A nucleus ${}^{A}_{Z}X$ has $Z$ protons and $N = A - Z$ neutrons (together: nucleons). Isotopes have the same $Z$, different $N$. Nucleons are packed like marbles in a bag, so the volume is proportional to $A$:

$$R = R_0A^{1/3}, \qquad R_0 \approx 1.2\ \text{fm} = 1.2\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}$$

Then the density $\rho = \dfrac{Am_u}{\tfrac43\pi R_0^3A} = \dfrac{3m_u}{4\pi R_0^3} \approx 2.3\times10^{17}$ kg/m³ is the same for every nucleus (a teaspoon would weigh about a billion tonnes). The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force: very strong, attractive between all nucleons, but short range (about 2 fm). Protons also repel each other electrically; this is why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons.

3D simulation

Try: change the number of protons and neutrons, or press a nucleus button. Drag to rotate; increase the jiggle to show nucleons moving.

What to notice: the radius grows only as $A^{1/3}$ (eight times the nucleons gives twice the radius), so the density never changes. Light stable nuclei have $N \approx Z$; heavy ones need many more neutrons than protons. Too many of either makes the nucleus unstable.

23.2Mass defect and binding energy

Derivation

Step 1Measure the mass $M$ of a nucleus. It is always less than the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons. The difference is the mass defect:

$$\Delta m = Zm_p + Nm_n - M_{\text{nucleus}} \;\approx\; Zm_{\text{H}} + Nm_n - M_{\text{atom}}$$

(using atomic masses, the $Z$ electron masses cancel because $m_{\text{H}}$ includes one electron).

Step 2To pull the nucleus apart we must supply energy. By $E = mc^2$, this energy appears as the extra mass of the separated parts. So the binding energy is

$$B = \Delta m\,c^2, \qquad 1\ \text{u}\times c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$$

Step 3The useful measure of stability is the binding energy per nucleon $B/A$. It rises quickly for light nuclei, peaks at about 8.8 MeV near ${}^{56}$Fe, and falls slowly for heavy nuclei (proton repulsion grows).

Step 4Any reaction that moves nuclei towards the peak releases energy: fusion of light nuclei, or fission of heavy ones.

Simulation

Binding energy per nucleon and the mass defect calculator

Try: press a nucleus button to calculate its mass defect from its measured mass. Or drag the slider to move along the curve (calculated from the liquid-drop formula).

What to notice: ${}^4$He sits far above its neighbours (very tightly bound: this is why $\alpha$ particles are emitted as a unit). Iron is at the top. Going from uranium (7.6 MeV) to two middle nuclei (about 8.5 MeV) gains about 0.9 MeV per nucleon: roughly 200 MeV per fission.

Worked example: helium-4

$M({}^4\text{He}) = 4.002603$ u, $m_{\text{H}} = 1.007825$ u, $m_n = 1.008665$ u.

$\Delta m = 2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665) - 4.002603 = 0.030377$ u.

$B = 0.030377\times931.5 = 28.3$ MeV, so $B/A = 7.07$ MeV per nucleon.

23.3Radioactivity and the decay law

Derivation

Step 1Each unstable nucleus has the same chance $\lambda\,dt$ of decaying in the next short time $dt$, regardless of its age. $\lambda$ is the decay constant.

Step 2With $N$ nuclei, the number decaying in $dt$ is $-dN = \lambda N\,dt$, so

$$\frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; \ln\frac{N}{N_0} = -\lambda t \;\Rightarrow\; N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Step 3Activity (decays per second) $A = -\dfrac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N = A_0e^{-\lambda t}$. Unit: becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay/s); 1 curie = $3.7\times10^{10}$ Bq.

Step 4Half-life: set $N = N_0/2$: $\tfrac12 = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}$, so

$$T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{\lambda}, \qquad N = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$$

Step 5Mean life (average lifetime of a nucleus): $\tau = \dfrac{\int_0^\infty t\,\lambda N\,dt}{N_0} = \lambda\int_0^\infty te^{-\lambda t}dt = \dfrac1\lambda = 1.443\,T_{1/2}$.

Simulation

Random decay of many nuclei

Try: change the number of nuclei and the half-life, then press Restart. Compare the counted curve with the theory curve.

What to notice: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next (they flash red at random), but the total follows $N_0e^{-\lambda t}$, halving every $T_{1/2}$. With fewer nuclei the count is noisier: decay is a statistical law.

23.4Radioactive series

Parent → daughter → stable

Heavy nuclei decay in a chain (for example ${}^{238}$U → … → ${}^{206}$Pb in 14 steps). The four natural series are the thorium ($4n$), neptunium ($4n+1$), uranium ($4n+2$) and actinium ($4n+3$) series. For A → B → C:

$$\frac{dN_A}{dt} = -\lambda_AN_A, \qquad \frac{dN_B}{dt} = \lambda_AN_A - \lambda_BN_B$$

Solving with $N_B(0) = 0$:

$$N_B = N_0\frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_B-\lambda_A}\left(e^{-\lambda_At} - e^{-\lambda_Bt}\right), \qquad t_m = \frac{\ln(\lambda_B/\lambda_A)}{\lambda_B-\lambda_A}$$

If the parent lives much longer ($\lambda_A \ll \lambda_B$), the daughter reaches secular equilibrium: $\lambda_AN_A = \lambda_BN_B$ (equal activities).

Simulation

Parent, daughter and stable product

Try: change the two half-lives. Watch the bars and the moving cursor.

What to notice: the daughter builds up, reaches a maximum at $t_m$, then falls. When the parent lives much longer, the daughter follows the parent’s curve (equilibrium). The stable product C only ever grows.

23.6Nuclear fission, chain reactions and fusion

Key results

Fission: a slow neutron is absorbed by ${}^{235}$U, which splits into two medium nuclei plus 2 or 3 neutrons:

$${}^1_0n + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U}\to{}^{236}_{92}\text{U}^*\to{}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba}+{}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr}+3\,{}^1_0n + Q, \qquad Q\approx200\ \text{MeV}$$

Chain reaction: the released neutrons cause further fissions. The multiplication factor $k$ is the average number of neutrons from one fission that go on to cause another fission. After $g$ generations the number of fissions per generation is $k^g$.

  • $k < 1$: subcritical, the reaction dies out;
  • $k = 1$: critical, a steady power output (nuclear reactor: control rods absorb neutrons to keep $k=1$; a moderator slows them);
  • $k > 1$: supercritical, it grows exponentially (atomic bomb).

Fusion: light nuclei join, for example ${}^2_1\text{H}+{}^3_1\text{H}\to{}^4_2\text{He}+{}^1_0n+17.6$ MeV. It needs temperatures of about $10^7$–$10^8$ K so the nuclei can overcome their electric repulsion. Fusion powers the Sun (proton–proton chain, net $4\,{}^1\text{H}\to{}^4\text{He}+26.7$ MeV).

3D simulation

Chain reaction in a sphere of uranium

Try: set the multiplication factor $k$ and press Send one neutron. Press Reset fuel and try a different $k$. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: with $k < 1$ the chain usually stops after a few fissions. Near $k = 1$ it carries on slowly. With $k > 1$ the number of neutrons in flight grows each generation and the fuel is used up very quickly. Each orange flash releases about 200 MeV.

Worked example: energy from 1 kg of uranium-235

Number of nuclei: $N = \dfrac{1000}{235}\times6.02\times10^{23} = 2.56\times10^{24}$.

Energy: $2.56\times10^{24}\times200\ \text{MeV}\times1.6\times10^{-13}\ \text{J/MeV} = 8.2\times10^{13}$ J, about the same as burning 2700 tonnes of coal.

Practice problems

  1. Find the radius and density of a ${}^{64}$Cu nucleus. easy

    $R = 1.2\times64^{1/3} = 4.8$ fm; $\rho = 64(1.66\times10^{-27})/(\tfrac43\pi(4.8\times10^{-15})^3) \approx 2.3\times10^{17}$ kg/m³.

  2. $M({}^{56}\text{Fe}) = 55.934936$ u. Find the mass defect, binding energy and $B/A$. medium

    $\Delta m = 26(1.007825)+30(1.008665)-55.934936 = 0.528464$ u; $B = 492$ MeV; $B/A = 8.79$ MeV.

  3. A source has half-life 8 days and initial activity 800 Bq. Find $\lambda$, $\tau$ and the activity after 24 days. easy

    $\lambda = 0.693/8 = 0.0866$ day$^{-1}$; $\tau = 11.5$ days; $A = 800/2^3 = 100$ Bq.

  4. What fraction of a sample remains after 3 mean lives? easy

    $e^{-3} = 0.0498$, about 5%.

  5. Carbon-14 ($T_{1/2} = 5730$ y) in an old bone is 25% of that in living bone. How old is it? medium

    25% = two half-lives, so 11 460 years.

  6. ${}^{226}_{88}$Ra emits an $\alpha$, then the product emits a $\beta^-$. Write the final nucleus. easy

    $\alpha$: ${}^{222}_{86}$Rn; then $\beta^-$: ${}^{222}_{87}$Fr.

  7. A reactor produces 1000 MW of heat from fission of ${}^{235}$U. How many fissions per second, and how much uranium is used per day? hard

    $10^9/(200\times1.6\times10^{-13}) = 3.1\times10^{19}$ fissions/s; per day $2.7\times10^{24}$ nuclei $\approx 1.05$ kg.

  8. For D + T → He-4 + n, use $m_D = 2.014102$, $m_T = 3.016049$, $m_{He} = 4.002603$, $m_n = 1.008665$ u to find $Q$. medium

    $\Delta m = 5.030151 - 5.011268 = 0.018883$ u; $Q = 17.6$ MeV.
