Key ideas

Real oscillators lose energy to friction or resistance. With a damping force $-bv$ the amplitude decays exponentially. A spring–mass system with friction and an RLC circuit obey the same differential equation, with $m\leftrightarrow L$, $b\leftrightarrow R$, $k\leftrightarrow 1/C$ and $x\leftrightarrow q$. Here, I have defined $\dot x=\frac{dx}{dt}$.

Damped oscillator$$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
Standard form$$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\ \gamma=\frac b{2m}$$
Characteristic equation$$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0$$
Roots$$\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
Underdamped solution$$x=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega' t+\phi),\ \omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$$
LC circuit$$\frac{d^2q}{dt^2}+\frac{q}{LC}=0,\quad f=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$
Series RLC$$L\ddot q+R\dot q+\frac qC=0,\ \gamma=\frac R{2L}$$
Oscillatory if$$\frac1{LC}>\frac{R^2}{4L^2}$$
Log decrement$$\lambda=\ln\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}=\gamma T'$$
Reactance and impedance$$X_L=\omega L,\ X_C=\frac1{\omega C},\ Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}$$

Setting up the equation for a mass in water

Hang a block from a spring and let it oscillate inside a beaker of water. Its weight and the buoyant force only move the rest position a little, so we measure the displacement $x$ from that new rest position. When the block is at $x$ and moving with velocity $v=\dot x$, two forces act on it.

  1. Spring force. $F_s=-kx$. It always pulls the block back toward $x=0$.
  2. Damping force. At low speed the water pushes against the motion with a force proportional to the speed, $F_d=-bv=-b\dot x$. The constant $b$ is the damping coefficient, measured in kg/s. A thicker liquid such as oil or honey has a larger $b$.
  3. Newton's second law. $m\ddot x=-kx-b\dot x$, which gives $$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
  4. Divide by $m$. $$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad \gamma=\frac b{2m},\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac km}$$ Here $\gamma$ tells how strongly the liquid damps the motion and $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency the block would have with no damping.
  5. Same equation for a pendulum. For a bob swinging in a liquid, with small angle $\theta$, $$\ddot\theta+2\gamma\dot\theta+\frac gL\,\theta=0,\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac gL}$$ So everything below works for both systems.

Solving the damped equation with the exponential method

Exactly as for SHM, try $x=e^{\lambda t}$ in $\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$.

  1. Substitute. $(\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2)\,e^{\lambda t}=0$, so the characteristic equation is $$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
  2. Overdamped ($\gamma>\omega_0$). Two real negative roots. With $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{\beta t}+C_2e^{-\beta t}\right)$$ The body creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
  3. Critically damped ($\gamma=\omega_0$). One repeated root $\lambda=-\gamma$, $$x=(C_1+C_2t)\,e^{-\gamma t}$$ This returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without overshooting.
  4. Underdamped ($\gamma<\omega_0$). Complex roots $\lambda=-\gamma\pm i\omega'$ with $\omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{i\omega't}+C_2e^{-i\omega't}\right)=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega't+\phi)$$ The body oscillates at $\omega'$ inside a shrinking envelope $Ae^{-\gamma t}$.
  5. Check. Setting $\gamma=0$ gives back $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$, the SHM result.

AC through a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor

An AC source gives a voltage that changes like a sine wave, $v=V_0\sin\omega t$ with $\omega=2\pi f$. Below we find how the current in each part follows this voltage. The results explain every graph in the simulations that follow.

1. Resistor

Ohm's law holds at every instant, $v_R=iR$. With $v_R=V_0\sin\omega t$,

$$i=\frac{V_0}{R}\sin\omega t=I_0\sin\omega t,\qquad I_0=\frac{V_0}{R}$$

The current rises and falls exactly with the voltage, so they are in phase ($\phi=0$). R does not depend on frequency, and the power $p=i^2R$ is never negative, so the resistor always turns energy into heat.

2. Inductor, starting from the magnetic field and Lenz's law

  1. Field of the coil. A long coil with $N$ turns, length $l$ and cross-section $A$ carrying current $i$ has a field $B=\mu_0\dfrac{N}{l}\,i$ inside.
  2. Flux. The flux through one turn is $\Phi_B=BA=\dfrac{\mu_0NA}{l}\,i$. All $N$ turns are linked, so the total flux linkage is $$N\Phi_B=\frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}\,i=Li,\qquad L=\frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}$$ The self inductance $L$ depends only on the shape of the coil.
  3. Faraday's law with Lenz's rule. When the current changes, the flux changes and an EMF is induced in the coil itself, $$\varepsilon=-\frac{d(N\Phi_B)}{dt}=-L\frac{di}{dt}$$ The minus sign is Lenz's law. The induced EMF always opposes the change that causes it, so it pushes back when $i$ rises and pushes forward when $i$ falls. This is the back EMF.
  4. Voltage across the inductor. To keep the current changing, the source must supply a voltage equal and opposite to the back EMF, $$v_L=L\frac{di}{dt}$$
  5. With an AC current. Put $i=I_0\sin\omega t$, $$v_L=L\frac{d}{dt}\left(I_0\sin\omega t\right)=\omega LI_0\cos\omega t=\omega LI_0\sin\left(\omega t+90^\circ\right)$$ So the voltage reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the current. The current lags the voltage by $90^\circ$.
  6. Inductive reactance. The peaks are related by $V_0=I_0X_L$ with $$X_L=\omega L=2\pi fL$$ A higher frequency means the current must change faster, so the back EMF is bigger and less current flows.

Look at the graph. $v_L$ is largest when $i$ crosses zero, because that is where $i$ changes fastest. $v_L$ is zero when $i$ is at its peak, because there $i$ is momentarily not changing.

3. Capacitor

The charge on the plates follows the voltage, $q=Cv_C$. The current is the rate at which charge arrives, $$i=\frac{dq}{dt}=C\frac{dv_C}{dt}$$ With $v_C=V_0\sin\omega t$, $$i=\omega CV_0\cos\omega t=I_0\sin\left(\omega t+90^\circ\right),\qquad I_0=\omega CV_0=\frac{V_0}{X_C},\quad X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}$$ The current reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the voltage, so it leads by $90^\circ$. No charge crosses the gap. Electrons simply pile up on one plate and then the other. At high frequency the plates never have time to fill, so the capacitor passes current easily and $X_C$ is small.

4. Two or three parts in series

In a series circuit the same current $i=I_0\sin\omega t$ flows through every part. From the results above,

$$v_R=I_0R\sin\omega t,\qquad v_L=I_0X_L\cos\omega t,\qquad v_C=-I_0X_C\cos\omega t$$

Adding them gives the source voltage, $$v=I_0\left[R\sin\omega t+(X_L-X_C)\cos\omega t\right]$$ Write $R=Z\cos\phi$ and $X_L-X_C=Z\sin\phi$. Then $\sin\omega t\cos\phi+\cos\omega t\sin\phi=\sin(\omega t+\phi)$, so $$v=I_0Z\sin(\omega t+\phi),\qquad Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2},\qquad \tan\phi=\frac{X_L-X_C}{R}$$ $v_L$ and $v_C$ are always exactly opposite, so they partly cancel. If $X_L>X_C$ the circuit acts like an inductor and the current lags. If $X_C>X_L$ it acts like a capacitor and the current leads. They cancel completely when $\omega L=1/\omega C$, at the resonant frequency $f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$. There $Z=R$ and the current is largest.

Worked examples

Example 1

A 1.0 µF capacitor, a 0.2 H inductor and an 800 Ω resistor are in series. Is the discharge oscillatory? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=5.0\times10^6$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=4.0\times10^6$ s⁻². Since $1/LC$ is larger, it oscillates.

$\omega'=\sqrt{5.0\times10^6-4.0\times10^6}=1000$ rad/s, so $f=159$ Hz.

Example 2

Is the discharge of a capacitor oscillatory for $C=0.1$ µF, $L=10$ mH, $R=200$ Ω? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=10^9$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=10^8$ s⁻². Oscillatory. $\omega'=\sqrt{9\times10^8}=3.0\times10^4$ rad/s, $f=4.77$ kHz.

Example 3

A series circuit has $R=100$ Ω, $L=0.5$ H, $C=10$ µF on a 220 V, 50 Hz supply. Find $X_L$, $X_C$, $Z$, the current and the phase angle.

Show solution

$X_L=2\pi(50)(0.5)=157$ Ω, $X_C=1/(2\pi\cdot50\cdot10^{-5})=318$ Ω.

$Z=\sqrt{100^2+(157-318)^2}=190$ Ω, $I=220/190=1.16$ A.

$\tan\phi=(X_L-X_C)/R$ gives $\phi=-58^\circ$. The current leads the voltage (capacitive circuit).

  1. A spring is suspended vertically in air, and a mass of $2.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ is attached to its free end. The spring undergoes an extension of $0.50\,\mathrm{m}$. The same spring--mass system is then completely immersed in a jar of water, displaced slightly from its new equilibrium position, and released. The damping coefficient of the system in water is $25\,\mathrm{kg/s}$.
    Answer
    Overdamped.
  2. The amplitude of a damped oscillator falls from 10 cm to 8 cm in one period of 2 s. Find the log decrement and $\gamma$.
    Answer
    λ = 0.223, γ = 0.112 s⁻¹
  3. For a series RLC with $L=10$ mH and $C=1$ µF, what resistance gives critical damping?
    Answer
    R = 2√(L/C) = 200 Ω
  4. An LC circuit has $L=2$ mH and $C=5$ nF. Find its natural frequency.
    Answer
    50.3 kHz
