Damped Harmonic Motion and the RLC Circuit

  • Damping force −bv
  • Differential equation of DHM
  • Under, critical and over damping
  • Logarithmic decrement, damping coefficient
  • LC and series RLC circuits
  • Reactance and impedance

Key ideas

Real oscillators lose energy to friction or resistance. With a damping force $-bv$ the amplitude decays exponentially. A spring–mass system with friction and an RLC circuit obey the same differential equation, with $m\leftrightarrow L$, $b\leftrightarrow R$, $k\leftrightarrow 1/C$ and $x\leftrightarrow q$. Here, $$\ddot x = \frac{dx/dt}$$

Damped oscillator$$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
Standard form$$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\ \gamma=\frac b{2m}$$
Characteristic equation$$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0$$
Roots$$\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
Underdamped solution$$x=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega' t+\phi),\ \omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$$
LC circuit$$\frac{d^2q}{dt^2}+\frac{q}{LC}=0,\quad f=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$
Series RLC$$L\ddot q+R\dot q+\frac qC=0,\ \gamma=\frac R{2L}$$
Oscillatory if$$\frac1{LC}>\frac{R^2}{4L^2}$$
Log decrement$$\lambda=\ln\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}=\gamma T'$$
Reactance and impedance$$X_L=\omega L,\ X_C=\frac1{\omega C},\ Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}$$

Setting up the equation for a mass in water

Hang a block from a spring and let it oscillate inside a beaker of water. Its weight and the buoyant force only move the rest position a little, so we measure the displacement $x$ from that new rest position. When the block is at $x$ and moving with velocity $v=\dot x$, two forces act on it.

  1. Spring force. $F_s=-kx$. It always pulls the block back toward $x=0$.
  2. Damping force. At low speed the water pushes against the motion with a force proportional to the speed, $F_d=-bv=-b\dot x$. The constant $b$ is the damping coefficient, measured in kg/s. A thicker liquid such as oil or honey has a larger $b$.
  3. Newton's second law. $m\ddot x=-kx-b\dot x$, which gives $$m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$$
  4. Divide by $m$. $$\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad \gamma=\frac b{2m},\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac km}$$ Here $\gamma$ tells how strongly the liquid damps the motion and $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency the block would have with no damping.
  5. Same equation for a pendulum. For a bob swinging in a liquid, with small angle $\theta$, $$\ddot\theta+2\gamma\dot\theta+\frac gL\,\theta=0,\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac gL}$$ So everything below works for both systems.

Solving the damped equation with the exponential method

Exactly as for SHM, try $x=e^{\lambda t}$ in $\ddot x+2\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=0$.

  1. Substitute. $(\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2)\,e^{\lambda t}=0$, so the characteristic equation is $$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
  2. Overdamped ($\gamma>\omega_0$). Two real negative roots. With $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{\beta t}+C_2e^{-\beta t}\right)$$ The body creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
  3. Critically damped ($\gamma=\omega_0$). One repeated root $\lambda=-\gamma$, $$x=(C_1+C_2t)\,e^{-\gamma t}$$ This returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without overshooting.
  4. Underdamped ($\gamma<\omega_0$). Complex roots $\lambda=-\gamma\pm i\omega'$ with $\omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{i\omega't}+C_2e^{-i\omega't}\right)=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega't+\phi)$$ The body oscillates at $\omega'$ inside a shrinking envelope $Ae^{-\gamma t}$.
  5. Check. Setting $\gamma=0$ gives back $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$, the SHM result.

Worked examples

Example 1

A 1.0 µF capacitor, a 0.2 H inductor and an 800 Ω resistor are in series. Is the discharge oscillatory? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=5.0\times10^6$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=4.0\times10^6$ s⁻². Since $1/LC$ is larger, it oscillates.

$\omega'=\sqrt{5.0\times10^6-4.0\times10^6}=1000$ rad/s, so $f=159$ Hz.

Example 2

Is the discharge of a capacitor oscillatory for $C=0.1$ µF, $L=10$ mH, $R=200$ Ω? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=10^9$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=10^8$ s⁻². Oscillatory. $\omega'=\sqrt{9\times10^8}=3.0\times10^4$ rad/s, $f=4.77$ kHz.

Example 3

A series circuit has $R=100$ Ω, $L=0.5$ H, $C=10$ µF on a 220 V, 50 Hz supply. Find $X_L$, $X_C$, $Z$, the current and the phase angle.

Show solution

$X_L=2\pi(50)(0.5)=157$ Ω, $X_C=1/(2\pi\cdot50\cdot10^{-5})=318$ Ω.

$Z=\sqrt{100^2+(157-318)^2}=190$ Ω, $I=220/190=1.16$ A.

$\tan\phi=(X_L-X_C)/R$ gives $\phi=-58^\circ$. The current leads the voltage (capacitive circuit).

Practice

  1. The amplitude of a damped oscillator falls from 10 cm to 8 cm in one period of 2 s. Find the log decrement and $\gamma$.
    Answer
    λ = 0.223, γ = 0.112 s⁻¹
  2. For a series RLC with $L=10$ mH and $C=1$ µF, what resistance gives critical damping?
    Answer
    R = 2√(L/C) = 200 Ω
  3. An LC circuit has $L=2$ mH and $C=5$ nF. Find its natural frequency.
    Answer
    50.3 kHz
  4. Sketch $x(t)$ for under, critical and over damping on the same axes and explain which one a car shock absorber should use.
