Key ideas

Real oscillators lose energy to friction or resistance. With a damping force $-bv$ the amplitude decays exponentially. A spring–mass system with friction and an RLC circuit obey the same differential equation, with $m\leftrightarrow L$, $b\leftrightarrow R$, $k\leftrightarrow 1/C$ and $x\leftrightarrow q$.

Damped oscillator$$m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+b\dfrac{dx}{dt}+kx=0$$
Standard form$$\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+2\gamma\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\omega_0^2x=0,\ \gamma=\frac b{2m}$$
Characteristic equation$$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0$$
Roots$$\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
Underdamped solution$$x=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t+\phi),\ \omega'=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$$
LC circuit$$\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2}+\frac{q}{LC}=0,\quad f=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$
Series RLC$$L\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2}+R\dfrac{dq}{dt}+\frac qC=0,\ \gamma=\frac R{2L}$$
Oscillatory if$$\frac1{LC}>\frac{R^2}{4L^2}$$
Log decrement$$\lambda=\ln\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}=\gamma T'$$
Reactance and impedance$$X_L=\omega L,\ X_C=\frac1{\omega C},\ Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2}$$

Setting up the equation for a mass in water

Hang a block from a spring and let it oscillate inside a beaker of water. Its weight and the buoyant force only move the rest position a little, so we measure the displacement $x$ from that new rest position. When the block is at $x$ and moving with velocity $v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, two forces act on it.

  1. Spring force. $F_s=-kx$. It always pulls the block back toward $x=0$.
  2. Damping force. At low speed the water pushes against the motion with a force proportional to the speed, $F_d=-bv=-b\dfrac{dx}{dt}$. The constant $b$ is the damping coefficient, measured in kg/s. A thicker liquid such as oil or honey has a larger $b$.
  3. Newton's second law. $m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx-b\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, which gives $$m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+b\dfrac{dx}{dt}+kx=0$$
  4. Divide by $m$. $$\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+2\gamma\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\omega_0^2x=0,\qquad \gamma=\frac b{2m},\quad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac km}$$ Here $\gamma$ tells how strongly the liquid damps the motion and $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency the block would have with no damping.
  5. Same equation for a pendulum. For a bob swinging in a liquid, with small angle $\theta$, $$\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+2\gamma\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}+\frac gL\,\theta=0,\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac gL}$$ So everything below works for both systems.

Solving the damped equation with the exponential method

Exactly as for SHM, try $x=e^{\lambda t}$ in $\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+2\gamma\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\omega_0^2x=0$.

  1. Substitute. $(\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2)\,e^{\lambda t}=0$, so the characteristic equation is $$\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+\omega_0^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$$
  2. Overdamped ($\gamma>\omega_0$). Two real negative roots. With $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{\beta t}+C_2e^{-\beta t}\right)$$ The body creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating.
  3. Critically damped ($\gamma=\omega_0$). One repeated root $\lambda=-\gamma$, $$x=(C_1+C_2t)\,e^{-\gamma t}$$ This returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without overshooting.
  4. Underdamped ($\gamma<\omega_0$). Complex roots $\lambda=-\gamma\pm i\omega'$ with $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$, $$x=e^{-\gamma t}\left(C_1e^{i\omega_dt}+C_2e^{-i\omega_dt}\right)=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_dt+\phi)$$ The body oscillates at $\omega_d$ ( or with time period $T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_d})$ inside a shrinking envelope $Ae^{-\gamma t}$.
  5. Check. Setting $\gamma=0$ gives back $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$, the SHM result.
  6. Lifetime of DHM ($\tau$): It is defined as the time required for the amplitude of oscillation to decrease $\frac{1}{e}$ times its initial amplitude. Mathematically, $$A(\tau) = \frac{A_0}{e}\\ \Rightarrow A_0e^{-\gamma\tau} = \frac{A_0}{e}\\ \Rightarrow e^{-\gamma\tau} = e^{-1}\\ \Rightarrow -\gamma\tau = -1\\ \Rightarrow \tau = \frac{1}{\gamma}\\ \therefore \boxed{\tau = \frac{2m}{b}}$$
  7. Half-life of DHM ($t_{1/2}$): It is defined as the time required for the amplitude of oscillation to fall to half of its initial amplitude. Mathematically, $$A(t_{1/2}) = \frac{A_0}{2}\\ \Rightarrow A_0e^{-\gamma t_{1/2}} = \frac{A_0}{2}\\ \Rightarrow e^{-\gamma t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2}\\ \Rightarrow -\gamma t_{1/2} = -\ln 2\\ \Rightarrow t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\gamma}\\ \therefore \boxed{t_{1/2} = \frac{2m\ln 2}{b}}$$ So the amplitude halves in a little less than one lifetime. After every further $t_{1/2}$ it halves again, $A_0 \to \frac{A_0}{2} \to \frac{A_0}{4} \to \frac{A_0}{8}\dots$ The energy goes as $A^2$, so it falls to half in $\frac{t_{1/2}}{2} = \frac{m\ln 2}{b}$.

The LC circuit is a spring and a pendulum in disguise

Compare the three equations. The LC circuit obeys $L\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2}+\dfrac{q}{C}=0$, a spring obeys $m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+kx=0$ and a pendulum at small angles obeys $m\ell\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+mg\theta=0$. They have exactly the same form, so they behave in exactly the same way. Charge on the capacitor behaves like the stretch of the spring or the angle of the pendulum, and current behaves like velocity. The inductor fights any change in current just as mass fights any change in velocity, so $L$ acts like $m$. A small capacitor is a stiff spring, so $1/C$ acts like $k$.

AC through a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor (LRC circuits)

How is AC voltage generated? 
Ac is generated through Faraday's law, which states that a changing magnetic flux induces an EMF. So, in a generator we can rotate a coil of conducting write in a magnetic field and generate time dependent voltage. Simulation below illustrates this phenomena: 


An AC generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy through electromagnetic induction. When a coil rotates inside a magnetic field, the changing magnetic flux induces an alternating electromotive force (EMF).

Why do we even need AC voltages? 

We primarily need AC voltage to transmit signals over large distances. Imagine a power station generating electricity $200$ km away from your home. We need to: 
Generate electrical energy $\Longrightarrow$  Transport over long distances $\Longrightarrow$ Deliver to home safely with the voltage the appliances require. Now, we know that, $$P=VI\Rightarrow I=\frac{P}{V}$$ So larger the voltage higher the current and since $P_{loss} = I^2R$ the loss is large as well (wasted as heat). Therefore, we need some device that will raise/lower the voltage accordingly. This device is known as a transformer, which can either increase (step up transformer) or decrease voltage (step down transformer) when needed. However, transformers need AC voltage because of electromagnetic induction (Again Faraday's law, $\epsilon = -N\frac{d\phi_B}{dt}$).  

Consequently, an AC source gives a voltage that changes like a sine wave, $v=V_0\sin\omega t$ with $\omega=2\pi f$. Below we find how the current in each part follows this voltage. The results explain every graph in the simulations that follow.

1. Resistor

Ohm's law holds at every instant, $v_R=iR$. With $v_R=V_0\sin\omega t$,

$$i=\frac{V_0}{R}\sin\omega t=I_0\sin\omega t,\qquad I_0=\frac{V_0}{R}$$

The current rises and falls exactly with the voltage, so they are in phase ($\phi=0$). R does not depend on frequency, and the power $p=i^2R$ is never negative, so the resistor always turns energy into heat.

2. Inductor, starting from the magnetic field and Lenz's law

  1. Field of the coil. A long coil with $N$ turns, length $l$ and cross-section $A$ carrying current $i$ has a field $B=\mu_0\dfrac{N}{l}\,i$ inside.
  2. Flux. The flux through one turn is $\Phi_B=BA=\dfrac{\mu_0NA}{l}\,i$. All $N$ turns are linked, so the total flux linkage is $$N\Phi_B=\frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}\,i=Li,\qquad L=\frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}$$ The self inductance $L$ depends only on the shape of the coil.
  3. Faraday's law with Lenz's rule. When the current changes, the flux changes and an EMF is induced in the coil itself, $$\varepsilon=-\frac{d(N\Phi_B)}{dt}=-L\dfrac{di}{dt}$$ The minus sign is Lenz's law. The induced EMF always opposes the change that causes it, so it pushes back when $i$ rises and pushes forward when $i$ falls. This is the back EMF.
  4. Voltage across the inductor. To keep the current changing, the source must supply a voltage equal and opposite to the back EMF, $$v_L=L\dfrac{di}{dt}$$
  5. With an AC current. Put $i=I_0\sin\omega t$, $$v_L=L\frac{d}{dt}\left(I_0\sin\omega t\right)=\omega LI_0\cos\omega t=\omega LI_0\sin\left(\omega t+90^\circ\right)$$ So the voltage reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the current. The current lags the voltage by $90^\circ$.
  6. Inductive reactance. We can define reactance in a similar way to resistance in Ohm’s law. By comparing the voltage-current relationship (V=IR) with the voltage-current relationship for an inductor, we obtain an analogous quantity called inductive reactance, given by:$$X_L=\omega L=2\pi fL$$Thus, inductive reactance plays a role similar to resistance in an AC circuit and is also measured in ohms ((\Omega)). A higher frequency means the current must change faster, so the back EMF is bigger and less current flows.

Look at the graph. $v_L$ is largest when $i$ crosses zero, because that is where $i$ changes fastest. $v_L$ is zero when $i$ is at its peak, because there $i$ is momentarily not changing.

3. Capacitor

The charge on the plates follows the voltage, $q=Cv_C$. The current is the rate at which charge arrives, $$i=\dfrac{dq}{dt}=C\frac{dv_C}{dt}$$ With $v_C=V_0\sin\omega t$, $$i=\omega CV_0\cos\omega t=I_0\sin\left(\omega t+90^\circ\right),\qquad I_0=\omega CV_0=\frac{V_0}{X_C},\quad X_C=\frac{1}{\omega C}$$ The current reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the voltage, so it leads by $90^\circ$. No charge crosses the gap. Electrons simply pile up on one plate and then the other. At high frequency the plates never have time to fill, so the capacitor passes current easily and $X_C$ is small.

4. Two or three parts in series

In a series circuit the same current $i=I_0\sin\omega t$ flows through every part. From the results above,

$$v_R=I_0R\sin\omega t,\qquad v_L=I_0X_L\cos\omega t,\qquad v_C=-I_0X_C\cos\omega t$$

Adding them gives the source voltage, $$v=I_0\left[R\sin\omega t+(X_L-X_C)\cos\omega t\right]$$ Write $R=Z\cos\phi$ and $X_L-X_C=Z\sin\phi$. Then $\sin\omega t\cos\phi+\cos\omega t\sin\phi=\sin(\omega t+\phi)$, so $$v=I_0Z\sin(\omega t+\phi),\qquad Z=\sqrt{R^2+(X_L-X_C)^2},\qquad \tan\phi=\frac{X_L-X_C}{R}$$ $v_L$ and $v_C$ are always exactly opposite, so they partly cancel. If $X_L>X_C$ the circuit acts like an inductor and the current lags. If $X_C>X_L$ it acts like a capacitor and the current leads. They cancel completely when $\omega L=1/\omega C$, at the resonant frequency $f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$. There $Z=R$ and the current is largest.

Worked examples

Example 1

A 1.0 µF capacitor, a 0.2 H inductor and an 800 Ω resistor are in series. Is the discharge oscillatory? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=5.0\times10^6$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=4.0\times10^6$ s⁻². Since $1/LC$ is larger, it oscillates.

$\omega'=\sqrt{5.0\times10^6-4.0\times10^6}=1000$ rad/s, so $f=159$ Hz.

Example 2

Is the discharge of a capacitor oscillatory for $C=0.1$ µF, $L=10$ mH, $R=200$ Ω? If so find the frequency.

Show solution

$1/LC=10^9$ s⁻² and $R^2/4L^2=10^8$ s⁻². Oscillatory. $\omega'=\sqrt{9\times10^8}=3.0\times10^4$ rad/s, $f=4.77$ kHz.

Example 3

A series circuit has $R=100$ Ω, $L=0.5$ H, $C=10$ µF on a 220 V, 50 Hz supply. Find $X_L$, $X_C$, $Z$, the current and the phase angle.

Show solution

$X_L=2\pi(50)(0.5)=157$ Ω, $X_C=1/(2\pi\cdot50\cdot10^{-5})=318$ Ω.

$Z=\sqrt{100^2+(157-318)^2}=190$ Ω, $I=220/190=1.16$ A.

$\tan\phi=(X_L-X_C)/R$ gives $\phi=-58^\circ$. The current leads the voltage (capacitive circuit).
