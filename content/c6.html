Key ideas

A periodic driving force makes the system oscillate at the driving frequency, not its own. The amplitude becomes very large when the driving frequency is close to the natural frequency. This is resonance. It tunes a radio and it also brought down the Tacoma Narrows bridge.

Forced oscillation in everyday words

Pull a swing back and let go. It swings at its own natural frequency and slowly dies away because of air friction. That is the damped motion of the last class. Now keep giving it a push, again and again, at some steady rate. The swing is now being forced (or driven). Many things in daily life are driven like this.

  • A parent pushing a child on a swing.
  • Footsteps on a footbridge, or soldiers marching across it.
  • A washing machine drum spinning and shaking its body.
  • A singer's note hitting a wine glass.
  • Ground shaking a building during an earthquake.
  • Radio waves pushing the electrons in an aerial.

Three words do all the work.

  1. Natural frequency $\omega_0$. The frequency the system likes to oscillate at by itself, $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ for a spring.
  2. Driving frequency $\omega$. How often the outside force repeats. We choose it.
  3. Resonance. When $\omega$ is close to $\omega_0$ even a small force builds up a very large oscillation.

What happens when the force starts? For the first few cycles the motion is untidy, because the system's own natural swing is mixed in. This part is called the transient and damping kills it. After that the system settles into the steady state. It then oscillates at the driver's frequency $\omega$, not its own, with a fixed amplitude that depends on how close $\omega$ is to $\omega_0$ and on how much damping there is.

Setting up the equation of motion

Take a block of mass $m$ on a smooth floor, joined to a wall by a spring (constant $k$) and a damper (constant $b$), and pushed by a force $F(t)=F_0\cos\omega t$. When the block is at $x$ with velocity $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ three forces act on it.

  1. Spring force $-kx$, pulling back towards $x=0$.
  2. Damping force $-b\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, against the velocity.
  3. Driving force $F_0\cos\omega t$.

Newton's second law gives $m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-kx-b\dfrac{dx}{dt}+F_0\cos\omega t$. Divide by $m$ and use $\omega_0^2=k/m$ and $2\gamma=b/m$,

$$\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}+2\gamma\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\omega_0^2x=\frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t$$

This is the differential equation of forced harmonic motion (FHM). Without the right-hand side it is exactly the damped equation, and with $\gamma=0$ as well it is SHM.

Solving the equation step by step

Step 1. Two parts to the solution

The full solution is a sum of two parts, $x(t)=x_{\text{tr}}(t)+x_{\text{ss}}(t)$.

  • The transient $x_{\text{tr}}$ solves the equation with the right side set to zero. It is the damped motion we already know, for example $Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega' t+\phi)$. Because of $e^{-\gamma t}$ it dies away.
  • The steady state $x_{\text{ss}}$ is one particular solution that keeps going as long as the force does. After a few multiples of $1/\gamma$ it is all that is left.

Step 2. Guess the steady state

A force that repeats at $\omega$ should give motion that repeats at $\omega$, possibly delayed. So try

$$x=A\cos(\omega t-\delta)$$

where $A$ is the steady amplitude and $\delta$ is how far the motion lags behind the force. We must find $A$ and $\delta$. Write $\theta=\omega t-\delta$ for short. Then

$$\dfrac{dx}{dt}=-A\omega\sin\theta,\qquad \dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-A\omega^2\cos\theta$$

Step 3. Substitute

The left side becomes $A(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\cos\theta-2\gamma\omega A\sin\theta$. On the right write $\omega t=\theta+\delta$ and expand,

$$\frac{F_0}{m}\cos(\theta+\delta)=\frac{F_0}{m}\left(\cos\theta\cos\delta-\sin\theta\sin\delta\right)$$

Step 4. Match the $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ parts

The equation must hold at every instant, so the coefficients of $\cos\theta$ and of $\sin\theta$ must match separately,

$$A(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)=\frac{F_0}{m}\cos\delta,\qquad 2\gamma\omega A=\frac{F_0}{m}\sin\delta$$

Step 5. Amplitude and phase

Square both and add them. Since $\cos^2\delta+\sin^2\delta=1$,

$$A=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}}$$

Divide the second by the first,

$$\tan\delta=\frac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}$$

So the complete motion is $x(t)=(\text{transient})+A\cos(\omega t-\delta)$, and after a while only the second term remains.

Resonance

Amplitude resonance

$A$ is largest when the bottom of the fraction, $D=(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2$, is smallest. Differentiate with respect to $\omega$ and set it to zero,

$$\frac{dD}{d\omega}=-4\omega(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)+8\gamma^2\omega=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\gamma^2}$$

Putting this back gives the peak amplitude $A_{\max}=\dfrac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}}$. For light damping $\omega_r\approx\omega_0$ and $A_{\max}\approx\dfrac{F_0}{2m\gamma\omega_0}$, which grows without limit as $\gamma\to0$.

Why the force wins at resonance

At $\omega=\omega_0$ the lag is $\delta=90^\circ$. The velocity $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ is a quarter cycle ahead of $x$, so it is exactly in step with the force. The force always pushes in the direction the body is already moving, like pushing a swing just as it starts forward, so it does positive work in every part of every cycle. At other frequencies the force spends part of each cycle pushing against the motion.

Power absorbed and the quality factor

The average power fed in by the driver is $\bar P=\tfrac12F_0\,\omega A\sin\delta$. Using the results above,

$$\bar P=\frac{F_0^2}{m}\,\frac{\gamma\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}$$

The sharpness of the amplitude resonance curve is measured by the quality factor,

$$Q=\frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma}$$

A high $Q$ means a tall, narrow resonance peak. 

Resonance in real life

Resonance is sometimes wanted and sometimes dangerous. Try each example.

Pushing a swing

Small pushes timed with the swing's natural frequency add energy every cycle. Push at the wrong rate and some pushes cancel others.

Try it with a volunteer who did not read the safety notice. Start with the ratio $f/f_0$ far from 1, then slowly move it towards 1.

Barton's pendulums

Tuning a radio

Every station broadcasts at its own frequency. The radio's LC circuit has a resonant frequency $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. Turning the dial changes $C$ so that only the wanted station is in resonance and is picked up strongly. MRI scanners, quartz watch crystals and musical instruments also rely on resonance.

Breaking a wine glass with sound

Bridges and marching soldiers

In 1831 the Broughton Suspension Bridge in England collapsed under soldiers marching in step, and soldiers have been ordered to break step on bridges ever since. London's Millennium Bridge swayed sideways on its opening day in 2000 because people walking on it fell into step with its sway. (The famous 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse was caused by wind-driven flutter, which is related but is not simple forced resonance.)

Buildings and earthquakes

Engineers keep a building's natural frequency away from that of likely ground shaking and add damping. Some towers, such as Taipei 101, hang a huge tuned mass damper near the top.

Worked examples

Example 1

Find $Q$ and the resonant frequency of the circuit $R=100$ Ω, $L=0.5$ H, $C=10$ µF.

Show solution

$f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=71.2$ Hz and $Q=\frac1R\sqrt{L/C}=\frac{1}{100}\sqrt{5\times10^4}=2.24$.

Example 2

The CO molecule vibrates like two masses (12 u and 16 u) joined by a spring with $k=1860$ N/m. Find its vibration frequency.

Show solution

$\mu=\frac{12\times16}{28}$ u $=6.86$ u $=1.14\times10^{-26}$ kg. $f=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/\mu}=6.4\times10^{13}$ Hz, in the infrared.
