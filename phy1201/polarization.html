Polarization of Light

Polarization shows that light is a transverse wave. Polarizing filters cut glare from water and roads, photoelastic models reveal stress patterns in structural members, and polarimeters measure sugar concentration. This unit covers how polarized light is produced, analysed and changed.

Class 19

Polarization: plane, circular and elliptical light

By the end you can: explain unpolarized and polarized light; distinguish the plane of vibration and the plane of polarization; derive the conditions for plane, circular and elliptical polarization; list methods of producing polarized light and its uses.

19.1What polarization means

Big idea

In light, the electric field $\vec E$ oscillates at right angles to the direction of travel. Ordinary light (sun, lamp) is unpolarized: $\vec E$ points in random, rapidly changing directions in that plane. If $\vec E$ oscillates in a single fixed direction, the light is plane (linearly) polarized. The plane containing $\vec E$ and the direction of travel is the plane of vibration; the plane perpendicular to it (containing $\vec B$) is traditionally called the plane of polarization.

Only transverse waves can be polarized; sound in air (longitudinal) cannot. Methods: reflection, refraction through a pile of plates, double refraction (Nicol prism), selective absorption (Polaroid, tourmaline), and scattering (blue sky). Uses: glare-free sunglasses, LCD screens, 3D cinema, photoelastic stress analysis of structures, polarimeters, microscopy of rocks and concrete aggregates.

19.2Plane, circular and elliptical polarization

Derivation

Step 1Any polarized wave can be written as two perpendicular components with a phase difference $\delta$: $E_x = a\sin\omega t$, $E_y = b\sin(\omega t + \delta)$.

Step 2Eliminating $t$ (as for perpendicular SHMs) gives the path of the tip of $\vec E$:

$$\frac{E_x^2}{a^2} + \frac{E_y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2E_xE_y}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$$

Plane$\delta = 0$ or $\pi$: a straight line, $E_y = \pm\dfrac baE_x$.

Circular$\delta = \pi/2$ and $a = b$: $E_x^2 + E_y^2 = a^2$; the tip of $\vec E$ turns in a circle (right- or left-handed depending on the sign of $\delta$).

Ellipticalany other case: the tip traces an ellipse. Plane and circular light are special cases of elliptical light.

3D simulation

Plane, circular and elliptical light

Try: press the three buttons, then change the amplitudes and phase difference yourself. Drag to rotate and look along the beam.

What to notice: with $\delta = 0$ the arrows all lie in one plane. With equal amplitudes and $\delta = 90^\circ$ the arrow tips form a helix and the face-on view is a circle. Anything else gives an ellipse.

19.3Polarization by reflection

When unpolarized light is reflected from glass or water, the reflected light is partly polarized with $\vec E$ mainly parallel to the surface (that is why sunglasses with vertical transmission axes cut glare from roads and lakes). At one special angle, the polarizing angle, the reflected light is completely plane polarized (Class 21).

Class 20

Malus’ law and double refraction

By the end you can: state and derive Malus’ law and calculate intensities and angles; describe double refraction in calcite and the properties of the O-ray and E-ray.

20.1Malus’ law

Statement and derivation

Step 1Plane-polarized light of amplitude $E_0$ meets an analyzer whose axis makes angle $\theta$ with the plane of vibration. Resolve $E_0$ along and across the axis: $E_0\cos\theta$ passes, $E_0\sin\theta$ is absorbed.

Step 2Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared:

$$I = I_0\cos^2\theta$$

For unpolarized light the average of $\cos^2\theta$ over all directions is $\tfrac12$, so an ideal polarizer passes $I_0/2$. Crossed polarizers ($\theta = 90^\circ$) pass nothing, but a third sheet between them at 45° lets $I_0/8$ through.

3D simulation

Polarizer and analyzer: Malus’ law

Try: turn the analyzer from 0° to 90°. Press Cross them, then Insert a middle polarizer and turn it. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: the first sheet always halves unpolarized light. The analyzer then passes $\cos^2\theta$ of it: 75% at 30°, 25% at 60°, 0 at 90°. Surprisingly, adding a third sheet between crossed polarizers lets light through (12.5% at 45°).

20.2Double refraction

Key ideas

In some crystals (calcite, quartz) a single ray splits into two: the ordinary (O) ray, which obeys Snell’s law with a fixed index $\mu_o$, and the extraordinary (E) ray, whose speed and index $\mu_e$ depend on direction, and which in general does not obey Snell’s law. Looking through calcite you see two images; turning the crystal makes the E image circle around the O image.

  • Both rays are plane polarized, at right angles to each other: the O-ray perpendicular to the principal section, the E-ray in it.
  • Along the optic axis both travel at the same speed and there is no double refraction.
  • Calcite is negative ($\mu_e < \mu_o$: 1.486 vs 1.658), quartz is positive ($\mu_e > \mu_o$).
  • The principal section is any plane containing the optic axis and the normal to a face.
Simulation

Double refraction in calcite

Try: rotate the crystal and change its thickness. Switch on the analyzer and turn it.

What to notice: the O image stays put while the E image goes around it; a thicker crystal separates them more. Through an analyzer the two images brighten and fade alternately: they are polarized at right angles to each other.

Practice problems

  1. Unpolarized light of 40 W/m² passes two polarizers at 30°. Find the transmitted intensity. easy

    $20\cos^230^\circ = 15$ W/m².

  2. At what angle between polarizer and analyzer is the transmitted intensity reduced to 1/4 of the polarized intensity? easy

    $\cos^2\theta = 1/4$, $\theta = 60^\circ$.

  3. Three polarizers at 0°, 45° and 90° with unpolarized light $I_0$. Find the final intensity. medium

    $\tfrac12I_0\cos^245^\circ\cos^245^\circ = I_0/8$.

Class 21

Brewster’s law, retardation plates, Nicol prism, optical activity and the polarimeter

By the end you can: derive Brewster’s law; explain the Brewster window; derive the thickness of quarter- and half-wave plates; describe the Nicol prism as polarizer and analyzer; define specific rotation and use a polarimeter.

21.1Brewster’s law

Derivation

Step 1Brewster found that the reflected light is completely polarized when the reflected and refracted rays are at right angles: $i_p + r = 90^\circ$. (Physically: the electrons in the glass oscillate along the refracted $\vec E$; for the p-component that direction points along the reflected ray, and an oscillating charge does not radiate along its own line of motion.)

Step 2Snell: $\sin i_p = \mu\sin r = \mu\sin(90^\circ - i_p) = \mu\cos i_p$, so

$$\tan i_p = \mu$$

For glass ($\mu = 1.5$) $i_p = 56.3^\circ$; for water 53.1°. A Brewster window (a plate tilted at $i_p$) transmits p-polarized light with no reflection loss; it is used on the ends of laser tubes, making the laser output polarized.

Simulation

Reflection and Brewster’s angle

Try: sweep the angle of incidence and press Go to Brewster angle. Change the refractive index.

What to notice: the p-polarized reflection (blue) drops to zero at Brewster’s angle, exactly when reflected and refracted rays are at 90°. Near grazing incidence almost everything is reflected, which is why a wet road shines so brightly in low sunlight.

21.2Retardation plates

Derivation

Step 1Plane-polarized light enters a doubly refracting plate (thickness $t$) cut with its optic axis parallel to the faces. It splits into E and O components that travel in the same direction at different speeds.

Step 2On leaving, their path difference is $(\mu_o - \mu_e)t$, so their phase difference is

$$\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)t$$

Quarter-wave plate$\delta = \pi/2$: $t = \dfrac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o - \mu_e)}$. Plane light at 45° to the axis becomes circular; at other angles, elliptical.

Half-wave plate$\delta = \pi$: $t = \dfrac{\lambda}{2(\mu_o - \mu_e)}$. Plane light stays plane but its direction is turned through $2\theta$.

3D simulation

Quarter- and half-wave plates

Try: press Quarter-wave plate with input at 45°, then change the input angle. Try the half-wave plate. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: a quarter-wave plate turns plane light at 45° into circular light, and at other angles into elliptical light. A half-wave plate keeps the light plane but flips its direction to the other side of the optic axis.

21.3The Nicol prism

Construction and action

A calcite crystal is cut diagonally and the two halves are cemented with Canada balsam ($\mu = 1.55$, between $\mu_e = 1.486$ and $\mu_o = 1.658$). The O-ray meets the balsam at more than its critical angle and is totally reflected to the side, where it is absorbed by a blackened face. The E-ray passes through. Out comes plane-polarized light: the Nicol is a polarizer. A second Nicol used as an analyzer transmits it according to Malus’ law; crossed Nicols give darkness.

21.4Optical activity and the polarimeter

Specific rotation

Some substances (quartz, sugar solutions, turpentine) rotate the plane of vibration of plane-polarized light as it passes through: optical activity. Clockwise (looking towards the source) is dextro-rotatory, anticlockwise laevo-rotatory. For a solution, the rotation is proportional to the path length $l$ and the concentration $C$:

$$\theta = [\alpha]\,l\,C \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad [\alpha] = \frac{\theta}{lC} = \frac{10\,\theta}{l_{\text{cm}}\,C}$$

$[\alpha]$ is the specific rotation ($l$ in decimetres, $C$ in g/cm³). It depends on the substance, the wavelength (roughly $\propto1/\lambda^2$), the temperature and the solvent.

Polarimeter (Laurent’s half-shade): sodium lamp → polarizer Nicol → half-shade plate → tube with solution → analyzer Nicol → telescope. Set for equal darkness with water; fill with the solution and turn the analyzer to restore equal darkness. The angle turned is $\theta$. Used to measure sugar concentrations in industry and medicine.

Simulation

Measure a sugar concentration with a polarimeter

Try: set a concentration and tube length, then turn the analyzer until the eyepiece is dark. Read the concentration found.

What to notice: doubling either the concentration or the tube length doubles the rotation. The plane of vibration turns steadily along the tube, and the analyzer must follow it to find darkness.

Practice problems

  1. Find the polarizing angle for water ($\mu = 1.33$) and the angle of refraction there. easy

    $i_p = \tan^{-1}1.33 = 53.1^\circ$; $r = 36.9^\circ$.

  2. Find the thickness of a quartz quarter-wave plate for 589 nm ($\mu_e = 1.553$, $\mu_o = 1.544$). easy

    $t = \lambda/4|\mu_e - \mu_o| = 589\times10^{-9}/0.036 = 16.4\ \mu$m.

  3. A 20 cm tube of sugar solution rotates the plane of vibration by 13°. $[\alpha] = 65^\circ$ dm$^{-1}$(g/cm³)$^{-1}$. Find the concentration. easy

    $C = \theta/([\alpha]l) = 13/(65\times2) = 0.1$ g/cm³.

  4. Why can sound waves not be polarized? easy

    Sound in air is longitudinal: the vibration is along the direction of travel, so there is no transverse direction to select.
