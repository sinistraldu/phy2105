Interference of Light

When two coherent light waves overlap they add, and the result is a pattern of bright and dark fringes. Interference proves that light is a wave, and it lets us measure wavelengths, thin-film thicknesses and tiny displacements to a fraction of a micrometre, the basis of precision instruments used in surveying and materials testing.

Class 12

Theories of light and Huygens’ principle

By the end you can: describe the corpuscular, wave, electromagnetic and quantum theories of light with their successes and failures; state Huygens’ principle and use it to derive the laws of reflection and refraction.

12.1Theories of light

TheoryIdeaExplainsFails to explain
Corpuscular (Newton, 1675)Light is a stream of tiny particlesStraight-line travel, reflection, refraction (but predicts light is faster in glass)Interference, diffraction, polarization; light is actually slower in glass (Foucault)
Wave (Huygens, 1678; Young, Fresnel)Light is a wave in a medium (“ether”)Reflection, refraction, interference, diffraction; transverse waves explain polarizationTravel through vacuum; photoelectric effect
Electromagnetic (Maxwell, 1864)Light is oscillating electric and magnetic fields, speed $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$All wave optics; no medium neededPhotoelectric effect, blackbody radiation, Compton effect
Quantum (Planck 1900, Einstein 1905)Light energy comes in photons $E = hf$Photoelectric effect, blackbody, ComptonOn its own, interference

Today light is described by wave–particle duality: it travels and interferes as a wave and is emitted and absorbed as photons. In this course, wave optics explains everything we need.

12.2Huygens’ principle

Principle and derivation of Snell’s law

Principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets that spread at the wave speed; the new wavefront a time $t$ later is the surface touching all the wavelets (their envelope).

Step 1A plane wavefront AB meets a surface at angle $i$. While the edge B travels the distance BC $= v_1t$ in air, the wavelet from A travels AD $= v_2t$ in glass.

Step 2In triangles ABC and ADC (common hypotenuse AC): $\sin i = \dfrac{v_1t}{AC}$, $\sin r = \dfrac{v_2t}{AC}$. Dividing:

$$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \mu \qquad (\text{Snell’s law})$$

The same construction with both media equal gives reflection with $i = r$. Wave theory therefore predicts $v_2 = v_1/\mu$: light is slower in glass, as measured.

Simulation

Huygens’ wavelets bend the wavefront

Try: change the angle of incidence and the refractive index of the lower medium.

What to notice: in the slower medium the wavefronts are closer together ($\lambda/\mu$) and turn towards the normal. The wavelet from A grows only to radius $v_2t$ while the incident front moves $v_1t$ to B: that ratio sets the angle.

Class 13

Interference of light and Young’s double-slit experiment

By the end you can: state the conditions for sustained interference; distinguish interference by division of wavefront and of amplitude; derive the positions of bright and dark fringes, the fringe width and the intensity distribution in Young’s experiment.

13.1Conditions for interference

Key ideas

When two waves overlap, their displacements add (superposition). Where crest meets crest the intensity is large (constructive, path difference $n\lambda$); where crest meets trough it is small (destructive, path difference $(n+\tfrac12)\lambda$).

For a steady pattern the sources must be coherent: same frequency and a constant phase difference. Two separate lamps are never coherent, so we split one source: by division of wavefront (Young’s slits, Fresnel biprism) or by division of amplitude (thin films, Newton’s rings, Michelson). For good contrast the amplitudes should be nearly equal and the sources narrow and close together.

3D simulation

Two coherent sources in a ripple tank

Try: change the separation of the sources and the wavelength. Give one source a phase lag of 180°. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: calm “nodal lines” fan out from between the sources where the waves always cancel. Moving the sources apart or shortening the wavelength gives more, narrower lines. A 180° phase difference swaps calm and rough lines: the centre line becomes calm.

13.2Young’s double-slit experiment

Derivation: fringe positions and width

Step 1Slits S₁, S₂ are a distance $d$ apart; the screen is $D$ away ($D\gg d$). For a point P at height $y$, the path difference is $S_2P - S_1P \approx d\sin\theta\approx\dfrac{yd}{D}$.

Step 2Bright fringes: $\dfrac{yd}{D} = n\lambda \Rightarrow y_n = \dfrac{n\lambda D}{d}$. Dark fringes: $y_n = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$.

Step 3The spacing of neighbouring bright (or dark) fringes, the fringe width, is

$$\beta = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
Derivation: intensity distribution

Step 1Waves from the two slits at P: $y_1 = a\sin\omega t$, $y_2 = a\sin(\omega t + \delta)$ with phase difference $\delta = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\dfrac{yd}{D}$.

Step 2Their sum is $R\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ with $R^2 = a^2 + a^2 + 2a^2\cos\delta = 4a^2\cos^2(\delta/2)$.

$$I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac\delta2 = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\pi yd}{\lambda D}$$

$I$ swings between $4I_0$ and 0; the average is $2I_0$, the same as the two slits separately: interference only redistributes energy, it does not create or destroy it.

Simulation

Young’s double slit

Try: change $\lambda$, $d$ and $D$. Switch to white light, and cover one slit.

What to notice: red light gives wider fringes than blue; moving the slits closer together or the screen further away widens the fringes. White light gives a white central fringe with coloured fringes beside it that quickly wash out. With one slit covered the fringes disappear.

Practice problems

  1. In Young’s experiment $d = 0.5$ mm, $D = 1$ m, $\lambda = 600$ nm. Find the fringe width and the distance of the 3rd dark fringe from the centre. easy

    $\beta = 1.2$ mm; 3rd dark fringe $(2.5)\beta = 3.0$ mm.

  2. The fringe width is 0.4 mm. The apparatus is immersed in water ($\mu = 1.33$). Find the new fringe width. easy

    $\lambda\to\lambda/\mu$: $\beta = 0.4/1.33 = 0.30$ mm.

  3. The intensities from two slits are in the ratio 9 : 1. Find the ratio of maximum to minimum intensity. medium

    Amplitudes 3 : 1; $I_{\max}/I_{\min} = (3+1)^2/(3-1)^2 = 4$.

Class 14

Displacement of fringes, Fresnel biprism, wedge-shaped films, Newton’s rings and interferometers

By the end you can: derive the shift of fringes caused by a thin plate and use it to find the plate’s thickness; find $\lambda$ with a biprism; derive the fringe spacing of a wedge film; derive the radii of Newton’s rings and find $\lambda$ or $\mu$ from them; explain the Michelson interferometer.

14.1Displacement of fringes

Derivation

Step 1A thin transparent plate of thickness $t$ and index $\mu$ covers slit S₁. Light takes the same time to cross it as to travel $\mu t$ in air, so the optical path from S₁ increases by $(\mu - 1)t$.

Step 2The central fringe (zero path difference) now sits where $\dfrac{yd}{D} = (\mu-1)t$:

$$x_0 = \frac{D}{d}(\mu - 1)t = \frac{\beta}{\lambda}(\mu-1)t$$

Step 3The whole pattern shifts towards the covered slit by $N = (\mu-1)t/\lambda$ fringes; the fringe width is unchanged. Use: measure the shift with white light (to identify the central fringe) and get the thickness of a very thin sheet: $t = \dfrac{x_0 d}{D(\mu-1)}$.

Simulation

A thin plate shifts the fringes

Try: increase the plate thickness from zero and change its refractive index.

What to notice: the pattern slides sideways while the fringe spacing stays the same. A plate with $\mu = 1$ (no plate at all) gives no shift. A few micrometres of mica can shift the pattern by several fringes.

14.2Fresnel biprism

Derivation

Step 1A biprism (two thin prisms of angle $\alpha$ base to base) bends light from a slit S up and down by $\delta = (\mu - 1)\alpha$, so it seems to come from two virtual coherent sources S₁, S₂.

Step 2At distance $a$ from the prism, their separation is $d = 2a\delta = 2a(\mu - 1)\alpha$.

Step 3With the screen at total distance $D = a + b$: $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda(a+b)}{d}$, so $\lambda = \dfrac{\beta d}{a+b}$. In the lab $d$ is found by the displacement method: $d = \sqrt{d_1d_2}$ from the two lens positions.

Simulation

Fresnel biprism and its virtual sources

Try: change the prism angle, its index and the distances.

What to notice: a larger prism angle or index moves the virtual sources apart, which narrows the fringes but widens the overlap region. Moving the source closer to the prism brings S₁ and S₂ together and widens the fringes.

14.3Interference in thin films and wedge-shaped films

Derivation

Step 1Light reflected from the top and bottom of a film of thickness $t$ and index $\mu$ (angle of refraction $r$) has path difference $2\mu t\cos r$, plus an extra $\lambda/2$ from reflection at the denser surface.

Step 2Reflected light: dark when $2\mu t\cos r = n\lambda$, bright when $2\mu t\cos r = (n+\tfrac12)\lambda$. (Transmitted light is the reverse.) Soap bubbles and oil films are coloured because the condition depends on $\lambda$.

Step 3A wedge: two plates touching at one edge with a tiny angle $\theta$. At distance $x$ from the edge $t = x\theta$. At normal incidence, the $n$th dark fringe is at $2\mu x_n\theta = n\lambda$, so neighbouring fringes are

$$\beta = \frac{\lambda}{2\mu\theta}$$

apart: straight, equally spaced fringes parallel to the edge, with a dark fringe at the contact. Uses: testing the flatness of a surface (fringes bend where it is not flat) and measuring the diameter of a thin wire placed at the open end: $\theta = d_{\text{wire}}/L$.

Simulation

Fringes in a wedge-shaped air film

Try: change the wedge angle, the wavelength, and fill the wedge with a liquid.

What to notice: a steeper wedge packs the fringes closer. Filling the gap with a liquid of index $\mu$ also packs them closer by the factor $\mu$. The contact line is always dark.

14.4Newton’s rings

Derivation

Step 1A plano-convex lens (radius of curvature $R$) rests on a flat glass plate, leaving an air film whose thickness grows outwards. At radius $r$ the film thickness $t$ satisfies $r^2 = t(2R - t)\approx 2Rt$, so $t = \dfrac{r^2}{2R}$.

Step 2In reflected light (normal incidence) the $n$th dark ring has $2\mu t = n\lambda$:

$$r_n^2 = \frac{n\lambda R}{\mu}, \qquad D_n^2 = \frac{4n\lambda R}{\mu}$$

The radii grow as $\sqrt n$, so the rings crowd together outwards. The centre is dark (the film is zero but there is a $\lambda/2$ reflection change).

Step 3Wavelength: to avoid the uncertain contact point use two rings: $\lambda = \dfrac{D_{n+p}^2 - D_n^2}{4pR}$. Refractive index of a liquid: fill the gap; the rings shrink: $\mu = \dfrac{(D_{n+p}^2 - D_n^2)_{\text{air}}}{(D_{n+p}^2 - D_n^2)_{\text{liquid}}}$.

3D simulation

Newton’s rings

Try: change the lens’s radius of curvature, the wavelength, and fill the gap with a liquid. Switch between reflected and transmitted light. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: a flatter lens (larger $R$) gives larger rings; a liquid makes them shrink by $\sqrt\mu$. The reflected and transmitted patterns are complementary: dark centre in reflection, bright in transmission.

14.5Interferometers: the Michelson interferometer

Principle

A half-silvered plate splits a beam into two at right angles (division of amplitude). The beams reflect from mirrors M₁ and M₂ and recombine on the screen. If M₂ is a distance $d$ further away than M₁’s image, the path difference at angle $\theta$ is $2d\cos\theta$: circular fringes, bright when $2d\cos\theta = n\lambda$.

Moving M₂ by $\lambda/2$ moves the pattern by one fringe. Counting $N$ fringes while the mirror moves $x$:

$$\lambda = \frac{2x}{N}$$

Uses: measuring wavelengths and tiny distances, the refractive index of gases, the standard metre, and (in giant form) detecting gravitational waves. Michelson and Morley used it to show there is no ether.

3D simulation

Michelson interferometer

Try: press Move M₂ slowly and count the fringes appearing from the centre. Change the wavelength. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: each time the mirror moves half a wavelength (about 0.3 µm) a new fringe is born at the centre. As $d$ grows the rings get thinner and more numerous.

Practice problems

  1. A mica sheet ($\mu = 1.58$) over one slit shifts the central fringe by 6 fringes of 600 nm light. Find its thickness. easy

    $t = N\lambda/(\mu - 1) = 6(600\times10^{-9})/0.58 = 6.2\ \mu$m.

  2. In a biprism experiment, $a + b = 1$ m, $d = 0.9$ mm and $\beta = 0.65$ mm. Find $\lambda$. easy

    $\lambda = \beta d/D = 585$ nm.

  3. An air wedge between two glass plates 10 cm long is formed by a wire at one end; 20 dark fringes per cm are seen with 589 nm light. Find the wire diameter. medium

    $\beta = 0.5$ mm; $\theta = \lambda/2\beta = 5.89\times10^{-4}$; $d = \theta L = 58.9\ \mu$m.

  4. In Newton’s rings the diameters of the 5th and 15th dark rings are 3.36 mm and 5.90 mm; $R = 1$ m. Find $\lambda$. medium

    $\lambda = (5.90^2 - 3.36^2)\times10^{-6}/(4\times10\times1) = 588$ nm.

  5. A liquid between lens and plate reduces the diameter of the 10th ring from 1.40 cm to 1.27 cm. Find $\mu$. easy

    $\mu = (1.40/1.27)^2 = 1.215$.

  6. Moving a Michelson mirror by 0.0295 mm passes 100 fringes. Find $\lambda$. easy

    $\lambda = 2x/N = 590$ nm.
