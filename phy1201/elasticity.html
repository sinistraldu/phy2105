Elasticity

Every beam, column, cable and foundation deforms a little under load and springs back when the load is removed, as long as we stay within its elastic limit. This unit gives the language engineers use for that: stress, strain, the elastic moduli, Poisson’s ratio and the energy stored in a strained body.

Class 1

States of matter, stress and strain, the stress–strain diagram and Hooke’s law

By the end you can: compare solids, liquids and gases; define stress and strain of each kind; read a stress–strain diagram (proportional limit, elastic limit, yield point, ultimate strength, fracture); state Hooke’s law; distinguish ductile and brittle materials.

1.1States of matter

Big idea

Atoms attract each other at moderate distances and repel strongly when pushed too close. In a solid each atom vibrates about a fixed place in a lattice, so a solid has a definite shape and volume and resists every kind of deformation. In a liquid the atoms stay close but slide past each other: fixed volume, no fixed shape, no resistance to shear. In a gas the atoms are far apart and move freely: no fixed shape or volume, and easy to compress.

1.2Stress and strain

Definitions

Stress is the internal restoring force per unit area, $\text{stress} = F/A$ (unit N/m² = Pa). Strain is the fractional change in size (no unit). There are three kinds:

DeformationStressStrainModulus
Longitudinal (tension or compression)$F/A$ normal to the face$\Delta L/L$Young’s $Y = \dfrac{F/A}{\Delta L/L}$
Shear$F/A$ tangential to the faceangle $\theta$ (rad)Rigidity $\eta = \dfrac{F/A}{\theta}$
Volume (bulk)pressure change $\Delta P$$-\Delta V/V$Bulk $K = \dfrac{-\Delta P}{\Delta V/V}$; compressibility $1/K$

Hooke’s law: within the proportional limit, stress $\propto$ strain, i.e. $\text{stress} = \text{modulus}\times\text{strain}$.

1.3Behaviour of a solid under stress: the stress–strain diagram

Reading the curve of a tensile test

O–AProportional limit: a straight line, Hooke’s law holds; the slope is $Y$.

A–BElastic limit: up to here the wire still returns to its original length when unloaded.

B–CYield point: the material flows: strain grows with almost no increase in stress (plastic deformation). Unloading now leaves a permanent set; the unloading line is parallel to O–A.

C–DStrain hardening up to the ultimate tensile strength D (the maximum stress).

D–ENecking: the bar thins at one place and breaks at the fracture point E.

Ductile materials (mild steel, copper, aluminium) have a long plastic region, so they give warning before failure. Brittle materials (cast iron, glass, concrete in tension) break almost at the end of the elastic region. This is why concrete is reinforced with steel bars on the tension side of a beam.

Simulation

Try: choose a material and stretch the specimen step by step to breaking. Watch the dashed unloading line once you pass the elastic limit.

What to notice: steel and aluminium stretch a lot (many %) after yielding, and absorb a large energy (the shaded area) before breaking: that is toughness. Cast iron and concrete break at a tiny strain with little warning. The elastic part is very steep: real elastic strains are only about 0.1%.

Practice problems

  1. A steel rod of diameter 20 mm carries a tension of 50 kN. Find the stress. easy

    $A = \pi(0.01)^2 = 3.14\times10^{-4}$ m²; stress $= 1.59\times10^8$ Pa $= 159$ MPa.

  2. A 2 m wire stretches 1 mm. Find the strain and the stress if $Y = 2\times10^{11}$ Pa. easy

    Strain $= 5\times10^{-4}$; stress $= Y\times$ strain $= 1.0\times10^8$ Pa.

  3. Water is compressed by 0.5% of its volume. What pressure is needed? ($K = 2.2$ GPa) easy

    $\Delta P = K\,\Delta V/V = 2.2\times10^9\times0.005 = 1.1\times10^7$ Pa (about 110 atm).

  4. A rubber pad 2 cm thick with a top area of 100 cm² carries a horizontal force of 500 N. Its top shifts by 2 mm. Find the shear strain and rigidity modulus. medium

    $\theta = 2/20 = 0.1$ rad; stress $= 500/0.01 = 5\times10^4$ Pa; $\eta = 5\times10^5$ Pa.

Class 2

Elastic moduli, Poisson’s ratio, relations among the constants and strain energy

By the end you can: find $Y$ from a loaded wire; define Poisson’s ratio and its limits; derive $Y = 2\eta(1+\sigma)$, $Y = 3K(1-2\sigma)$ and $9/Y = 1/K + 3/\eta$; derive the strain energy and energy density for stretching, shearing and compression.

2.1Determining Young’s modulus

Searle’s method

Two identical wires hang from the same support. One carries a constant load to keep it straight (reference); the other carries the load $m$ under test, and a spirit level and micrometer read its extension $\Delta L$ relative to the reference (so sagging of the support and temperature changes cancel).

$$Y = \frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L} = \frac{mgL}{\pi r^2\,\Delta L}$$

Plot $\Delta L$ against $m$: a straight line through the origin (Hooke’s law) with slope $gL/(\pi r^2Y)$.

Simulation

Stretch a wire: extension, strain energy, breaking

Try: change the load, length and diameter, and pick different metals. Try a thin wire with a heavy load.

What to notice: $\Delta L$ doubles when you double the load or the length, but falls by 4 when you double the diameter (area $\propto d^2$). Stiffer metals (larger $Y$) stretch less. A thin wire snaps once the stress passes its breaking stress, whatever its length.

2.2Poisson’s ratio

Definition and limits

When a bar is stretched it gets thinner. Poisson’s ratio is

$$\sigma = -\frac{\text{lateral strain}}{\text{longitudinal strain}} = -\frac{\Delta d/d}{\Delta L/L}$$

From the relations below, $K > 0$ needs $\sigma < \tfrac12$ and $\eta > 0$ needs $\sigma > -1$. In practice $0 < \sigma < 0.5$: concrete 0.1–0.2, steel 0.3, rubber almost 0.5 (it keeps its volume when stretched). Cork has $\sigma\approx0$, which is why it fits easily into a bottle.

2.3Relations among the elastic constants

Derivation: $Y$, $\eta$ and $\sigma$

Step 1A unit cube is acted on by a shearing stress $S$ on its faces. This is equivalent to a tensile stress $S$ along one diagonal and an equal compressive stress $S$ along the other.

Step 2The extension per unit length of the stretched diagonal is $\dfrac SY + \sigma\dfrac SY = \dfrac SY(1+\sigma)$ (stretch from the tension plus the Poisson stretch from the compression).

Step 3Geometry of the sheared cube: the diagonal strain is $\theta/2$, with $\theta = S/\eta$. So $\dfrac{S}{2\eta} = \dfrac SY(1+\sigma)$:

$$Y = 2\eta(1+\sigma)$$
Derivation: $Y$, $K$ and $\sigma$

Step 1A unit cube under equal pressure $P$ on all faces. Along each axis, the strain from the stress on that axis is $-P/Y$, and each of the other two adds $+\sigma P/Y$ (Poisson), giving $-\dfrac PY(1-2\sigma)$.

Step 2Volume strain = sum of the three linear strains: $\dfrac{\Delta V}V = -\dfrac{3P}{Y}(1-2\sigma)$, and $K = \dfrac{P}{-\Delta V/V}$:

$$Y = 3K(1-2\sigma), \qquad \text{and eliminating }\sigma:\quad \frac9Y = \frac1K + \frac3\eta, \qquad \sigma = \frac{3K - 2\eta}{6K + 2\eta}$$
3D simulation

Stretch, shear and squeeze a cube

Try: choose Tension, Shear or Bulk. Change the stress, Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio. The deformation is magnified so you can see it; drag to rotate.

What to notice: in tension the cube gets longer and thinner; with $\sigma = 0$ it does not get thinner at all, and with negative $\sigma$ it actually gets fatter. In shear the faces slide (the volume does not change). In bulk compression the cube shrinks evenly; as $\sigma\to0.5$, $K$ becomes enormous and the cube hardly shrinks.

Simulation

How $\eta$ and $K$ depend on Poisson’s ratio

Try: move Poisson’s ratio across its whole range, or press a material button. Change $Y$ and check that $9/Y = 1/K + 3/\eta$ always holds.

What to notice: $\eta$ is always a bit less than half of $Y$ for real materials. $K$ shoots up as $\sigma\to\tfrac12$ (rubber, water-like solids are almost incompressible) and would become negative beyond it, which is impossible: that is the upper limit of $\sigma$.

2.4Strain energy

Derivation: work done in stretching a wire

Step 1When the extension is $x$, the force is $F = \dfrac{YA}{L}x$ (Hooke’s law).

Step 2Work to extend further by $dx$: $dW = F\,dx$. Total for extension $l$:

$$W = \int_0^l \frac{YA}{L}x\,dx = \frac12\frac{YA}{L}l^2 = \frac12 F\,l$$

Step 3Divide by the volume $AL$: energy density

$$u = \frac12\,\frac FA\,\frac lL = \frac12\,\text{stress}\times\text{strain} = \frac{(\text{stress})^2}{2Y}$$

The same form holds for the other deformations: shear $u = \tfrac12 S\theta = S^2/2\eta$; bulk $u = \tfrac12\,\Delta P\,(\Delta V/V) = (\Delta P)^2/2K$. On a graph, the energy is the area under the stress–strain line.

Worked example

A steel cable ($Y = 200$ GPa) 10 m long and 1 cm² in cross-section carries 10 kN. Find the extension, the stored energy and the energy density.

Stress $= 10^4/10^{-4} = 10^8$ Pa; strain $= 10^8/2\times10^{11} = 5\times10^{-4}$; $\Delta L = 5$ mm.

$W = \tfrac12F\Delta L = \tfrac12(10^4)(0.005) = 25$ J; $u = \tfrac12(10^8)(5\times10^{-4}) = 2.5\times10^4$ J/m³ (check: $25/(10^{-4}\times10) = 2.5\times10^4$).

Practice problems

  1. A wire 3 m long and 0.6 mm in diameter stretches 1.2 mm under a 4 kg load. Find $Y$. easy

    $Y = mgL/(\pi r^2\Delta L) = 4(9.8)(3)/(\pi(3\times10^{-4})^2\times1.2\times10^{-3}) \approx 3.5\times10^{11}$ Pa.

  2. For steel $Y = 200$ GPa and $\sigma = 0.29$. Find $\eta$ and $K$. easy

    $\eta = 200/2.58 = 77.5$ GPa; $K = 200/(3\times0.42) = 159$ GPa.

  3. A material has $K = 140$ GPa and $\eta = 80$ GPa. Find $Y$ and $\sigma$. medium

    $9/Y = 1/140 + 3/80 \Rightarrow Y = 201$ GPa; $\sigma = (3K-2\eta)/(6K+2\eta) = 260/1000 = 0.26$.

  4. Show that $\sigma$ cannot exceed 0.5. medium

    $Y = 3K(1-2\sigma)$ with $Y, K > 0$ requires $1 - 2\sigma > 0$.

  5. Find the work needed to stretch a 1 m wire of area 1 mm² by 1 mm ($Y = 2\times10^{11}$ Pa). easy

    $W = \tfrac12(YA/L)l^2 = \tfrac12(2\times10^{11}\times10^{-6})(10^{-6}) = 0.1$ J.

  6. A column of concrete 3 m high and 0.3 m × 0.3 m in section carries 270 kN. Find the shortening and the energy stored ($Y = 30$ GPa). hard

    Stress $= 3$ MPa; strain $= 10^{-4}$; shortening $= 0.3$ mm; $W = \tfrac12(2.7\times10^5)(3\times10^{-4}) = 40.5$ J.
