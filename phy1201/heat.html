Class 22

Temperature scales, measurement principles and thermometers

By the end you can: convert between the Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin and Rankine scales; explain the principle of a thermometric property; describe resistance, thermoelectric and radiation thermometers and use their equations.

22.1Temperature and the zeroth law

Big idea

Two bodies in thermal equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other (zeroth law). That is what lets a thermometer work: it reaches equilibrium with the object and then reads its own temperature. Any property that changes steadily with temperature (length of a liquid column, gas pressure, electrical resistance, a thermocouple EMF, emitted radiation) can serve as a thermometric property.

Key results
$$T_X = 100\,\frac{X - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0}\ ^\circ\text{C}, \qquad \frac{C}{100} = \frac{F-32}{180} = \frac{K-273.15}{100} = \frac{R-491.67}{180}$$

$X_0$ and $X_{100}$ are the readings of the property at the ice point and the steam point. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero; the Rankine scale is its Fahrenheit-sized counterpart.

Simulation

One temperature, four scales

Try: slide the temperature and read the four scales. Find the temperature where Celsius and Fahrenheit read the same.

What to notice: all scales are straight-line (linear) relations of each other. $-40^\circ$C $= -40^\circ$F. Kelvin and Celsius have the same step size; Fahrenheit and Rankine steps are 5/9 as big.

22.2Resistance, thermoelectric and radiation thermometers

Platinum resistance thermometer
$$R_T = R_0\left(1 + \alpha T + \beta T^2\right) \qquad (\text{Pt100: } R_0 = 100\ \Omega,\ \alpha = 3.908\times10^{-3},\ \beta = -5.78\times10^{-7})$$

Construction: a fine platinum coil wound on mica in a sealed tube, connected in a Wheatstone bridge with compensating leads. Very accurate from about −200 °C to 1200 °C, but slow to respond.

Thermocouple (Seebeck effect)
$$\varepsilon = aT + \tfrac12bT^2, \qquad T_n = -\frac{a}{b}\ \text{(neutral temperature, }\varepsilon\text{ max)}, \qquad T_i = 2T_n\ \text{(inversion)}$$

Two different metals joined at a hot and a cold junction produce an EMF that depends on the temperature difference. Small, fast and cheap; used up to about 1600 °C.

Radiation pyrometer
$$P = \sigma A\varepsilon T^4\ \ (\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}), \qquad \lambda_{\max}T = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$$

Focuses the radiation from a hot body on a detector (total radiation pyrometer) or compares its colour with a heated filament (optical pyrometer). No contact needed, so it works for furnaces and molten metal.

Simulation

Three thermometers, three physical laws

Try: pick a thermometer and slide the temperature. For the thermocouple, change the material constants and find the neutral and inversion temperatures.

What to notice: the Pt resistance rises almost linearly; the thermocouple EMF rises, peaks at $T_n$ and falls back to zero at $T_i = 2T_n$ (so a thermocouple must be used well below $T_n$); the radiated power grows as $T^4$, so the pyrometer is very sensitive at high temperature.

Practice problems

  1. Convert 37 °C to °F, K and °R. easy

    98.6 °F; 310.15 K; 558.27 °R.

  2. At what temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales agree? easy

    Put $F = C$ in $C = (F-32)\times5/9$: $9C = 5C - 160$, so $C = F = -40$.

  3. A thermometric property reads 2.0 at the ice point, 7.6 at the steam point and 5.2 in a bath. Find the bath temperature. easy

    $T = 100(5.2-2.0)/(7.6-2.0) \approx 57.1$ °C.

  4. Find the resistance of a Pt100 at 100 °C. easy

    $R = 100(1 + 0.3908 - 0.00578) \approx 138.5\ \Omega$.

  5. A thermocouple has $a = 40\ \mu$V/°C and $b = -0.08\ \mu$V/°C². Find $T_n$ and $T_i$. medium

    $T_n = -a/b = 500$ °C; $T_i = 1000$ °C.

  6. A furnace’s absolute temperature doubles from 600 K to 1200 K. By what factor does its radiated power change? easy

    $P \propto T^4$: factor 16.

Class 23

Kinetic theory: pressure, temperature, equipartition, mean free path, Brownian motion

By the end you can: state the postulates; derive $P = \tfrac13\rho\overline{c^2}$ and $\overline{KE} = \tfrac32kT$; count degrees of freedom and use equipartition; derive the mean free path; build the ideal gas equation from the gas laws and explain its failure; explain Brownian motion.

23.1Postulates of kinetic theory

  • A gas is made of a very large number of identical molecules, small compared with the distances between them.
  • They move randomly in all directions with a range of speeds, obeying Newton’s laws.
  • Collisions with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic and take negligible time.
  • There are no forces between molecules except during collisions.

23.2Pressure of a gas

Derivation

Step 1A molecule of mass $m$ with velocity component $c_x$ hits a wall of a cube of side $L$ and rebounds: momentum change $2mc_x$.

Step 2It returns to the same wall every $2L/c_x$, so the average force it exerts is $2mc_x / (2L/c_x) = mc_x^2/L$.

Step 3For $N$ molecules: $F = \dfrac{m}{L}\sum c_x^2 = \dfrac{mN}{L}\overline{c_x^2}$. By symmetry $\overline{c_x^2} = \tfrac13\overline{c^2}$.

Step 4$P = F/L^2 = \dfrac{mN}{3L^3}\overline{c^2}$, and with $V = L^3$, $\rho = mN/V$:

$$P = \frac13\frac{N}{V}m\overline{c^2} = \frac13\rho\,\overline{c^2}$$
Derivation: kinetic energy and temperature

Step 1Compare $PV = \tfrac13Nm\overline{c^2}$ with the gas law $PV = NkT$ ($k = R/N_A = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K).

Step 2$\tfrac13m\overline{c^2} = kT$, so

$$\overline{KE} = \tfrac12m\overline{c^2} = \tfrac32kT, \qquad c_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$$

Temperature is a measure of the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules.

3D simulation

Molecules in a box: where pressure comes from

Try: change the temperature, the number of molecules and the box volume (move the piston). Watch the pressure measured from wall hits.

What to notice: the measured pressure (from momentum delivered to the walls) follows $P = NkT/V$: double $N$ or $T$ and it doubles; double $V$ and it halves. Hotter molecules (redder) move faster: $c_{\text{rms}}\propto\sqrt T$.

23.3Maxwell speed distribution

Not all molecules have the same speed. The fraction with speeds near $v$ is given by Maxwell’s distribution, from which three useful speeds follow:

$$v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m}}, \qquad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi m}}, \qquad v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3kT}{m}}$$
Simulation

Maxwell speed distribution

Try: change the temperature and the gas (molar mass). Compare the three speeds.

What to notice: hotter or lighter gases spread to higher speeds and the peak gets lower (the area stays 1). Always $v_p < \bar v < v_{\text{rms}}$. Hydrogen molecules are fast enough that some escape from the atmosphere.

23.4Degrees of freedom and equipartition of energy

Key results

Each independent quadratic term in a molecule’s energy (a degree of freedom) holds on average $\tfrac12kT$.

$$U = \frac f2 nRT, \qquad C_V = \frac f2R, \qquad C_P = C_V + R, \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = 1 + \frac2f$$

3D simulation

Degrees of freedom of a molecule

Try: choose a monatomic, diatomic or polyatomic molecule. Turn the vibration on (high temperature) and change the temperature.

What to notice: each way the molecule can move or turn holds $\tfrac12kT$. A diatomic molecule cannot usefully spin about its own axis, so it has 2 rotational, not 3. Vibration adds kinetic and potential energy (2 more) only when hot.

23.5Mean free path

Derivation

Step 1Treat molecules as spheres of diameter $d$. A moving molecule hits any other whose centre lies within $d$ of its path: it sweeps a collision cylinder of cross-section $\pi d^2$.

Step 2In time $t$ it travels $\bar vt$ and sweeps volume $\pi d^2\bar vt$, which contains $n\pi d^2\bar vt$ molecules ($n = N/V$), so that many collisions.

Step 3Mean free path = distance / collisions $= 1/(n\pi d^2)$. Allowing for the motion of the other molecules (relative speed $\sqrt2\,\bar v$):

$$\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2 n} = \frac{kT}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2P}$$
3D simulation

Follow one molecule: the mean free path

Try: change the number density and the molecular diameter. Follow the orange molecule’s zig-zag path.

What to notice: double the density or double the diameter and the average straight run between collisions gets shorter ($\lambda\propto 1/nd^2$). The measured average approaches the formula as more collisions are counted.

23.6Equation of state and its failure

Derivation: ideal gas equation from the gas laws

Boyle: $V\propto1/P$ (fixed $T$, $n$). Charles: $V\propto T$ (fixed $P$, $n$). Avogadro: $V\propto n$ (fixed $P$, $T$). Together $V\propto nT/P$, i.e. $PV = nRT$ with $R = 8.314$ J mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$.

Failure: at high pressure and low temperature real gases deviate: molecules have their own volume and attract each other, and gases liquefy, which $PV = nRT$ can never predict.

The corrections for molecular size and attraction lead to the van der Waals equation (Class 24).

23.7Brownian motion

Evidence for molecules

In 1827 Robert Brown saw pollen grains in water jiggling for ever in random directions. The grain is hit on all sides by billions of invisible water molecules each second; at any instant the kicks do not quite cancel, so the grain makes a random walk.

Step 1Equipartition: the grain has the same average kinetic energy as a molecule, $\tfrac12M\overline{v_x^2} = \tfrac12kT$, but because $M$ is huge its speed is tiny.

Step 2In a random walk the displacement does not grow with $t$ but its square does. Einstein (1905) showed, with Stokes’ drag $6\pi\eta r$ on a sphere of radius $r$ in a fluid of viscosity $\eta$:

$$\overline{x^2} = 2Dt, \qquad D = \frac{kT}{6\pi\eta r}$$

Measuring $\overline{x^2}$ under a microscope gave Perrin the value of $k$, and hence Avogadro’s number $N_A = R/k$: the first direct proof that atoms exist.

3D simulation

Brownian motion of a pollen grain

Try: change the temperature, the grain radius and the viscosity of the liquid. Press Clear track to start a new walk. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: hotter liquid, a smaller grain or a thinner liquid make the grain wander faster ($D = kT/6\pi\eta r$). The track never settles: the mean square distance keeps growing in proportion to time, as the live readout shows.

Practice problems

  1. Find $c_{\text{rms}}$ for nitrogen ($M = 28$ g/mol) at 300 K. easy

    $\sqrt{3RT/M} = \sqrt{3\times8.314\times300/0.028} \approx 517$ m/s.

  2. Find the average translational KE of a molecule at 300 K. easy

    $\tfrac32kT = 6.21\times10^{-21}$ J $\approx 0.039$ eV.

  3. Air has density 1.29 kg/m³ and $c_{\text{rms}} = 485$ m/s. Find the pressure. easy

    $P = \tfrac13\rho c^2 \approx 1.01\times10^5$ Pa (1 atm).

  4. Find $C_V$, $C_P$ and $\gamma$ for a diatomic gas (no vibration). easy

    $C_V = \tfrac52R = 20.8$, $C_P = \tfrac72R = 29.1$ J mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $\gamma = 1.4$.

  5. Find the mean free path of air molecules ($d = 3.7\times10^{-10}$ m) at STP ($n = 2.69\times10^{25}$ m$^{-3}$). medium

    $\lambda = 1/(\sqrt2\pi d^2n) \approx 6.1\times10^{-8}$ m, about 165 molecular diameters.
