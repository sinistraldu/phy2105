Diffraction of Light

Light bends slightly around edges and spreads out after passing through small openings. Diffraction sets the limit on how much detail any lens, camera, microscope or surveying telescope can resolve, and a diffraction grating turns that spreading into one of the most precise tools for measuring wavelengths.

Class 16

Diffraction: Fresnel and Fraunhofer, and the single slit

By the end you can: explain the geometrical shadow and when diffraction is noticeable; distinguish Fresnel and Fraunhofer diffraction and interference from diffraction; derive the intensity of single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction and the positions of its minima and maxima.

16.1The geometrical shadow and the condition for diffraction

Big idea

Ray optics predicts a sharp-edged geometrical shadow. In reality, by Huygens’ principle, the unblocked part of every wavefront sends wavelets into the shadow region. The effect is noticeable when the size of the obstacle or opening is not too large compared with the wavelength: sound (λ ≈ 1 m) bends easily around buildings; light (λ ≈ 0.5 µm) needs openings of a fraction of a millimetre.

Examples: straight edge, small round obstacle (bright Poisson spot)Examples: single slit, grating, circular aperture of a telescope
InterferenceDiffraction

Simulation

Fresnel diffraction at a straight edge

Try: move the screen closer to and further from the edge; change the wavelength.

What to notice: light leaks into the geometric shadow and fades smoothly; outside it there are bright and dark bands, the first one brighter than the unobstructed light. At the edge of the shadow the intensity is exactly one quarter. The bands spread out as $\sqrt{\lambda b}$: this is Fresnel diffraction, so the pattern changes as the distance changes.

16.2Fraunhofer diffraction at a single slit

Derivation

Step 1A plane wave falls on a slit of width $a$. Divide the slit into many strips $dy$, each sending a wavelet. In the direction $\theta$, the wavelet from height $y$ has an extra path $y\sin\theta$, a phase $ky\sin\theta$.

Step 2Add the wavelets (integrate across the slit):

$$E(\theta)\propto\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}e^{iky\sin\theta}dy = a\,\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$$

Step 3Intensity:

$$I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2$$

Minima$\sin\alpha = 0$, $\alpha = n\pi$: $a\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ($n = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$).

Secondary maxima$\dfrac{d}{d\alpha}\left(\dfrac{\sin\alpha}\alpha\right) = 0\Rightarrow\tan\alpha = \alpha$: $\alpha\approx1.43\pi, 2.46\pi, \ldots$ with intensities $\approx4.7\%$, $1.6\%$ of $I_0$.

WidthOn a screen at distance $D$ (or the focal plane of a lens), the central maximum spans $\pm\lambda D/a$: width $\dfrac{2\lambda D}{a}$. A narrower slit gives a wider pattern.

Simulation

Single-slit diffraction pattern

Try: narrow and widen the slit; change the wavelength.

What to notice: the central bright band is twice as wide as the others and holds most of the light. Halving the slit width doubles the pattern’s width. Red light spreads more than blue.

Practice problems

  1. Light of 600 nm falls on a 0.2 mm slit; a lens of focal length 1 m forms the pattern. Find the width of the central maximum. easy

    $2\lambda f/a = 6$ mm.

  2. The first minimum of a single-slit pattern is at 30° for 500 nm light. Find the slit width. easy

    $a = \lambda/\sin30^\circ = 1\ \mu$m.

  3. Find the intensity at the point midway between the central maximum and the first minimum. medium

    $\alpha = \pi/2$: $I/I_0 = (1/(\pi/2))^2 = 4/\pi^2 = 0.405$.

Class 17

Diffraction at a double slit and an N-slit grating

By the end you can: derive the intensity for two and for $N$ slits; locate principal maxima, minima and secondary maxima; explain missing orders; use the grating equation with the grating element.

17.1Double slit

Derivation

Step 1Two slits of width $a$ with centres $d = a + b$ apart. Each slit gives the single-slit amplitude $\dfrac{\sin\alpha}\alpha$; the two are out of phase by $2\beta$ with $\beta = \dfrac{\pi(a+b)\sin\theta}{\lambda}$.

Step 2Adding them, $|1 + e^{2i\beta}|^2 = 4\cos^2\beta$:

$$I = 4I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2\cos^2\beta$$

Young’s $\cos^2$ interference fringes sit inside the single-slit diffraction envelope. Interference maxima: $(a+b)\sin\theta = n\lambda$; diffraction minima: $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$.

Missing ordersIf both conditions hold at once, $\dfrac{a+b}{a} = \dfrac nm$, that interference order is suppressed. With $b = a$, orders 2, 4, 6, … are missing.

17.2N slits: the plane diffraction grating

Derivation

Step 1$N$ equally spaced slits; neighbouring waves differ in phase by $2\beta$. The sum of $N$ equal phasors is a geometric series: $\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{2ij\beta}\right| = \dfrac{\sin N\beta}{\sin\beta}$.

$$I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin N\beta}{\sin\beta}\right)^2$$

Principal maxima$\sin\beta = 0$, $\beta = n\pi$: the grating equation

$$(a+b)\sin\theta = n\lambda$$

There the intensity is $N^2$ times that of one slit. $(a+b)$ is the grating element; with $N'$ lines per metre, $a+b = 1/N'$.

Minima$\sin N\beta = 0$ but $\sin\beta\ne0$: $N - 1$ minima and $N - 2$ weak secondary maxima between neighbouring principal maxima. More slits make the principal maxima sharper and brighter.

Highest order$\sin\theta\le1$: $n_{\max} = (a+b)/\lambda$.

Simulation

From one slit to a grating

Try: increase the number of slits from 1 to 12. Set the spacing ratio $(a+b)/a$ to 2, 3 and 4 and look for missing orders.

What to notice: the principal maxima stay in the same places but get narrower as $N$ grows, with $N-2$ tiny bumps between them. The single-slit envelope (dashed) controls their heights; where an envelope zero lands on a principal maximum, that order disappears.

Practice problems

  1. A grating has 5000 lines/cm. Find the angle of the 2nd-order maximum for 589 nm light and the highest order visible. easy

    $a+b = 2\ \mu$m; $\sin\theta = 0.589$, $\theta = 36.1^\circ$; $n_{\max} = 2/0.589 = 3$.

  2. In a double-slit pattern, slit width 0.1 mm and separation 0.4 mm. Which orders are missing? easy

    $(a+b)/a = 4$: orders 4, 8, 12, …

  3. How many interference fringes lie within the central diffraction maximum when $(a+b) = 3a$? medium

    Orders $-2$ to $2$: 5 fringes (order 3 is missing at the envelope zero).

Class 18

Circular aperture, resolving power, dispersive power and the Poynting vector

By the end you can: describe the Airy pattern and its central width; use the f-number; state the Rayleigh criterion; derive the resolving power and dispersive power of a grating; find the intensity of an electromagnetic wave from the Poynting vector.

18.1Diffraction by a circular aperture

Key results

A circular hole of diameter $D$ (a lens, a telescope mirror, the pupil of the eye) gives a bright central Airy disc surrounded by faint rings. The first dark ring is at

$$\sin\theta = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}$$

(the factor 1.22 comes from the first zero of the Bessel function $J_1$). In the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$, the Airy disc has radius $1.22\lambda f/D = 1.22\lambda N$, where $N = f/D$ is the f-number. About 84% of the light falls in the central disc; its half-width at half maximum is about half its radius.

3D simulation

Diffraction pattern as a landscape of intensity

Try: change the aperture size; switch between a circular and a rectangular hole and change its aspect ratio. Press Exaggerate faint rings. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: the circular hole gives a tall central peak with rings; the rectangular hole gives a cross, widest along the hole’s short side. A smaller hole spreads the pattern wider: diffraction always goes as $\lambda$ / size.

18.2Resolving power

Rayleigh criterion and resolving power

Rayleigh criterion: two point sources are just resolved when the central maximum of one falls on the first minimum of the other. For a telescope or camera the smallest resolvable angle is $\theta_{\min} = 1.22\lambda/D$; its resolving power is $1/\theta_{\min}$. A bigger objective resolves finer detail.

Grating, Step 1The $n$th order principal maximum at $\lambda$ is at $(a+b)\sin\theta = n\lambda$. Its edge (the next minimum) is at $(a+b)\sin\theta = n\lambda + \lambda/N$ for $N$ illuminated lines.

Step 2Two wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + d\lambda$ are just resolved when the $n$th maximum of $\lambda + d\lambda$ falls on that minimum: $n(\lambda + d\lambda) = n\lambda + \lambda/N$:

$$\text{resolving power } \frac{\lambda}{d\lambda} = nN$$

Dispersive powerDifferentiate the grating equation: $(a+b)\cos\theta\,d\theta = n\,d\lambda$:

$$\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{(a+b)\cos\theta}$$

Higher orders and finer gratings spread the spectrum more. Dispersive power (how far apart the lines are) is not the same as resolving power (whether they can be seen as separate).

Simulation

Can the telescope separate two stars?

Try: bring the two stars closer together, then enlarge the aperture. Change the wavelength.

What to notice: when the separation drops below $1.22\lambda/D$, the two Airy discs merge into one blob. A larger aperture or a shorter wavelength makes the discs smaller and separates them again.

Simulation

Grating spectra and the sodium doublet

Try: change the number of illuminated lines until the two sodium lines separate. Then view the 2nd and 3rd orders.

What to notice: to separate 589.0 and 589.6 nm you need $nN\ge982$: about 1000 lines in first order, but only 500 in second. Finer gratings spread the spectrum further (dispersion) and allow fewer orders.

18.3The Poynting vector

Energy flow in an electromagnetic wave

Light is an electromagnetic wave: $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ oscillate in phase, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, with $E = cB$. The energy flow per unit area per second is the Poynting vector

$$\vec S = \frac1{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B, \qquad \langle S\rangle = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0c} = \frac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$$

The energy density is shared equally between the electric field ($\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$) and the magnetic field ($B^2/2\mu_0$). Sunlight at the Earth carries about 1.36 kW/m² ($E_0\approx1000$ V/m).

3D simulation

Electric and magnetic fields of light and the energy they carry

Try: change the field amplitude. Drag to rotate and look along the beam.

What to notice: $\vec E$ (red) and $\vec B$ (blue) rise and fall together at right angles; $\vec S$ (green) always points forward, pulsing at twice the wave frequency. Doubling $E_0$ makes the intensity four times bigger.

Practice problems

  1. What is the smallest angle a 10 cm telescope can resolve at 550 nm? How far apart must two car headlights be to be resolved at 10 km? easy

    $\theta = 1.22(550\times10^{-9})/0.1 = 6.7\ \mu$rad; separation $6.7\times10^{-6}\times10^4 = 0.067$ m.

  2. A camera lens of focal length 50 mm is set to f/8. Find the radius of the Airy disc for 550 nm light. easy

    $1.22\times550\times10^{-9}\times8 = 5.4\ \mu$m.

  3. What is the minimum number of lines a grating needs to resolve the sodium doublet in the 2nd order? easy

    $\lambda/d\lambda = 589.3/0.6 = 982$; $N = 982/2 = 491$.

  4. Find the dispersive power of a 6000 lines/cm grating in the first order at 500 nm. medium

    $\sin\theta = 0.3$, $\cos\theta = 0.954$; $d\theta/d\lambda = 1/(1.667\times10^{-6}\times0.954) = 6.3\times10^5$ rad/m.

  5. A laser beam has intensity 1 kW/m². Find $E_0$ and $B_0$. easy

    $E_0 = \sqrt{2\mu_0cS} = 868$ V/m; $B_0 = 2.9\ \mu$T.
