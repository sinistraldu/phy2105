Oscillations

Buildings sway, bridges vibrate, car suspensions bounce and circuits ring. All of these obey the same few differential equations: simple, damped and forced harmonic motion. This unit derives them, solves them, and shows how resonance can amplify a small push into a large motion.

Class 2 (second part)

Types of motion and types of waves

Big idea

Translational motion moves the whole body from place to place; rotational motion turns it about an axis; periodic motion repeats itself after a fixed time $T$ (the Earth around the Sun). Oscillatory (vibratory) motion is periodic motion back and forth about an equilibrium position, driven by a restoring force (a mass on a spring, a pendulum, a swaying tower). When the restoring force is proportional to the displacement, the oscillation is simple harmonic.

A wave carries energy from place to place without carrying matter. Mechanical waves need a medium (sound, waves on a string, seismic waves); electromagnetic waves do not (light). In transverse waves the particles move at right angles to the wave (string, light); in longitudinal waves they move along it (sound, the P wave of an earthquake).

Classes 3–4

Simple harmonic motion: its equation, graphs and energy

By the end you can: derive and solve $\ddot x + \omega^2x = 0$; find period, frequency, maximum velocity and acceleration; draw $x$, $v$, $a$ against time; derive the total energy and the average kinetic and potential energies.

3.1The differential equation of SHM

Derivation

Step 1A mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$. Displaced by $x$, the spring pulls back with $F = -kx$ (Hooke’s law).

Step 2Newton’s second law: $m\ddot x = -kx$, so

$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \omega^2x = 0, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac km}$$

Step 3Try $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$: $\ddot x = -\omega^2A\cos(\omega t+\phi) = -\omega^2x$. It works for any amplitude $A$ and phase $\phi$ (set by how the motion starts).

Step 4Velocity and acceleration:

$$v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi) = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}, \qquad a = -\omega^2x$$

So $v_{\max} = A\omega$ at $x = 0$, $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$ at $x = \pm A$; period $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, frequency $f = 1/T$.

Simulation

SHM as the shadow of circular motion: x, v and a

Try: change the amplitude, frequency and initial phase. Watch the three graphs as the phasor turns.

What to notice: velocity is largest at the centre and zero at the ends; acceleration is largest at the ends and always points back towards the centre (opposite to $x$). Doubling $f$ doubles $v_{\max}$ but quadruples $a_{\max}$.

3.2Energy in SHM

Derivation: total and average energy

Potential$U = \int_0^x kx\,dx = \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12kA^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi)$.

Kinetic$K = \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi) = \tfrac12kA^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)$ (using $m\omega^2 = k$).

Total$E = K + U = \tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2$, constant, proportional to the square of the amplitude.

AveragesOver one period $\overline{\sin^2} = \overline{\cos^2} = \tfrac12$, so

$$\overline{K} = \overline{U} = \tfrac14kA^2 = \tfrac12E$$

(Averaged over position instead of time, $\overline K = \tfrac13 kA^2$ and $\overline U = \tfrac16kA^2$, because the mass spends longer near the ends.)

Simulation

Energy sloshing between kinetic and potential

Try: change the mass, the spring constant and the amplitude.

What to notice: the total stays on the flat line $E = \tfrac12kA^2$; the mass does not change $E$, only how fast the exchange happens. KE and PE oscillate at twice the frequency of the motion and each averages to $E/2$.

3D simulation

Vertical spring–mass system with a chart recorder

Try: change $m$ and $k$, press Pull down and release, and add a little damping. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: the pen draws a sine curve. A heavier mass or softer spring gives a longer period ($T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$), but the amplitude does not change the period. Gravity only shifts the equilibrium position; the oscillation about it is the same SHM.

Worked example

A body executes SHM with amplitude 5 cm and period 0.4 s. Find $v_{\max}$, $a_{\max}$, and the speed when $x = 3$ cm.

$\omega = 2\pi/0.4 = 15.7$ rad/s; $v_{\max} = 0.05\times15.7 = 0.785$ m/s; $a_{\max} = 0.05\times15.7^2 = 12.3$ m/s²; $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2} = 15.7\times0.04 = 0.63$ m/s.

Practice problems

  1. A 0.5 kg mass on a spring ($k = 200$ N/m) oscillates with amplitude 4 cm. Find $T$, $v_{\max}$ and $E$. easy

    $\omega = 20$ rad/s, $T = 0.314$ s, $v_{\max} = 0.8$ m/s, $E = \tfrac12(200)(0.04)^2 = 0.16$ J.

  2. At what displacement are KE and PE equal? easy

    $\tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac14kA^2 \Rightarrow x = A/\sqrt2$.

  3. The displacement is $x = 0.1\sin(5\pi t + \pi/6)$ m. Find amplitude, frequency, initial phase and the velocity at $t = 0.1$ s. medium

    $A = 0.1$ m, $f = 2.5$ Hz, $\phi = 30^\circ$; $v = 0.5\pi\cos(\pi/2 + \pi/6) = -0.785$ m/s.

  4. The ratio of the maximum acceleration to the maximum velocity of an SHM is 10 s$^{-1}$. Find the period. easy

    $A\omega^2/A\omega = \omega = 10$ rad/s, so $T = 0.628$ s.

Class 5

Combination of SHMs, Lissajous figures, physical and torsional pendulums

By the end you can: combine two perpendicular SHMs and identify the figure; read a frequency ratio from a Lissajous figure; derive the period of a compound pendulum and of a torsional pendulum.

5.1Two perpendicular SHMs of the same frequency

Derivation

Step 1$x = a\sin(\omega t + \delta)$, $y = b\sin\omega t$. From the second, $\sin\omega t = y/b$, $\cos\omega t = \sqrt{1-y^2/b^2}$.

Step 2Expand the first: $\dfrac xa = \dfrac yb\cos\delta + \sqrt{1-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}}\sin\delta$. Isolate the root, square, and rearrange:

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$$

Cases$\delta = 0$: straight line $y = (b/a)x$; $\delta = \pi$: line $y = -(b/a)x$; $\delta = \pi/2$: ellipse $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1$ (a circle if $a = b$); other $\delta$: a tilted ellipse.

With frequencies in a whole-number ratio the path is a closed Lissajous figure. Counting where it touches the bounding box gives the ratio: $\dfrac{f_y}{f_x} = \dfrac{\text{touches on a vertical side}}{\text{touches on the top}}$. Engineers use this with an oscilloscope to compare an unknown frequency with a known one.

Simulation

Lissajous figures

Try: set equal frequencies and change the phase from 0° to 180°. Then try ratios 1:2, 2:3, 3:4. Press Let the phase drift.

What to notice: with 1:1 the figure goes line → ellipse → circle → ellipse → line as $\delta$ grows. With a slight mismatch in frequency the phase drifts and the figure slowly rotates: that is how an oscilloscope shows that two frequencies are nearly, but not exactly, equal.

5.2Compound (physical) pendulum

Derivation

Step 1A rigid body of mass $m$ pivoted a distance $d$ from its centre of mass G. Turned by $\theta$, gravity gives a restoring torque $\tau = -mgd\sin\theta\approx-mgd\,\theta$.

Step 2$I\ddot\theta = -mgd\,\theta$ with $I = m(K^2 + d^2)$ (parallel axes; $K$ is the radius of gyration about G):

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{K^2 + d^2}{gd}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac lg}, \qquad l = \frac{K^2}{d} + d$$

Step 3$l$ is the length of the equivalent simple pendulum. $T$ is smallest when $d = K$, giving $T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{2K/g}$. For a uniform rod of length $L$, $K = L/\sqrt{12}$.

3D simulation

Compound pendulum and its equivalent simple pendulum

Try: move the pivot from near the centre of the rod to its end. Press Pivot for shortest period. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: pivoting very close to the centre makes the period very long (little torque). Moving the pivot out first shortens and then lengthens it again. The blue simple pendulum of length $l$ always swings in step with the rod.

5.3Torsional pendulum

Derivation

Step 1Twisting a wire (length $l$, radius $r$, rigidity $\eta$) through $\theta$ needs a couple $\tau = C\theta$. Treat the wire as thin cylindrical shells of radius $x$; each shell is sheared by $\phi = x\theta/l$, giving stress $\eta x\theta/l$ on area $2\pi x\,dx$ at lever arm $x$:

$$C = \int_0^r \frac{\eta x\theta}{l}\,2\pi x\cdot x\,dx\Big/\theta = \frac{\pi\eta r^4}{2l}$$

Step 2A disc of moment of inertia $I$ on the wire: $I\ddot\theta = -C\theta$, so

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac IC} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2Il}{\pi\eta r^4}}$$

Because $C\propto r^4$, a wire of half the radius is 16 times easier to twist. Measuring $T$ gives $\eta$ (Maxwell’s needle method).

3D simulation

Torsional pendulum

Try: change the wire’s length, radius and rigidity, and the disc radius. Follow the red stripe twisting along the wire.

What to notice: halving the wire radius makes the period 4 times longer ($T\propto r^{-2}$). A bigger disc (larger $I$) also swings more slowly. The twist is spread evenly along the wire.

Practice problems

  1. Two SHMs at right angles, $x = 3\sin\omega t$ and $y = 4\sin(\omega t + \pi/2)$. What is the path? easy

    Ellipse $x^2/9 + y^2/16 = 1$.

  2. A Lissajous figure touches the top edge 3 times and a side 2 times. If $f_x = 300$ Hz, find $f_y$. easy

    $f_y/f_x = 2/3$, so $f_y = 200$ Hz.

  3. A metre stick pivoted at one end swings. Find its period and equivalent length. medium

    $d = 0.5$, $K^2 = 1/12$; $l = (1/12 + 0.25)/0.5 = 0.667$ m; $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.667/9.8} = 1.64$ s.

  4. A disc ($I = 0.02$ kg m²) hangs from a steel wire 1 m long, radius 0.5 mm, $\eta = 80$ GPa. Find the period. medium

    $C = \pi(8\times10^{10})(0.5\times10^{-3})^4/2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}$ N m/rad; $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.02/0.00785} = 10.0$ s.

Classes 6–7

Damped oscillation, LC and RLC circuits, damping conditions, reactance and impedance

By the end you can: set up and solve the equation of damped harmonic motion; identify under-, critical and over-damping; find the damping coefficient from decaying amplitude; map the spring–mass–dashpot onto an RLC circuit; use time constants, reactance and impedance.

6.1The damped harmonic oscillator

Derivation

Step 1Add a resisting force proportional to velocity, $-b\dot x$ (air, oil, a shock absorber): $m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x$,

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0, \qquad \gamma = \frac{b}{2m},\ \ \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac km}$$

Step 2Try $x = e^{\lambda t}$: $\lambda^2 + 2\gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = -\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$.

Underdamped($\gamma < \omega_0$): the root is imaginary:

$$x = A_0e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2}$$

It oscillates a little more slowly than without damping, inside an exponentially shrinking envelope.

Critical($\gamma = \omega_0$): $x = (A + Bt)e^{-\gamma t}$, the fastest return without overshoot (door closers, car suspensions, meters).

Overdamped($\gamma > \omega_0$): $x = Ae^{-(\gamma-\beta)t} + Be^{-(\gamma+\beta)t}$, $\beta = \sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$: a slow creep back.

Measuring damping

Energy $E = \tfrac12kA^2\propto e^{-2\gamma t}$: it decays with lifetime $\tau = 1/2\gamma$; the amplitude decays with time constant $1/\gamma$.

Logarithmic decrement: the ratio of successive maxima is constant, $\dfrac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = e^{\gamma T'}$, so $\Lambda = \ln\dfrac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \gamma T'$. Measuring two amplitudes gives the damping coefficient $\gamma$.

Quality factor $Q = \omega_0/2\gamma = 2\pi\times\dfrac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}}$ (for light damping).

6.2The same equation in electric circuits

LC and RLC circuits

LCA charged capacitor connected to an inductor: around the loop $L\dfrac{di}{dt} + \dfrac qC = 0$ with $i = dq/dt$, so $\ddot q + \dfrac1{LC}q = 0$: charge oscillates with $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, energy passing between the capacitor ($q^2/2C$) and the inductor ($\tfrac12Li^2$).

RLCAdd a resistor: $L\ddot q + R\dot q + \dfrac qC = 0$, exactly the damped oscillator with

Mechanical$x$$m$$b$$k$$\gamma = b/2m$$\tfrac12kx^2$$\tfrac12mv^2$
Electrical$q$$L$$R$$1/C$$\gamma = R/2L$$q^2/2C$$\tfrac12Li^2$

Critical damping when $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$.

Simulation

Damped oscillation: spring–dashpot or RLC circuit

Try: start with no damping, then increase $b$ (or $R$). Press Critical damping, then go beyond it. Switch to the RLC circuit view.

What to notice: light damping keeps the period almost unchanged while the envelope $e^{-\gamma t}$ shrinks. At critical damping the system returns to zero fastest without crossing it. Overdamped systems are slow. The circuit behaves identically, with the capacitor’s charge swapping sign each half cycle.

6.3Time constant: charging and discharging

Derivation: RC circuit

Charging$\varepsilon = iR + q/C$ with $i = dq/dt$: $\dfrac{dq}{\varepsilon C - q} = \dfrac{dt}{RC}$, so $q = \varepsilon C(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ and $i = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R}e^{-t/RC}$.

Discharging$iR + q/C = 0$: $q = q_0e^{-t/RC}$.

The time constant $\tau = RC$ is the time to reach 63% of the final charge (or fall to 37%). For an RL circuit, $\tau = L/R$.

Simulation

Charging and discharging a capacitor

Try: press Charge and Discharge; change $R$ and $C$.

What to notice: after one time constant the capacitor is 63% charged; after 5$\tau$ it is essentially full. Doubling $R$ or $C$ doubles $\tau$. The current is largest at the start and dies away exponentially.

6.4Reactance and impedance

Key results (series RLC on an ac supply)
$$X_L = \omega L, \qquad X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}, \qquad Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}$$

The inductor opposes fast changes (large $X_L$ at high frequency); the capacitor blocks slow ones (large $X_C$ at low frequency). When $X_L = X_C$, i.e. $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Z = R$ is smallest and the current is largest: electrical resonance. $Q = \dfrac1R\sqrt{\dfrac LC}$ measures how sharp the peak is.

Simulation

Series RLC: impedance triangle and resonance

Try: sweep the supply frequency through $f_0$. Then increase $R$ and watch the peak.

What to notice: below $f_0$ the circuit is capacitive (current leads), above it inductive (current lags), and at $f_0$ the reactances cancel. A small resistance gives a tall, narrow peak (high $Q$, as in a radio tuner); a large one flattens it.

Practice problems

  1. The amplitude of a damped oscillator falls from 10 cm to 5 cm in 20 oscillations of period 0.5 s. Find $\gamma$ and the logarithmic decrement. medium

    $e^{-\gamma(10)} = 0.5 \Rightarrow \gamma = 0.0693$ s$^{-1}$; $\Lambda = \gamma T = 0.0347$.

  2. A 2 kg mass on a 50 N/m spring. What damping constant $b$ gives critical damping? easy

    $b = 2\sqrt{km} = 2\sqrt{100} = 20$ kg/s.

  3. $L = 10$ mH, $C = 1\ \mu$F. Find the natural frequency and the resistance for critical damping. easy

    $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-8}}) = 1.59$ kHz; $R = 2\sqrt{L/C} = 200\ \Omega$.

  4. $R = 1$ M$\Omega$, $C = 10\ \mu$F, $\varepsilon = 12$ V. Find $\tau$ and the voltage across $C$ after 20 s of charging. easy

    $\tau = 10$ s; $V = 12(1 - e^{-2}) = 10.4$ V.

  5. Series RLC: $R = 30\ \Omega$, $X_L = 80\ \Omega$, $X_C = 40\ \Omega$, 100 V supply. Find $Z$, current and phase. easy

    $Z = 50\ \Omega$; $I = 2$ A; $\phi = \tan^{-1}(40/30) = 53^\circ$ (current lags).

Class 8

Forced oscillation, resonance, Q factor, two-body oscillation and reduced mass

By the end you can: solve the equation of forced harmonic motion for the steady state; find the amplitude and phase against driving frequency; find the resonance frequency, Q and bandwidth; compare SHM, DHM and FHM; derive the reduced mass of a two-body oscillator.

8.1Forced harmonic motion

Derivation

Step 1Drive the damped oscillator with $F_0\cos\omega t$:

$$\ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t$$

Step 2After the transient (the DHM solution) dies away, the body moves at the driving frequency: try $x = A\cos(\omega t - \theta)$. Substituting and matching the $\cos\omega t$ and $\sin\omega t$ terms:

$$A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}$$

Step 3$A$ is largest when the denominator is smallest: $\dfrac{d}{d\omega}[\ldots] = 0$ gives the amplitude resonance $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2}$, with $A_{\max} = \dfrac{F_0/m}{2\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}}$. The phase lag is $90^\circ$ at $\omega = \omega_0$.

Q and bandwidthThe power absorbed peaks at $\omega_0$ and falls to half at $\omega_0\pm\gamma$: bandwidth $\Delta\omega = 2\gamma$ and $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega$.

Simulation

Resonance curve and phase lag

Try: sweep the driving frequency through $\omega_0$ with light damping, then with heavy damping.

What to notice: at low frequency the body follows the force; near $\omega_0$ the amplitude shoots up and the response lags by $90^\circ$; far above, the body moves opposite to the force with a tiny amplitude. Less damping gives a taller, sharper peak (larger $Q$). Engineers design buildings so that their natural frequencies are far from wind, traffic and earthquake frequencies, and add dampers to keep $Q$ low.

3D simulation

Barton’s pendulums: which one resonates?

Try: change the length of the heavy red driver pendulum and wait a few seconds. Lower the damping. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: all the small pendulums are pushed at the driver’s frequency, but only the one with the same length (same natural frequency) builds up a large swing. With less damping its swing grows bigger and takes longer to build up.

8.2Two-body oscillation and reduced mass

Derivation

Step 1Masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ joined by a spring $k$ (a diatomic molecule, two railway wagons). If the spring is stretched by $x = x_2 - x_1 - l_0$: $m_1\ddot x_1 = kx$ and $m_2\ddot x_2 = -kx$.

Step 2Divide and subtract: $\ddot x_2 - \ddot x_1 = -kx\left(\dfrac1{m_1}+\dfrac1{m_2}\right)$, i.e. $\mu\ddot x = -kx$ with

$$\mu = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac k\mu}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac\mu k}$$

Step 3The centre of mass does not move (no external force); the two masses move in opposite directions with amplitudes in the ratio $m_2 : m_1$. If one mass is very heavy, $\mu\to$ the lighter mass (a mass on a spring fixed to a wall).

3D simulation

Two masses on a spring

Try: make the masses equal, then make one much heavier than the other.

What to notice: the green marker (centre of mass) never moves. With equal masses each moves equally; with very unequal masses the heavy one hardly moves and $\mu$ approaches the light mass.

Practice problems

  1. A system with $\omega_0 = 10$ rad/s and $\gamma = 0.5$ s$^{-1}$ is driven. Find the resonance frequency, $Q$ and bandwidth. easy

    $\omega_r = \sqrt{100 - 0.5} = 9.97$ rad/s; $Q = 10$; $\Delta\omega = 1$ rad/s.

  2. For the system above with $F_0/m = 1$ m/s², find the steady amplitude at $\omega = 5$ rad/s and at resonance. medium

    $\omega = 5$: $A = 1/\sqrt{75^2 + 25} = 0.0133$ m. Resonance: $A \approx 1/(2\times0.5\times10) = 0.1$ m.

  3. Find the reduced mass of an HCl molecule (H = 1 u, Cl = 35 u) and its vibration frequency if $k = 480$ N/m. medium

    $\mu = 35/36$ u $= 1.61\times10^{-27}$ kg; $f = \tfrac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/\mu} = 8.7\times10^{13}$ Hz.

  4. Why can soldiers be ordered to break step when marching across a bridge? easy

    Marching in step applies a periodic force; if its frequency matches a natural frequency of the bridge, resonance can build up dangerous amplitudes.
