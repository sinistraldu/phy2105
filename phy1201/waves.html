Waves and Acoustics

A wave carries energy without carrying matter. This unit derives the wave equation, the power a wave carries, standing waves, group velocity, and then turns to sound: loudness in decibels, beats, the Doppler effect and the acoustic design of halls, which every civil engineer meets when designing classrooms, auditoriums and noise barriers.

  • Class 9Progressive waves, power, stationary waves
  • Class 10Group and phase velocity, standing waves
  • Class 11Sound, beats, decibels, Doppler, Sabine
Class 9

The progressive wave: its equation, power and intensity

By the end you can: write the equation of a travelling wave; relate $v$, $f$, $\lambda$, $k$ and $\omega$; derive the differential wave equation and the speed of a wave on a string; derive the power and intensity carried by a wave.

9.1Equation of a travelling wave

Derivation

Step 1At $x = 0$ the source moves in SHM: $y = A\sin\omega t$. The disturbance travels with speed $v$, so a particle at distance $x$ repeats this motion a time $x/v$ later:

$$y(x,t) = A\sin\omega\left(t - \frac xv\right) = A\sin(\omega t - kx), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\ \ \omega = 2\pi f,\ \ v = f\lambda = \frac\omega k$$

($y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ describes the same wave with a shifted phase.) A wave moving in the $-x$ direction has $\omega t + kx$.

Step 2Differentiate twice: $\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2y$ and $\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2y$. Dividing:

$$\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = v^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}$$

This is the differential equation of a progressive wave. Any function $y = g(x - vt)$ satisfies it.

Step 3For a string with tension $F$ and mass per length $\mu$: a small piece $dx$ feels a net vertical force $F\,\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}dx$, and Newton’s law gives $\mu\,dx\,\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = F\dfrac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}dx$. Comparing, $v = \sqrt{F/\mu}$. (For sound in a fluid, $v = \sqrt{K/\rho}$; in a solid rod, $v = \sqrt{Y/\rho}$.)

Simulation

A travelling wave: transverse and longitudinal

Try: change $A$, $\lambda$ and $f$. Follow the red particle. Switch to Longitudinal.

What to notice: the wave pattern moves along, but each particle only oscillates about its own place. The wave speed is $f\lambda$, while the particle’s speed is at most $A\omega$, a completely different quantity. In the longitudinal wave the layers bunch together (compressions) and spread apart (rarefactions).

3D simulation

Transverse and longitudinal waves side by side

Try: change the amplitude, wavelength and frequency; view each type on its own. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: both waves travel at the same speed $f\lambda$. In the top (transverse) layer the red particles move up and down; in the bottom (longitudinal) layer they move back and forth along the direction of travel.

9.2Power and intensity of a wave

Derivation

Step 1A piece of string of length $dx$ has mass $\mu\,dx$ and oscillates in SHM with amplitude $A$; its total energy is $dE = \tfrac12(\mu\,dx)\omega^2A^2$.

Step 2Energy per unit length: $\dfrac{dE}{dx} = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2$. This energy moves along with speed $v$, so the power delivered is

$$P = \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac12\mu v\omega^2A^2 = 2\pi^2\mu vf^2A^2$$

Step 3For a wave in 3D (sound), the intensity is the power per unit area: $I = \tfrac12\rho v\omega^2A^2$. From a point source the power spreads over a sphere, so $I = P/4\pi r^2$: double the distance, a quarter of the intensity.

Power depends on the square of both amplitude and frequency.

Simulation

Power carried by a wave on a string

Try: double the amplitude, then double the frequency. Change the tension and linear density.

What to notice: doubling $A$ or $f$ makes the power four times larger. A heavier or tighter string carries more power at the same amplitude, because $\mu v = \sqrt{F\mu}$ grows.

Practice problems

  1. A wave is $y = 0.02\sin(4\pi t - 0.5\pi x)$ (SI). Find $A$, $f$, $\lambda$, $v$ and the direction. easy

    $A = 0.02$ m, $f = 2$ Hz, $\lambda = 4$ m, $v = 8$ m/s, travelling in $+x$.

  2. A string with $\mu = 5$ g/m and tension 80 N carries a 100 Hz wave of amplitude 2 mm. Find $v$ and the power. medium

    $v = \sqrt{80/0.005} = 126.5$ m/s; $P = \tfrac12(0.005)(126.5)(628)^2(0.002)^2 = 0.50$ W.

  3. Find the phase difference between two points 0.5 m apart on a wave of wavelength 2 m. easy

    $\Delta\phi = 2\pi\Delta x/\lambda = \pi/2$.

Class 10

Group and phase velocity; standing waves

By the end you can: distinguish phase and group velocity and relate them through the wave number; derive the equation of a standing wave from two travelling waves; locate nodes and antinodes; compare standing and travelling waves.

10.1Phase velocity and group velocity

Derivation

Step 1Add two waves of slightly different $k$ and $\omega$: $y = A\cos(k_1x - \omega_1t) + A\cos(k_2x - \omega_2t)$.

Step 2Using $\cos a + \cos b = 2\cos\tfrac{a-b}{2}\cos\tfrac{a+b}{2}$:

$$y = \underbrace{2A\cos\left(\frac{\Delta k}{2}x - \frac{\Delta\omega}{2}t\right)}_{\text{envelope (group)}}\ \cos(kx - \omega t)$$

Step 3The fast ripple moves at the phase velocity $v_p = \omega/k$; the envelope (which carries the energy and information) moves at the group velocity

$$v_g = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta k}\to\frac{d\omega}{dk} = v_p + k\frac{dv_p}{dk} = v_p - \lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}$$

If $v_p$ does not depend on wavelength (no dispersion, e.g. sound in air), $v_g = v_p$. Water waves and light in glass are dispersive.

Simulation

Phase velocity vs group velocity

Try: set the dispersion $\alpha$ to zero, then positive, then negative. Change the spread $\Delta k$.

What to notice: with $\alpha = 0$ the red crest and the green envelope move together. With dispersion the crests slide through the groups, forwards or backwards. A smaller $\Delta k$ makes longer groups ($\approx 2\pi/\Delta k$).

10.2Stationary (standing) waves

Derivation

Step 1A wave and its reflection travelling in opposite directions: $y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, $y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$.

Step 2Add: $y = 2A\sin kx\cos\omega t$. Every point oscillates in step, with an amplitude $2A\sin kx$ that depends on position.

Nodes$\sin kx = 0$: $x = 0, \tfrac\lambda2, \lambda, \ldots$ (never move). Antinodes$\sin kx = \pm1$: $x = \tfrac\lambda4, \tfrac{3\lambda}4, \ldots$ (largest motion). Adjacent nodes are $\lambda/2$ apart.

String fixed at both endsNodes at both ends require $L = n\lambda/2$, giving the harmonics $f_n = \dfrac{nv}{2L} = \dfrac n{2L}\sqrt{\dfrac F\mu}$.

Travelling waveStanding wave
Pattern moves with speed $v$Pattern stays in place
All particles have the same amplitudeAmplitude varies from 0 (nodes) to $2A$ (antinodes)
Phase changes continuously along the waveAll particles between two nodes move in phase
Carries energyNo net energy transport
Simulation

Standing waves on a string

Try: change the harmonic number, string length and wave speed. Show the two travelling waves that make up the standing wave.

What to notice: the $n$th harmonic has $n$ loops, $n+1$ nodes and $n$ antinodes. A shorter string or a faster wave (tighter string) raises every frequency: this is how a guitarist tunes and frets a string.

Practice problems

  1. Two waves $y_1 = 0.04\sin(2x - 100t)$ and $y_2 = 0.04\sin(2x + 100t)$ superpose. Find the amplitude at $x = 0.5$ m and the node spacing. easy

    $y = 0.08\sin2x\cos100t$; amplitude $0.08\sin1 = 0.067$ m; nodes every $\pi/2 = 1.57$ m.

  2. A 0.8 m string has a fundamental of 220 Hz. Find the wave speed and the third harmonic. easy

    $v = 2Lf = 352$ m/s; $f_3 = 660$ Hz.

  3. For deep-water waves $v_p = \sqrt{g\lambda/2\pi}$. Show that $v_g = v_p/2$. medium

    $v_p\propto\lambda^{1/2}$, so $\lambda\,dv_p/d\lambda = v_p/2$ and $v_g = v_p - v_p/2 = v_p/2$.

Class 11

Sound: nodes and antinodes in pipes, beats, intensity level, Doppler effect and architectural acoustics

By the end you can: find the harmonics of open and closed pipes; explain beats; use the decibel scale; apply the Doppler formula; derive and apply Sabine’s formula; list the requisites of a good auditorium.

11.1Standing sound waves in pipes

Key results

At a closed end the air cannot move: a displacement node (pressure antinode). At an open end the pressure equals atmospheric: a displacement antinode (pressure node).

$$\text{open pipe: } f_n = \frac{nv}{2L},\ n = 1,2,3,\ldots \qquad \text{closed pipe: } f_n = \frac{nv}{4L},\ n = 1,3,5,\ldots$$

A closed pipe sounds an octave lower than an open pipe of the same length and has only odd harmonics. (In practice add an end correction of about $0.6r$ at each open end.)

3D simulation

Standing sound waves in open and closed pipes

Try: step through the modes for an open pipe, then close one end. Change the length. Drag to rotate.

What to notice: the air particles pile up and spread out; where they move most (displacement antinode, blue curve) the pressure hardly changes, and vice versa (green curve). The closed end is always a displacement node; open ends are always antinodes.

11.2Beats

Derivation

Two sounds of nearly equal frequency: $y = A\cos2\pi f_1t + A\cos2\pi f_2t = 2A\cos\left(2\pi\dfrac{f_1-f_2}{2}t\right)\cos\left(2\pi\dfrac{f_1+f_2}2t\right)$. We hear the average pitch, with a loudness that rises and falls. The amplitude is largest twice per cycle of the slow cosine, so the beat frequency is

$$f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|$$

Musicians tune instruments by adjusting until the beats disappear; an unknown frequency can be found from the beats it makes with a known tuning fork.

Simulation

Beats from two tuning forks

Try: bring $f_2$ closer to $f_1$ until the beats disappear.

What to notice: 256 Hz and 252 Hz give 4 loud–soft cycles per second. The closer the frequencies, the slower the beats.

11.3Intensity level, loudness and pitch

Key results

The ear responds to intensities from $10^{-12}$ W/m² (threshold) to about 1 W/m² (pain): a range of $10^{12}$. So we use a logarithmic scale, the intensity level in bels (or tenths, decibels):

$$\beta = 10\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0}\ \text{dB}, \qquad I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$$

Doubling the intensity adds 3 dB; ten times the intensity adds 10 dB. Loudness is the listener’s sensation (it also depends on frequency); pitch is the sensation of frequency; quality (timbre) depends on the mix of harmonics.

Simulation

Intensity and the decibel scale

Try: change the source power, the distance and the number of identical sources (machines on a site).

What to notice: moving twice as far away only lowers the level by 6 dB; ten machines are only 10 dB louder than one. This is why noise control needs barriers and absorbers, not just distance.

11.4The Doppler effect

Derivation

Moving sourceA source moving towards the observer at $v_s$ emits a crest every $T = 1/f$; meanwhile it moves $v_sT$, so the crests are squeezed: $\lambda' = (v - v_s)/f$, and the observer hears $f' = v/\lambda'$.

Moving observerAn observer moving towards the source at $v_o$ meets crests at the relative speed $v + v_o$.

$$f' = f\,\frac{v + v_o}{v - v_s}$$

(Signs flip for motion away.) Used in police radar, Doppler ultrasound of blood flow, and measuring traffic speeds.

Simulation

Doppler effect and the sonic boom

Try: increase the source speed towards and beyond 343 m/s. Let the observer move too.

What to notice: wavefronts bunch up in front of the source (higher pitch) and spread out behind (lower pitch). At the speed of sound they pile into a single front; beyond it they form a cone, the shock wave of a sonic boom.

11.5Architectural acoustics and Sabine’s formula

Derivation (outline)

Step 1After a sound stops, it keeps bouncing around the room. Reverberation time $T$ is the time for the sound energy to fall to one-millionth (60 dB) of its initial value.

Step 2Each surface of area $S_i$ absorbs a fraction $\alpha_i$ (absorption coefficient; an open window has $\alpha = 1$). The total absorption is $A = \sum\alpha_iS_i$ (in sabin, m²).

Step 3With the sound energy density $E$ spread evenly, the energy hitting the walls per second is $\tfrac14EvA$ (kinetic-theory-like averaging over directions). So $V\dfrac{dE}{dt} = -\dfrac{vA}{4}E$, giving $E = E_0e^{-vAt/4V}$.

Step 4Setting $E/E_0 = 10^{-6}$ with $v = 343$ m/s:

$$T = \frac{0.161\,V}{\sum\alpha_iS_i}\ \text{s} \qquad (V \text{ in m}^3,\ S \text{ in m}^2)$$
Requisites of a good auditorium
  • Optimum reverberation time: about 0.5–1 s for speech, 1.5–2 s for music. Too short sounds “dead”, too long blurs syllables.
  • Adequate, uniform loudness: avoid focusing by concave walls and domes (they create loud spots and dead spots); use reflecting panels above the stage.
  • No echoes: a distinct echo needs a path difference over about 17 m (0.05 s); treat the rear wall with absorbers.
  • Noise insulation and reduction: double walls with air gaps, heavy doors and seals, floating floors, absorbent ceilings; keep machinery noise isolated.
  • No resonance of panels and windows; good sound distribution with diffusing surfaces.
3D simulation

Design a hall: reverberation time

Try: choose the hall size, wall and floor materials and the audience, then press Clap! to watch the sound bounce and die away (25× slow motion). Drag to rotate.

What to notice: a large bare concrete hall rings for many seconds. Curtains, acoustic tiles, carpet and a big audience all add absorption and shorten $T$. Each dot fades a little at every bounce; a softer room drains the energy in fewer bounces.

Worked example

A lecture hall $20\times15\times6$ m has plaster walls ($\alpha = 0.04$), a wooden floor (0.08), a plaster ceiling (0.04) and 300 students (0.45 each). Find $T$.

$V = 1800$ m³. Walls: $2(20+15)(6) = 420$ m² × 0.04 = 16.8; floor 300 × 0.08 = 24; ceiling 300 × 0.04 = 12; audience 135. $A = 187.8$ sabin.

$T = 0.161\times1800/187.8 = 1.54$ s: too long for speech. Acoustic tiles on the ceiling ($\alpha = 0.7$, +198 sabin) bring it to 0.75 s.

Practice problems

  1. Find the first two frequencies of a 0.5 m pipe, open at both ends and closed at one end ($v = 340$ m/s). easy

    Open: 340, 680 Hz. Closed: 170, 510 Hz.

  2. A fork of 384 Hz gives 4 beats/s with an unknown fork; loading the unknown fork with wax increases the beats. Find its frequency. medium

    380 or 388 Hz; wax lowers the frequency, and the beats increase, so it moved away from 384: it is 380 Hz.

  3. A sound has intensity $10^{-5}$ W/m². Find its level. What is the level of 10 such sources? easy

    70 dB; 80 dB.

  4. An ambulance at 30 m/s sounds a 700 Hz siren. What frequency does a stationary listener hear as it approaches and as it recedes? easy

    $700\times343/313 = 767$ Hz; $700\times343/373 = 644$ Hz.

  5. A hall of volume 5000 m³ has $T = 2$ s. How much extra absorption is needed to bring $T$ to 1 s? medium

    $A_1 = 0.161\times5000/2 = 402.5$; $A_2 = 805$; add about 403 sabin.
